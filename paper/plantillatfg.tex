\begin{abstract}
La construcció automàtica de màquines de recompensa (\foreign{english}{Reward Machines}, RM) en l'aprenentatge per reforç s'ha explorat recentment amb grans models fundacionals, que generen RM directament a partir de descripcions de tasques en llenguatge natural. Tot i ser prometedors, aquests mètodes no oferixen garanties formals de la correcció de les RM i depenen d'una persona o d'un segon model per a revisar-les. En aquest treball proposem FARM (\foreign{english}{A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning}), una cadena de processament basada en la lògica temporal lineal (LTL) per a generar RM: un model de llenguatge traduïx primer les tasques en llenguatge natural a fórmules LTL, que després es compilen en màquines de recompensa amb eines de la teoria d'autòmats. Aquest enfocament produïx RM que, per construcció, estan alineades amb l'especificació LTL, si bé la traducció mateixa encara pot ser errònia. L'avaluem amb dues proves: una construïx les RM i les comprova davant de la seua tasca sense entrenar cap agent, i l'altra hi entrena agents i jutja si aprenen la tasca. En un banc de proves de tasques amb estructura temporal, el nostre mètode basat en LTL produïx RM més fidels a l'especificació de la tasca que les d'una reconstrucció d'ARM-FM, un enfocament de l'estat de l'art basat en models fundacionals: sense editar a mà cap RM, FARM construïx el 99,6\,\% de les RM demanades i ARM-FM, el 57\,\%. Els agents entrenats amb les RM de FARM aprenen la tasca en el 72\,\% dels casos d'entrenament, enfront del 48\,\% amb les d'ARM-FM i del 38\,\% sense cap RM. Aquests resultats suggerixen que la síntesi formal de RM guiada per la lògica oferix una alternativa més fiable als mètodes de generació de recompenses purament neuronals.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}[spanish]
La construcción automática de máquinas de recompensa (\foreign{english}{Reward Machines}, RM) en el aprendizaje por refuerzo se ha explorado recientemente con grandes modelos fundacionales, que generan RM directamente a partir de descripciones de tareas en lenguaje natural. \begin{abstract}[english]
The automated construction of Reward Machines (RMs) in Reinforcement Learning has recently been explored using large foundation models, which generate RMs directly from natural language task descriptions. While promising, these methods lack formal guarantees of RM correctness and rely on a human or a second model to check the RMs. In this work, we propose FARM (A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning), a pipeline based on Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) for RM generation: natural language tasks are first translated by a language model into LTL formulas, which are then compiled into Reward Machines using automata-theoretic tools. This approach produces RMs that, by construction, are aligned with the LTL specification, though the translation itself can still be wrong. We evaluate it with two tests: one builds the RMs and checks them against their tasks without training an agent, and one trains agents on them and judges whether they learn the task. On a benchmark of temporally structured tasks, our LTL-based method produces RMs more faithful to the task specification than those of a rebuilt ARM-FM, a state-of-the-art foundation model approach: with no RM edited by hand, FARM builds 99.6\% of the RMs requested and ARM-FM 57\%. Agents trained on FARM's RMs learn the task in 72\% of the training cases, against 48\% with ARM-FM's and 38\% with no RM. These results suggest that logic-guided formal RM synthesis offers a more reliable alternative to purely neural reward generation methods.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Glossary}
\begin{description}
    \item[Arm] One way of training that the evaluation compares: FARM, ARM-FM, or training with no Reward Machine (``no RM'' in tables and figures).
    \item[ARM-FM] Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models: the method in which a foundation model writes a Reward Machine as text from a task in plain language. In this thesis it is also the arm that trains on the machines of its rebuilt version.
    \item[Card] One test case of the benchmark: a task written in plain language, together with the answers accepted for it. The word belongs to the evaluation; the other chapters say task.
    \item[Clause] One template chosen by the generator, with the propositions it applies to, a priority, hard or soft, and one plain sentence that states its part of the task.
    \item[Compiler] Steps 2 to 4 of FARM. They build the Reward Machine from the chosen clauses with fixed tools and call no language model.
    \item[Critic] The second language model, different from the generator. It accepts the result or rejects it with comments, and it never edits the machine.
    \item[DFA] Deterministic finite automaton: a machine with a finite set of states that reads a trace one step at a time and accepts or rejects it.
    \item[Final state] A state of the machine in which every hard clause is satisfied. Reaching it ends the episode, and only a task with a hard goal has one.
    \item[Generator] The language model that reads the task and writes it down: as clauses in FARM, as a machine in ARM-FM.
    \item[Goal and rule] A goal is a clause that asks for something to happen, so it stays unmet while nothing happens. Every other clause is a rule: it holds while nothing happens, and a later event may break it or make it wait for an answer.
    \item[Hard and soft] The priority of a clause. A hard clause must hold for the task to count as done; a soft clause is a preference, and each break costs reward but never prevents finishing. Hard rules and soft rules are the hard and soft clauses that are not goals; where the goal is included, the text says hard clauses.
    \item[Judged episode] One evaluation episode played after training, with the agent taking its most likely action at every step, and scored by the judge.
    \item[Judge] The scorer of the evaluation. It reads each judged episode through the benchmark's own labelling and checks the clauses expected for the task, in the same way for every arm.
    \item[Labelling function] The function that reads the world at every step and returns the propositions that hold, which the Reward Machine follows.
    \item[LARM] Language-Aligned Reward Machine, the output of ARM-FM: a Reward Machine, the code of its labelling function and a plain-language description of each state.
    \item[Learned] A training case counts as learned for an arm when at least two of its seeds succeed in 0.8 or more of their judged episodes. Shop tasks with a rule about serving orders also need enough orders served, and a task with no goal and only soft rules is compared with an idle agent (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). A case whose machine was not built counts as not learned.
    \item[Loop that pays] A cycle of transitions whose rewards add up to more than zero, so that an agent can collect reward by going round it without doing the task.
    \item[LTL and LTLf] Linear Temporal Logic, a logic of properties over sequences of states. LTLf reads the same formulas over finite sequences, such as an episode.
    \item[Plan check] The run of a task's scripted plans through a built machine, to read what each plan pays. No language model takes part.
    \item[Proposition] A fact about the world that is true or false at each step, such as \texttt{has\_key}. The labelling function reports which propositions hold.
    \item[Re-arming and folding] Two ways in which FARM handles a soft rule. A re-armed soft rule watches for the next break after each one, so every break costs again; a folded soft Absence adds no state to the machine, and its break becomes a loop that costs reward.
    \item[Reward Machine (RM)] A finite-state machine that reads which propositions hold at each step, moves between its states and pays a reward on each transition. Once the context is clear, the thesis says machine.
    \item[Rewording] The same task, said in other words.
    \item[Scripted plan] A fixed sequence of actions written for a task: one solves the task, others each break a named rule, and an idle one only waits.
    \item[Stage] One event still needed to finish the task. Every state of a FARM machine owes a number of stages, and progress pays as that number falls.
    \item[Task schema] The clauses of a task with the propositions left open. Tasks of one schema get machines of one shape, with the names exchanged.
    \item[Template] One of the eight fixed patterns of IBM nl2ltl, taken from Declare, such as Existence, Precedence or Absence. Each has a fixed LTLf formula in which only the propositions change.
    \item[Thinking level] How much a model reasons before it answers, from none to max. The frozen pipeline uses max.
    \item[Training case] One task trained under one setting. The training test has 60, on 53 different cards (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}).
    \item[Well] The one rejecting state of a FARM machine, which a machine may lack. Entering it ends the episode. An edge leads to it when a step breaks a hard clause or, in a task with a hard goal, leaves the task impossible to finish, and each such edge carries its own price.
\end{description}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
\section{Motivation}
In reinforcement learning (RL) an agent learns to act by maximising the reward it receives, so the reward decides what the agent learns \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Writing that reward is hard. When the reward arrives only at the end of a long task, the agent gets almost no signal while it learns, and reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it \cite{ng1999policy}. Shaping improves learning, but people write and tune reward functions by hand, which takes time and needs expertise in both the task and RL.

A tool that builds the reward from a statement of the task would save that time and open RL to people without that technical knowledge. Formal methods suit such a tool: a task stated in a formal language has one exact meaning, and fixed tools can turn it into a reward in the same way every time. Reward Machines, finite-state machines that pay reward as the steps of a task are completed, are a natural target, because they expose the structure of the task to the learner (Section~\ref{sec:sota-rm}).

Programming assistants built on large language models can help write code, so they could help with rewards as well. ARM-FM \cite{creus2026armfm} takes this route and lets a foundation model write the Reward Machine from a task in plain language (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}). A language model alone is not reliable, though: it can give different answers to the same input \cite{ouyang2025empirical} and state things that are false \cite{huang2025survey}, and ARM-FM relies on a person or on a second model to check its machines.

A second motivation is to bring AI closer to what people want. A task as a person states it may hold more than a goal: an order of steps, rules that must not be broken and preferences to keep when possible. A reward that captures all of them says more of what the person means. The aim is to build such rewards without a human expert, with machines robust enough not to depend on the uncertainty of the language model. Something to clarify, that robustness is an aim of the method, not a property of every part of it: the model's reading of the task can still be wrong.

This thesis presents FARM, a formal approach to Reward Machine generation. A language model writes the task as clauses from a fixed catalogue of temporal patterns, each marked hard or soft, and fixed tools build the Reward Machine from those clauses alone (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}).

\section{Objectives}
The main objective is to build Reward Machines from tasks stated in plain language, with no machine edited by hand, on which agents learn the tasks. In detail:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Capture the whole task as a person states it: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.
    \item Build the machine with fixed tools from a formal statement of the task, so that only the reading of the task is left to the language model.
    \item Need no human expert to check or repair the machines.
    \item Verify the correctness of the method both qualitatively and empirically and compare the results against ARM-FM and training with no Reward Machine.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Expected impact}
The intended benefit is reward shaping and task definition open to people who are not experts in RL, and better learning through machines that are built formally rather than written freely. Three kinds of user are in view: a developer who wants a machine that trains, a person without technical knowledge who states a task in plain language, and other agents that call the tool. The tool also brings AI closer to people: a person states a task, with its rules and preferences, in their own words, and the agent learns from a reward that carries all of them. These are intended benefits: no study with users was run.

\section{Structure of the thesis}
The thesis starts with the concepts it builds on, where current methods stand and what they leave open (Chapters~\ref{chap:sota} and~\ref{chap:proposal}). It then presents the new method, FARM, and its limits (Chapters~\ref{chap:farm} and~\ref{chap:limitations}), tests it and shows the results (Chapters~\ref{chap:evaluation} and~\ref{chap:results}). It ends with the conclusions and the open work (Chapters~\ref{chap:conclusions} and~\ref{chap:future-work}). A glossary precedes this chapter, and the appendices hold further examples and the implementation details.

\section{Scope and limits of the study}
FARM changes only one step of ARM-FM, the generation of the Reward Machine. The labelling functions, the state descriptions and their embeddings follow ARM-FM and sit outside the main comparison, so the comparison with ARM-FM is isolated to the generation of the machine.

The ARM-FM baseline is a reconstruction. It is rebuilt from the paper and from a public repository of its first author, so its results may differ from the original ones; Section~\ref{sec:critique} gives the details.

The models are small and cheap on purpose: they are not the strongest available, but they are enough to test the method and they keep it accessible. Both methods use \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} as generator and \texttt{glm-5.3-flash} as critic, where the ARM-FM paper used GPT-4o. Whether the results hold for other models is not tested.

FARM states tasks with the eight Declare templates of IBM nl2ltl, read as LTLf over finite traces. A task that these templates cannot state is outside its reach (Section~\ref{sec:lim-conditions}). A model that writes LTLf directly is future work (Section~\ref{sec:fw-ltlf}).

The work does not cover tasks that ask to optimise a quantity, such as \textit{Kill as many zombies as you can} or \textit{Reach the goal by the shortest path}, nor behaviour that a person cannot put into words. A clause is met or broken over a whole run, so it cannot ask for as much of something as possible, and a behaviour with no words gives no clauses to write (Section~\ref{sec:lim-quantities}).

\chapter{State of the art}
\label{chap:sota}
\section{Related work}
\subsection{Reward Machines}
\label{sec:sota-rm}
In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent learns to act in a Markov decision process (MDP) by maximising the reward it receives \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Most methods treat the reward function as a black box: the agent observes the number returned after each step and nothing about how it is computed \cite{icarte2022reward}. In practice a person writes that function by hand, so its structure is known and can be exploited to learn faster.

Sparse rewards pose a second problem. When the reward arrives only on completing a long task, the agent receives almost no signal while it learns. Reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it. Ng et al.~\cite{ng1999policy} prove that shaping with the difference of a potential function over states, $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$, leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that no other form of shaping gives this guarantee in general.

Reward Machines (RMs) address both problems by exposing the structure of the reward to the learner. Toro Icarte et al.~\cite{toroicarte2018using} introduced them at ICML 2018 and gave a fuller account in the Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research (JAIR) \cite{icarte2022reward}. This thesis follows the JAIR article for the formalisation, counterfactual learning and reward shaping.

An RM is a finite-state machine, written $\langle U, u_0, F, \delta_u, \delta_r\rangle$ in the JAIR formulation. $U$ is a finite set of states, $u_0 \in U$ the initial state and $F$ the set of terminal states. The transition function $\delta_u$ takes the current state and the set of propositions that hold and returns the next state. The reward function $\delta_r$ assigns to each state a reward function over environment transitions. The ICML formulation \textbf{attaches the reward to the pair of states of a transition instead}, which is the convention of the RM text format used in this thesis.

Propositions come from outside the machine. A labelling function $L$ maps each environment transition to the set of propositions that hold in it, for example ``the agent holds the key''. At every step the machine reads that set and moves. The agent learns in the product of the MDP and the RM, whose states pair an environment state with an RM state; in the product the reward is Markovian even when the task is not.

Exposing the structure enables dedicated learning methods. Q-learning for RMs (QRM) \cite{toroicarte2018using} decomposes the machine and learns, off-policy, one sub-policy per RM state in parallel. The JAIR article adds counterfactual reasoning for RMs (CRM), which reuses each experience to update every RM state; a hierarchical method (HRM); and automated reward shaping. The shaping potential is the negative optimal value of the RM state, $\Phi(s,u) = -v^{*}(u)$, computed by value iteration on the machine (Section~4.4 of the article, restricted to simple RMs).

RMs have two costs. Someone has to design the machine, and RMs do not by themselves solve the problem of specifying a reward that leads to the intended behaviour, although they may make temporally extended behaviours easier to specify \cite[p.~199]{icarte2022reward}. The labelling function is also assumed to exist and to be correct: the methods that learn RMs from experience all assume a correct labelling function, and learning both at once is one of the open problems listed in the article \cite[p.~201]{icarte2022reward}. Both costs motivate generating RMs automatically.

An RM is also a graph, a form that most people are not used to reading, and it becomes hard to follow once it has more than a few states and transitions. This motivates rewards that a person can read, and simplified drawings of the machines.

\subsection{ARM-FM}
\label{sec:sota-armfm}
ARM-FM (Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models), by Creus Castanyer et al.~\cite{creus2026armfm} and published at ICLR 2026, replaces the hand-built RM with one generated by a foundation model (FM) from a natural-language task description. The output is a Language-Aligned Reward Machine (LARM), made of three parts: an RM, the Python code of its labelling functions, and a natural-language description of each RM state. A text embedding of the description of the current state is given to the policy together with the environment observation, so that the policy knows which sub-goal is active. In this thesis \textbf{ARM-FM is the method and LARM its output}.

The RM is produced by a generator model and reviewed by a critic model over several rounds of self-improvement. A human may optionally approve the result or give corrective feedback. The generator prompt asks for the machine as plain text in a fixed format. The critic prompt asks, among other checks, that regressions are penalised and that no unintentional loops exist, and the critic answers either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED} \cite[pp.~26--27]{creus2026armfm}. The paper uses GPT-4o to generate all LARM components, except for the 1\,000 XLand-MiniGrid LARMs, which come from several open-source models \cite[p.~4]{creus2026armfm}.

The evaluation covers four environments: MiniGrid (DoorKey and the harder UnlockToUnlock, BlockedUnlockPickup and KeyCorridor), Craftium (a Minecraft-based environment where the agent mines a diamond after gathering wood, stone and iron), Meta-World manipulation tasks, and XLand-MiniGrid. On DoorKey the agent trained with the LARM outperforms DQN with ICM, ReAct and plain DQN; on the three harder MiniGrid tasks it is the only agent that solves all of them; on Craftium it learns where an agent without the LARM makes no progress. On XLand-MiniGrid a single policy trains on ten tasks at once and solves unseen combinations of known tasks without fine-tuning.

\subsection{Linear Temporal Logic}
\label{sec:sota-ltl}
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) is a logic for stating properties of sequences of states, called traces. Pnueli \cite{pnueli1977temporal} proposed it to reason about programs. It is a language rather than a catalogue of patterns: a formula is built from propositions, Boolean connectives and four temporal operators.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{X}\,\varphi$ (next): $\varphi$ holds at the next step.
    \item $\mathrm{F}\,\varphi$ (eventually): $\varphi$ holds at the current step or at a later one.
    \item $\mathrm{G}\,\varphi$ (always): $\varphi$ holds at every step from the current one on.
    \item $\varphi\ \mathrm{U}\ \psi$ (until): $\psi$ holds at some step from the current one on, and $\varphi$ holds at every step before it.
\end{itemize}
LTL is defined over infinite traces, whereas a reinforcement learning episode ends. LTLf, LTL over finite traces \cite{degiacomo2013linear}, keeps the syntax and reads a formula over a trace of finite length, where next requires a following step to exist. LTLf is as expressive as first-order logic over finite traces and corresponds to the star-free regular expressions.

In the DoorKey task of Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}, the propositions are \texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open} and \texttt{at\_goal}. \textit{The agent reaches the goal at some point} is
\[ \mathrm{F}\ \texttt{at\_goal}, \]
and \textit{the door is not open before the key is held} is
\[ (\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{has\_key}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}. \]

For every LTLf formula there is a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) that accepts exactly the traces satisfying it \cite{degiacomo2013linear}. In practice the formula is rewritten in monadic second-order logic over finite words, from which MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} builds the minimal DFA. This is the bridge used by Step~2 of FARM (Section~\ref{sec:step2}).

Camacho et al.~\cite{camacho2019ltl} bring this logic to RL. They show that RMs can express reward specifications written in LTL, LTLf and other formal languages, translate a specification into an RM and combine several specifications with a product construction.

Declare defines a catalogue of templates, such as Existence, Response or Precedence, each with an LTL semantics, to specify business processes declaratively; Pesic and van der Aalst \cite{pesic2006declarative} introduced the language under the name ConDec. nl2ltl \cite{fuggitti2023nl2ltl} translates natural language into LTL by letting a language model choose a Declare template and its arguments.

\subsection{Hard and soft constraints in planning}
\label{sec:sota-preferences}
In automated planning, a plan is a sequence of actions that takes a system from an initial state to a goal, and PDDL is the standard language to describe such problems. PDDL3 \cite{gerevini2006preferences} adds two things to it. State trajectory constraints are conditions on the whole sequence of states that the plan goes through, not only on the last one, written with operators such as \texttt{always}, \texttt{sometime}, \texttt{at-most-once} and \texttt{sometime-before}, close to those of LTL. Preferences are soft constraints: a valid plan must satisfy every hard constraint, while each preference it breaks only adds a cost to the plan. Goals and trajectory constraints can both be stated as preferences, so a condition over time can be either required or only preferred.

\subsection{Conclusions}
Each line of work covers part of the problem. Reward Machines expose the structure of a reward but are built by hand. ARM-FM generates them from language, and its guarantee holds only if the generated machine meets conditions that no automatic step checks. LTLf gives task statements a precise meaning and a route to automata, and planning separates the constraints that must hold from the preferences that only cost. Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal} states the aims that follow from them, and Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} combines these pieces.

\section{Critique of the state of the art}
\label{sec:critique}
\subsection{Narrow task variety}
The tasks that ARM-FM evaluates are simple in what they ask: most are a list of steps that ends in one goal. Its MiniGrid tasks are long and hard to explore, as the paper stresses \cite[pp.~5, 14]{creus2026armfm}, but what they ask is still such a list. In DoorKey the agent `finds a key`, `unlocks a door` and `reaches the goal`; in BlockedUnlockPickup it `moves a ball out of the way`, `takes a key`, `unlocks a door` and `picks up a box`; in Craftium it gathers `wood`, `stone`, `iron` and `diamond` in this order; etc.

Each machine is a chain of states, one per step, with at most a few edges that skip a step or go back after a loss. No task states a rule that must hold throughout the episode, and none separates what must happen from what is only preferable. 

\subsection{Inconsistent  and unstructured generation}
Language models do not return the same output on every run, even with the temperature set to zero. Ouyang et al.~\cite{ouyang2025empirical} measured this for ChatGPT on 829 code-generation problems, and the same task can therefore yield different machines. ARM-FM puts no fixed structure on what the model writes: the machine is plain text in a format described by the prompt, and the rewards are free numbers.

Free rewards allow loops that pay without progress. Proposition~1 of ARM-FM guarantees that optimising the LARM also solves the sparse task, provided the machine has no loop that pays (a cycle whose rewards add up to more than zero) and its final reward is larger than what any run that does not finish collects \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. The paper describes no automatic check of these conditions; the critic prompt only asks the model to verify that regressions are penalised, that reward magnitudes do not allow reward hacking and that there are no unintentional loops \cite[p.~27]{creus2026armfm}. 

This means the check fully relies on LLM inference, and \textbf{thus is not robust}. The Meta-World machine printed in the paper breaks the first condition: the cycle $u_0 \to u_1 \to u_2 \to u_0$ pays $+0.20$, $+0.40$ and $-0.40$, a total of $+0.20$ per lap \cite[p.~32]{creus2026armfm}, although the paper states that its LARMs satisfy the property \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. The DoorKey loop that pays $+0.2$ for picking the key up and $-0.2$ for dropping it sums to zero, so it meets the condition because two chosen values cancel, and nothing in the method enforces it.

A critic is itself a language model, and language models can state things that are false \cite{huang2025survey}. The survey covers generated text in general, and it warns that methods that rely on a model's self-correction ``may be unreliable'' \cite[Section~5.3.1]{huang2025survey}. A critic's approval is therefore no guarantee either; this is an inference, not a measured result.

\subsection{Verification by a human or another model}
ARM-FM states the limitation itself: human verification during RM generation is a trade-off that presupposes access to human verifiers. The authors add that such verifiers are not strictly required, although they can improve the output, and leave reducing this dependence by exploiting the automaton structure, for example through formal verification, as future work \cite[pp.~9--10]{creus2026armfm}. 

In 3 of the 7 settings of its Table~3 a human corrected or extended the machine: in UnlockPickup a human asked for a transition for a dropped key, which the model then added; in KeyCorridorS3R3 a human advised intermediate rewards; and in Meta-World a human rescaled the reward values \cite[p.~20]{creus2026armfm}. Some others do not need any human intevertion, which is worth noting.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The ARM-FM paper releases no code. Its appendices print the generator and critic prompts, six Reward Machines, the labelling functions of four MiniGrid tasks and the hyperparameters. A public repository of the first author \cite{armfm_code}, which the paper does not reference, adds prompts, generated machines and labelling code for four MiniGrid tasks only. This material allows the method to be rebuilt but not its exact outputs, so the ARM-FM baseline of this thesis is a reconstruction whose results may differ from the original ones. The authors did not answer the contact attempts made for further material.

\chapter{Proposal}
\label{chap:proposal}
The methods of Chapter~\ref{chap:sota} leave three needs open: a reward that says everything a person means, machines that need no expert to check them, and the same kind of machine for the same kind of task.

Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} presents a method built for these three aims and says how it meets each one.

\section{Understanding preferences and temporal relations}
An RL agent pursues whatever its reward pays for, so the reward is the way a person tells the agent what to achieve \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. AI alignment aims to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values \cite{ji2025alignment}. For an RL agent, one part of this aim is a reward that says everything the person means: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.

A task is rarely a single chain of steps. A person says, in plain language, what to do, in which order, what to do in response to an event and what must never happen. The first aim is to catch these relations of order, response and prohibition, together with the person's preferences, in a shape from which a reward can be built. That needs a translator from human intent into a language that encodes such relations exactly, a part that a foundation model can play.

Each statement is either hard or soft. A hard statement must hold for the task to count as done. A soft statement is a preference: the agent may give it up, at a cost. This split between constraints and preferences comes from planning (Section~\ref{sec:sota-preferences}), and the aim is to carry it into Reward Machines.

\section{Machines without a human expert}
ARM-FM relies on a human or on a second model to verify its machines, and its authors name human verification as a trade-off of their approach (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). The second aim is generation without a human expert: a person states the task, and no one who knows Reward Machines or reinforcement learning has to check or repair the machine. The properties that make a machine safe to train on, such as having no loop that pays, should follow from how the machine is built, and not from a reviewer who may miss them.

\section{Same kind of task, same kind of machine}
\label{sec:schemas}
A language model that writes a machine freely may write a different one on every run (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). The third aim is that tasks of one kind share a schema, the form of the task with its objects left open, and that one schema gives one kind of machine. \textit{Gather wood, then stone, then iron} and \textit{attend the client, prepare the order, then serve it} share the schema ``first $a$, then $b$, then $c$''; Table~\ref{tab:schemas} gives both with their clauses and formulas. Their machines should then have the same shape, with only the objects exchanged, so that the result is predictable and the variability of the language model is confined to how it reads the task. This is an aim, not a guarantee: tasks of one kind get one kind of machine only when the model reads them into the same schema. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} measures the closest case, whether the rewordings of one task get the same machine (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}).

\chapter{FARM}
\label{chap:farm}
% \section{Introduction and motivation}
This chapter describes the process by which FARM (A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation) turns a task written in plain language into a Reward Machine. The implementation details, with the list of what FARM adds to the tools it builds on, are in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}. FARM replaces only the generation of the Reward Machine in ARM-FM (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}); the rest of the ARM-FM pipeline stays as it is.

Each aim of Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal} rests on one tool:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A language model reads the task and the environment description, and states what the person means: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules and the preferences.
    \item The Declare templates of IBM nl2ltl guide the model: it picks a template and fills it, and the template writes the formula.
    \item LTLf encodes these statements as formulas over finite traces (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}).
    \item FL-AT and MONA convert each formula into an automaton and combine the automata into one machine.
    \item FARM's post-process builds the Reward Machine from the chosen clauses alone.
    \item A second language model, the critic, reviews the clauses against the task.
\end{itemize}

\section{Global overview of the pipeline}
\label{sec:overview}
FARM turns a natural-language task into a Reward Machine in five steps: task interpretation (Step~1), LTLf to DFA (Step~2), the product of the DFAs (Step~3), the post-process (Step~4) and the critic (Step~5). A language model chooses clauses, fixed tools compile them into a reward machine, a post-process adds the rewards and the structure of the task, and a second model reviews the result. 

Steps 2 to 4 form the \textbf{compiler}: they call no language model and read only the chosen clauses and the run settings, making it the deterministic part of the pipeline. The two language models of Steps 1 and 5 are therefore the only source of variability.

\subsection{IBM nl2ltl}
IBM's nl2ltl \cite{fuggitti2023nl2ltl} goes from natural language to LTLf through templates. A language model chooses which templates to use and which propositions fill them, and each template maps to a fixed LTLf formula in which only the propositions change. 

All the work therefore lies in the choice: a template is a fixed pattern with holes, and the model only says \textbf{which pattern to use and what goes in the holes}. The choice is still hard, since the model has to understand the task, its order and the dependencies between its steps, rules and goals. 

FARM keeps the template classes of nl2ltl, improves its performance with prompting, and replaces its engine (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}) so other LLMs can be used.

The formulas use four operators (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}): $\textbf{F}\,a$ means that $a$ holds at some step from now on, $\textbf{G}\,a$ that $a$ holds at every step from now on, $\textbf{X}\,a$ that $a$ holds at the next step, and $a\ \textbf{U}\ b$ that $b$ holds at some step and $a$ holds at every step before it.

Response and Precedence are easy to confuse, and both allow the same step. Response$(a,b)$ lets $b$ appear without $a$, but \text{after} an $a$ a $b$ must follow, at the same step or later. Precedence$(a,b)$ lets $a$ appear without $b$, but a $b$ needs an $a$ \text{before} it or at the same step.

\subsection{Goals and rules}
A clause is a \textbf{goal} if it asks for something to happen, and a \textbf{rule} if it only limits what may happen. One test tells them apart: after a step in which \textbf{nothing happens}, that is, no proposition holds, a goal is not satisfied yet, while a rule is. 

For example, Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals since by doing nothing, they can not be satisfied. The other six templates are rules, Precedence included: they hold while nothing happens, and a later event may break them or make them wait for an answer. For example, if $b$ happens before $a$, Precedence$(a,b)$ is broken, but if nothing happens, it still holds.

The compiler applies the test to the DFA of each clause, so it needs no list of templates. The difference shapes Step~4: only a hard goal gives the machine a final state, and progress counts the stages still needed to reach it.

\subsection{The generator prompt}
IBM's default prompt is a few-shot list that offers five of the eight templates, with examples from business applications such as Slack or Salesforce, no definitions and no description of the environment. \textbf{FARM's prompt offers all eight}, adding Absence, Precedence and NotCoExistence. It gives the model the catalogue with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The meaning, kind and formula of each template, 
    \item A table from typical wording to template, 
    \item Worked examples, 
    \item The instructions for hard and soft, 
    \item The \textbf{environment description} in Markdown, 
    \item The \textbf{task} 
    \item And, on a retry, the comments of the critic.
\end{itemize}

The propositions come from the environment description, so the same LLM prompt serves any environment.
    
Three instructions shape the answer. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A step the task asks for gets its own goal, a hard Existence, unless a hard order already puts it before a later step. A single ``do $a$'' gets one, and so does the last step of an order; the earlier steps need none, since the order makes them come first. Without that goal nothing makes the agent take the step, because a rule holds while nothing happens.
    \item When the task states an order, the order stays as a Precedence even if the world already forces it, so the machine follows the instruction and not one layout of the world.
    \item When the task states no order, none is added. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives the prompt text and the form of an ordered task.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hard and soft clauses}
Each clause carries a \textbf{priority that the model chooses for that clause alone}. Clauses are hard by default. The model marks a clause soft only when the task uses a preference cue such as ``if possible'', ``ideally'' or ``try to'', and the cue covers the phrase it qualifies, not the whole sentence or the task. 

The priority never changes the formula. A hard clause must hold for the task to be completed. A soft clause is a preference: breaking it costs reward but never prevents finishing (Step~4). IBM's templates have no such notion, since \textit{never touch the lava} and \textit{if possible, never touch the lava} are the same Absence clause, so the priority has to come from the prompt. For FARM, the first would be a hard Absence and the second a soft one.

\subsection{FL-AT and MONA}
FL-AT, the Formal Language--Automaton Transmogrifier \cite{transmogrifier2020}, writes each LTLf formula in monadic second-order logic over finite strings, the input language of MONA. In that encoding a temporal operator becomes a quantifier over the positions of the trace, and a proposition becomes the set of positions where it holds. MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} decides a formula of this logic by building the minimal DFA of its models.

The fork of FL-AT that FARM uses runs MONA as a separate process and reads back the automaton that MONA prints: its initial state, its accepting states and its transitions, each with a guard over the propositions. The reading is strict, and a malformed automaton stops the build (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The fork changes two things: it drops MONA's first state when that state is redundant (see next at Section~\ref{sec:redundant}), and it returns the clause states behind every product state (Step~3).

\subsection{Redundant states in MONA's automata}
\label{sec:redundant}
MONA returns the smallest automaton for each formula, but two of its states do no work in a Reward Machine. Figure~\ref{fig:mona-stages} in Appendix~\ref{app:mona} shows both for the clause Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{key\_lost}$, but they are described here:

\paragraph{A pass-through first state.} MONA's automaton can begin with a state that every input leaves for the same next state: in panel~(a), S0 moves to S1 whatever happens. The FL-AT fork removes such a state and starts the automaton at its successor, which gives panel~(b). To be safe, it removes the state only when it is not accepting, has one successor, is never entered again and has transitions that cover every input exactly once. Otherwise the automaton stays as MONA made it.

\paragraph{A start for a run with no steps.} In a rule clause, MONA keeps a separate start for the moment before the first step. In panel~(b), S1 is that start. It moves exactly like S2, the state that a step without \texttt{key\_lost} leads to, and differs from S2 only in that it does not accept, because the automaton does not accept a run with no steps. An episode always has at least one step, so the difference never matters, and Step~4 merges S1 into S2 (Section~\ref{sec:start-merge}), which gives panel~(c).

\subsection{The product of the clause DFAs}
The idea of the product is to run the clause automata side by side. Each automaton follows its own clause, and after every step each one is in one of its states. A state of the product records where all of them are at that moment. In the \runex{careful key} it is a pair, written as (S1, S2): the state of Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}) first and the state of Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}) second.

A step of the agent moves every automaton at once, each along the edge that the events of the step choose in it. An edge of the product is therefore one edge of each automaton, taken together in the same step. Its guard asks for all of their guards to hold, so it joins them with \textit{and}, and it leads to the product state made of their destinations. When two of these guards contradict, as when one asks for \texttt{door\_open} and the other for $\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}$, no step can take both edges, and the product has no such edge.

FL-AT builds the product from its start state, which holds the start state of every automaton. It adds the edges out of that state, then the edges out of every new state they reach, until no new state appears. The product therefore contains only the combinations of states that some run can reach.

The \runex{careful key} example shows each part. Both automata start in S1, so the product starts in p0 = (S1, S1). In S1, Existence has two edges: $\neg\,\texttt{at\_goal}$ stays in S1 and \texttt{at\_goal} goes to S2. Absence has two as well: $\neg\,\texttt{key\_lost}$ goes to S2 and \texttt{key\_lost} to S3. One edge from each gives four combinations, so p0 has four edges. A step that reaches the goal and keeps the key, for instance, takes \texttt{at\_goal} in Existence and $\neg\,\texttt{key\_lost}$ in Absence, so its product edge has the guard $\texttt{at\_goal} \land \neg\,\texttt{key\_lost}$ and leads to p3 = (S2, S2). The other three edges lead to p1 = (S1, S2), where nothing has happened yet, p2 = (S1, S3), where the key is lost, and p4 = (S2, S3), where the goal is reached and the key lost. No guards contradict here, since the two clauses share no proposition. The edges out of p1 to p4 reach no new pair, so the product has the five states of Table~\ref{tab:run-product}. The two automata could form $2 \times 3 = 6$ pairs, but (S2, S1) never occurs: Absence leaves S1 on the first step and never comes back.

Before the product, Step~4 re-arms the automata of soft clauses (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}); the table and Figure~\ref{fig:run-product3} show the product of the automata as MONA returns them, which is also the product of the task with a hard Absence.

\subsection{Discarded features of FL-AT's product}
\label{sec:flat-discard}
FL-AT also prices the product and marks its final states, and FARM replaces both. FARM needs rewards that pay for each step of progress, never pay for going round a loop, and price a broken hard rule apart from a broken preference, and it needs final states that only the hard clauses decide. FL-AT's pricing gives none of these.

FL-AT pays each edge one unit for every clause whose DFA accepts after the step. This rewards the agent before the task is done, a simple form of shaping, but it falls short in four ways.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{It pays for staying, not for progress.} A clause pays on every step it spends accepting, not once when it is met, so a loop through an accepting state keeps paying, and a rule that holds from the start pays the agent for doing nothing. Such a reward is not potential-based shaping, so it can change which behaviour is best \cite{ng1999policy}: the agent may earn more by staying than by finishing. FARM pays for the change in the stages still owed, so every loop adds up to zero, and its loop check refuses any machine with a loop that pays (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Progress that completes no clause pays nothing.} A clause pays only once its DFA accepts. In the \runex{sequence} example, taking the key pays the same as a step in which nothing happens, because the Precedence of key and door accepts both before and after it. FARM pays $+1$ for each stage a step closes.
    \item \textbf{A break has no price of its own.} FL-AT has no hard or soft clauses: each clause is worth one unit. A broken clause just stops paying, one unit less on that step and on every later one, so the cost of a break depends on the steps left, not on what was broken, and an early break costs more than a late one. Nothing ends the episode either, so after a broken hard rule the agent still collects the units of the other clauses. FARM sends a hard break to the well, which ends the episode with a set charge (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}), and charges $-1$ for each soft break when it happens (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
    \item \textbf{The final states follow from the rewards.} FL-AT marks as final the destinations of the edges with the largest reward. A broken preference then stops the task from finishing, and in a task made only of rules every clause already accepts after a step in which nothing happens, so that step would finish the task. FARM marks a state final when every hard clause holds, ignores the soft ones, and gives a task with no hard goal no final state (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
\end{enumerate}

The loop on p1 pays 1 at every step in which nothing happens, because the Absence clause accepts (point 1). If p3 ended the episode, an agent that idles in p1 until the step limit would earn more than one that reaches the goal early. \subsection{Clause states}
Every state of a clause DFA gets one of three labels. It is \textbf{satisfied} when the DFA accepts there: the trace so far meets the clause. It is \textbf{pending} when the DFA does not accept yet but can still reach an accepting state, and \textbf{violated} when it can no longer reach one. The labels are exact, computed by reachability on the DFA of each clause, and through the tuples of Step~3 every product state knows the label of each of its clauses.

\subsection{Re-arming soft rules}
\label{sec:recurrent}
An LTLf formula gives one verdict per trace, so a broken soft rule would be charged \textbf{ONLY once} per episode however often it is broken again. FARM \textbf{re-arms} a soft rule instead: 

\keyidea{The violated state of its DFA drops its own edges and takes those of the start, so the rule watches for the next break, and every new break is charged again.} 

This only applies to the four templates whose DFA has a violated state: Absence, Precedence, ChainResponse and NotCoExistence. The other four, Existence, ExistenceTwo, RespondedExistence and Response, have no violated state, but a soft one still moves progress while it is pending: a soft Response trigger can cost $-1$ at once and its answer pay the $+1$ back, and finishing with the clause still pending costs $-5$.

A soft Absence is \textbf{folded} as well: in the product, the states in which it is violated merge into the matching states in which it is satisfied, and the break becomes a mark on the edge. The clause then adds no state to the machine, and its break is a loop that pays $-1$ (Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}). Re-arming and folding are settings, both on by default; with re-arming off, a soft rule costs one charge per episode.

To acomplish this, we dont use any table of 'template-conversion', we use computed reachability on the DFA and deterministic process. The listing we give aboved is just the result of those eight we have, but in the case of adding more, those would get automatically processed by the same algorithm.

\subsection{Final states and the well}
\label{sec:final-well}
FARM drops the final states that FL-AT marks on the product (Section~\ref{sec:flat-discard}), so the machine still has to say where an episode ends. Two kinds of state end it: the well, entered when the task can no longer be completed, and the final states, reached when it is done. Only the hard clauses decide which states these are.

\keyidea{A product state in which a hard clause is violated can never complete the task, so every edge into such a state goes to one shared \textbf{well}, a rejecting state that ends the episode.}

\noindent When the task has a hard goal, the edges into states from which no final state can be reached go to the well too. The final RM can work with one well, because the price of a break sits on the edge into it, so each edge can pay its own price (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).

\keyidea{A \textbf{final state} is a state in which every hard clause is satisfied, and only a task with a hard goal has one.}

The edges out of a final state are dropped, so reaching it ends the episode, and the saved machine merges all final states into one.

\paragraph{Soft clauses} They never affect the final condition: a preference broken on the way still lets the task finish, at a cost. In the \runex{careful key} the goal is the only hard clause, so the goal is final with or without the key (Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}). A hard clause that is only pending, such as a Response still waiting for its answer, blocks finishing but does not end the episode.

\paragraph{Unreachible states}States that the start cannot reach are pruned. In a task with a hard goal, if no reachable edge enters a final state, the clauses conflict and the build fails; a rule-only task compiles with no final state.

\subsection{Merging MONA's start state}
\label{sec:start-merge}
MONA gives each rule a ``no step yet'' start (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}), and the product inherits it as a start that no edge re-enters. Its \textbf{idle successor} is the state it moves to on a step in which no proposition holds. The idle successor becomes the start, and the old start is dropped, when three conditions hold: no edge re-enters the start, the idle successor is neither the start, the well nor a final state, and both states move to the same state on every input. Otherwise the machine is unchanged.

The merge makes the machine smaller. It is safe because no template of the catalogue is broken by a step in which nothing happens, so the start and its idle successor are priced alike. On 25 sample machines and 3,512 clause sets, the machines built with and without the merge had the same rewards, final states and wells.

\subsection{Reward assignment}
\label{sec:reward-assignment}
With the final states and the well in place, every edge of the machine still needs a price. FARM sets these prices itself, in place of FL-AT's one unit per accepting clause (Section~\ref{sec:flat-discard}), so that the agent is paid for progress rather than for staying, and every break has a price of its own.

Every price serves one order between the runs an agent can take. A \textbf{finished} run is the best run to the final state. A \textbf{started} run is the best run with a return of zero or more to a state closer to the final state than the start, measured in the fewest events still needed to finish. A \textbf{not started} run is one in which nothing happens, \textbf{soft break} is the cost of breaking soft rules and \textbf{hard break} the mildest edge into the well. A machine must pay them in this order:

\keyidea{\centering\upshape\bfseries\boldmath finished $>$ started $>$ not started $\geq$ soft break $>$ hard break}
\noindent Not started may tie with a soft break. Every reward of this section is set to keep this order. To keep it, the rewards follow one principle: getting closer to finishing the task or a rule \textbf{pays}, and stepping back from it, breaking a rule, or getting a new thing to do \textbf{costs}. A new thing to do can come from the agent, which triggered a rule, or from the environment, which changed so that the instructions now ask for a reaction.

Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, at the end of the section, gives every value. To clarify the rewards with an example, Appendix~\ref{sec:rewards-example} follows them step by step on one task, the \runex{dropped key}: what each state still owes, what each step pays and what five runs total.

\subsubsection{Progress}
Each step is paid first for its progress, measured by how much less it leaves to do. Progress is what puts a started run above a not started one. 

A \textbf{stage} is one event still needed, and every state owes a number of stages $\Phi(u)$. With a final state, $\Phi(u)$ counts the fewest events still needed to finish plus, for each soft clause (a soft goal included), the longest run of events its own automaton may still need; without one, every clause counts its own events (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). A step from $u$ to $u'$ pays
\[ r_{\mathrm{progress}}(u,u') = \Phi(u) - \Phi(u'), \]
\gain{one unit} for each stage it closes and \loss{minus one} for each stage it adds. This is potential-based shaping \cite{ng1999policy} with the potential $-\Phi$ and no discount, so around any loop the progress adds up to zero. The learner does discount its rewards, so the guarantee that shaping leaves the best behaviour unchanged holds only approximately. A final state owes nothing, so the finishing edge pays every stage still owed. 

Two exceptions apply: a step that breaks a soft rule pays no progress for that rule, and an edge into the well takes progress back (see \emph{Taking progress back} below).

\subsubsection{Completion and charges}
Besides progress, a few events have a fixed price. Completion puts a finished run at the top of the order, and the charges keep a soft break no better than a not started run. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Completion pays \gain{$+10$} on top of the progress of the finishing step. 
    \item Each new break of a re-armed soft rule is charged again, \loss{$-1$} per break (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}), so a repeated break is never free. 
    \item A soft clause still pending when the run finishes is charged \loss{$-5$} once, on the finishing edge, so a preference that was never met costs reward without blocking the end. 
    \item A soft break costs \loss{$-1$}, a tenth of completion, so one preference weighs less than finishing the task.
    \item A hard break costs \loss{$-10$} when the task has no soft clause. When the task also has soft clauses, the price grows with the step limit, about \loss{$-96$} for 300 steps, so that a hard break still costs more than breaking soft rules at every step and stays at the bottom of the order (see \emph{The price of a hard break} below).
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{The price of a hard break}
\phantomsection\label{sec:well-charge}
A hard break sends the run to the well, and its price must keep the hard break at the bottom of the order. In a task \textbf{without} a soft clause, an edge into the well pays the flat price of Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, \loss{$-10$}. Now, in the other case:

\keyidea{\textbf{With} a soft clause in the task, a hard break must cost more than breaking soft rules at every step until the episode ends; otherwise an agent facing many soft breaks could prefer the well.}
\noindent The edge into the well then pays
\[ -\left(\max\!\left(10,\; N c\,\frac{1-\gamma^{T}}{1-\gamma}\right) + 1\right), \]
where $c$ is the price of a soft break ($1$), $N$ the number of soft rules that can break in one step (a setting, assumes $1$ by default), $\gamma$ the discount ($0.99$) and $T$ the step limit of the environment. The fraction is the discounted number of steps in an episode of $T$ steps. 

With a step limit of 300 steps, the discount $0.99$, one soft rule per step and a soft-break price of $1$, the price is about \loss{$-96$} ($-96.096$): the discounted cost of breaking a soft rule at every one of the 300 steps, plus one. The price grows with the step limit and is never milder than \loss{$-11$}. The order holds only if no more than $N$ soft rules can break in one step, and the compiler does not check $N$ against the task. 

\paragraph{Taking progress back}
Progress pays as the run gets closer to the end, so a run that breaks a hard rule late has already been paid for the stages it closed. Without a correction, the same break would cost less the later it came, and the hard break could climb up the order.

\keyidea{The edge into the well takes back the progress the run made, so a hard break late in the run costs no less than one at the first step.}
\noindent A state is \textbf{ahead} of the start when it owes fewer stages than the start. The edge into the well takes \loss{one unit} for each stage by which the state it leaves is ahead, and nothing when that state is not ahead. The soft-rule charges paid on the way are not taken back.

In the \runex{sequence} machine (Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1}), the edge from u0 into the well pays \loss{$-10$}, and the edge from u1, one stage ahead of the start, pays \loss{$-11$}. A run that picks up the key and then reaches the goal with the door closed therefore totals \loss{$-10$}, the same as a break at the first step.

\subsubsection{Checks}
The rewards are set to keep the order of the start of this section, and three checks look at each built machine to catch one that does not pay as it should. They differ in what they look for, in when they run and in what a failure does:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loop check.} It searches the machine for a loop whose rewards add up to more than zero, counting every step without discount; an agent could circle such a loop for ever and earn more than by finishing: what is known as Reward Farming. It runs on every build and refuses the machine if it finds one.
    \item \textbf{Pay order.} It checks that the machine pays its five runs in the order of the start of this section; when the optional end charge is on (see \emph{Summary of the rewards} below), the started run includes it. It is a report, written when FARM prepares a machine for training and the step limit of the environment is known. A failed order prints a warning and stops nothing, and a machine built alone with the \texttt{generate-rm} command gets no report.
    \item \textbf{Plan check.} It plays the scripted plans of each task of the benchmark through the machine and reads what each plan pays (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). A recorded failure stops that machine from training, while a missing record or an unchecked result does not.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rule-only machines}
\phantomsection\label{sec:rule-only}
Some tasks only set rules, such as \textit{never touch the lava}, and ask for nothing to happen. A task with no hard goal has no final state. Only the well or the environment ends the episode, and the compiler warns that the task has no goal. 

\keyidea{Progress then counts every clause on its own and aims at the states where no clause is pending rather than to the goal.}

A step into a state from which no state with nothing pending can be reached pays no progress: the rules can no longer all be met, so there is nothing left to get closer to. 

\paragraph{Rule triggers} A rule that waits for an event, such as a Response, owes a stage once its trigger happens, whether the agent or the environment caused it: the trigger costs \loss{one unit} \textbf{per stage at once}, and the reaction that the rule asks for pays it back. A trigger that is answered therefore totals 0, the same as no trigger. With no final state, the pay order skips the comparison of started and not started.

\subsection{What the critic reads and checks}
Only the choice of clauses varies from run to run: Steps~2 to~4 turn the same clauses into the same machine every time. One review at the end therefore covers the whole pipeline, and it looks at the clauses.

\keyidea{The critic judges whether the clauses say what the task says (if they are the right selection). It reads the machine only to see what the clauses mean in practice, and does not judge its shape or its rewards, which follow from the clauses.}
\noindent The critic reads six inputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The environment description and the task, the same two that the generator read;
    \item The clauses and the machine compiled from them, which shows what the clauses do in practice: an empty set of final states, for instance, shows that a hard goal is missing;
    \item The \textbf{compiler clues}, which the compiler computes exactly from the clauses and the machine: each rule in plain words, the hard clauses that the others already imply, and the events that every way to finish includes;
    \item The \textbf{warnings}, which flag a task with no goal and a soft clause that changes no reward.
\end{itemize}

\noindent A clause passes when it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Comes from a sentence of the task;
    \item Uses the right template and argument order;
    \item Carries the priority that its wording asks for;
    \item Uses the declared propositions.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The critic also rejects a clause whose event can never occur in the environment and a hard Existence that the other clauses already imply. It does not judge the topology of the machine, its number of states or the size of its rewards, and it treats the warnings, such as a task with no goal, as valid output. Its verdict is either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or a list of changes that starts with \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED}.

\subsection{Fixing mistakes}
A rejection starts a new attempt. The generator's prompt then carries the history of the task's earlier attempts: for each, the clauses it proposed, the machine compiled from them when one was built, and why the attempt failed, the comments of the critic or the error of the compiler. The generator proposes new clauses, from which the compiler rebuilds the machine (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The critic sees none of this history and judges every attempt afresh. One stored case shows such a repair.

\textbf{Task.} \textit{Try to move the ball off the door before you take the key. Pick up the box in the other room.}

\textbf{First answer.} Two clauses: a hard Existence(\texttt{target\_picked\_up}) and a soft Precedence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}, \texttt{has\_door\_key}).

\textbf{Critic.} It asked for a soft Existence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}) as well, ``so the preferred move is expressed''.

\textbf{Accepted answer.} The second answer, with three clauses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Existence(\texttt{target\_picked\_up}), hard: $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{target\_picked\_up}$;
    \item Existence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}), soft: $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{blocker\_moved}$;
    \item Precedence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}, \texttt{has\_door\_key}), soft:\[(\neg\texttt{has\_door\_key}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{blocker\_moved}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\texttt{has\_door\_key}\]
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Different LLMs for generation and critique and bias}
A review helps only when it does not come from the model that wrote the clauses to aviod any inherited model bias. Generator and critic are therefore configured separately, and the build refuses a critic that resolves to the same model as the generator. The frozen pipeline uses \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} as generator and \texttt{glm-5.3-flash} as critic.

\noindent To further more this 'no-bias', the critic also sees none of the earlier attempts. 

\subsection{Clauses and environment facts}
The critic reads the environment description, but it judges the clauses against the task, not against the layout of one environment.

\keyidea{The machine matches what the task says, not what one environment happens to force.}
\noindent The critic rejects a clause set that drops an order the task states, even when the environment already forces that order, and it never asks for an order the task does not state. As a result, a machine can hold edges that the environment never takes. 

The \runex{sequence} machine (Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1}) has edges out of u0 on an open door without the key and on the goal before the door, while in DoorKey only the key opens the locked door. These edges never fire, so \textbf{they pay nothing in training}. Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations} discusses what the compiler cannot know about the environment.

\subsection{Things to keep in mind}
Two points bound what this step can promise: both models can be wrong, and thinking level of the models.

\subsubsection{LLMs can hallucinate}
The critic can hallucinate as well as the generator \cite{huang2025survey}.

\keyidea{The compiler builds the machine exactly from the chosen clauses and adds nothing they do not state, so a wrong machine always comes from a wrong clause set: \textbf{the generator proposed it and the critic missed it}.}


\subsubsection{Thinking level of the models}
Both models can think before they answer. Thinking levels go from none to low, medium, high or max. The default, and the level of the frozen pipeline, is max. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} shows how the share of correct machines changes with the level.

\subsection{Running examples: the verdict of the critic}
\label{sec:run-step5}
The critic accepted the three machines of Step~4 at the first attempt, each time with the verdict \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED}. The generator therefore did not try again, and the machines of Step~4 are the final output of FARM.

\section{The output format}
\label{sec:output-format}
The Reward Machine is what FARM hands over to training. FARM writes it in the plain-text format of ARM-FM (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}), whose training it reuses, and extends that format; Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} lists the differences.

\subsection{The fields}
Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1} shows the \runex{sequence} machine in this format.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=rm,caption={The Reward Machine that FARM builds for the \runex{sequence} example.},label={lst:farm-example1}]
REWARD_MACHINE:
STATES: u0, u1, u2, u3, u4
INITIAL_STATE: u0
FINAL_STATES: u4
REJECTING_STATES: u2
DEFAULT_REWARD: 0
TRANSITION_FUNCTION:
(u0, !at_goal&!door_open&has_key) -> u1
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&!has_key) -> u2
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&has_key) -> u3
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&!has_key) -> u2
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&has_key) -> u2
(u0, at_goal&door_open&!has_key) -> u2
(u0, at_goal&door_open&has_key) -> u4
(u1, !at_goal&door_open) -> u3
(u1, at_goal&!door_open) -> u2
(u1, at_goal&door_open) -> u4
(u3, at_goal) -> u4
REWARD_FUNCTION:
(u0, !at_goal&!door_open&has_key, u1) -> 1
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&!has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&has_key, u3) -> 2
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&!has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, at_goal&door_open&!has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, at_goal&door_open&has_key, u4) -> 13
(u1, !at_goal&door_open, u3) -> 1
(u1, at_goal&!door_open, u2) -> -11
(u1, at_goal&door_open, u4) -> 12
(u3, at_goal, u4) -> 11
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}

\noindent After the \texttt{REWARD\_MACHINE:} header come seven fields:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{STATES}: every state.
    \item \texttt{INITIAL\_STATE}: the start.
    \item \texttt{FINAL\_STATES}: the final state, at most one; empty when the task has no hard goal.
    \item \texttt{REJECTING\_\allowbreak STATES}: the well, left out when there is none.
    \item \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD}: the reward of a step that matches no transition. The machine then stays in its state and pays 0.
    \item \texttt{TRANSITION\_\allowbreak FUNCTION}: one line per transition, \texttt{(source, event) -> destination}, where the event combines propositions with \texttt{!} for ``not'' and \texttt{\&} for ``and''.
    \item \texttt{REWARD\_FUNCTION}: the reward of each transition, \texttt{(source, event, destination) -> reward}, leaving out the transitions that pay 0.
\end{itemize}

In the listing, u0 is the start, u1 holds the key, u3 holds the key with the door open, u4 is the final state and u2 the well. Each stage closed pays \gain{$+1$} and the goal adds \gain{$+10$}, so doing all three steps at once pays \gain{$+13$}. Every way to break the order costs \loss{$-10$}, or \loss{$-11$} from u1, which is one stage ahead of the start (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}, \emph{Taking progress back}).

\subsection{The machine in training}
Two more rules decide how the machine meets training: when an episode ends, and which reward the agent is trained on. A machine ends an episode when: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item It enters its \textbf{final} state 
    \item It enters the \textbf{rejecting well}.
    \item Otherwise the \textbf{environment's own end} 
    \item Its \textbf{time limit} ends the episode.
\end{itemize}


\noindent The setting \texttt{reward\_source} picks the reward:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{rm}, the default: the reward of the machine alone, plus a flat time-limit penalty (default \loss{$-5$}) on the last step of an episode that times out. The end-charge option of Table~\ref{tab:rewards} replaces that flat charge.
    \item \texttt{both}: the reward of the machine plus the reward of the environment.
    \item \texttt{env}: the reward of the environment alone, with no machine.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Limitations}
\label{chap:limitations}

A limit, in this chapter, is something FARM does not do or does not guarantee today. FARM's compiler knows the task only through the clauses the generator chooses while it does not know the world at all; the generator and the critic know the world only through the environment description. 

Each limit below is given with a case, so that a person knows what to avoid when writing a task or describing an environment. The first two limits concern the machines the compiler builds, and the other five what a task can state and how the generator reads it.

\section{States the world rules out}
\label{sec:lim-impossible}
A machine can hold states that no run in its world ever visits. The compiler prunes the states that no path from the start reaches (Section~\ref{sec:step4}), but it knows nothing of the world, so it keeps the states and transitions that only the world rules out. 

This follows from a choice of the generator prompt: an order that the task states stays in the machine even when the world already forces it, so the machine follows the instruction and not one layout of the world (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). The \runex{sequence} machine of Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1} shows the result. It has transitions out of u0 on an open door without the key and on the goal before the door, while in DoorKey only the key opens the locked door (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). Such a transition never fires, and a state that only such transitions enter is never visited. These states and transitions make the machine bigger and harder to read, but they \textbf{do not make training worse}.

FARM does not cut them, because a cut needs facts about the world that are certain. The constraints an environment description states rest on reading the code of the world and on random episodes, which can show a constraint false but never prove it. A wrong cut would also fail silently: if the world did take a cut transition, the machine would have no edge for it, so it would stay in its state and pay nothing.

\section{Machines grow with the clauses}
\label{sec:lim-size}
A FARM machine is the product of one automaton per clause, so it can grow exponentially with the number of clauses: on the tasks of the shop, a benchmark world where the agent takes orders, cooks and serves (Table~\ref{tab:eval-suites}), each added rule that waits for an answer nearly doubles the states (Table~\ref{tab:res-shop}). 

On the trained tasks where both FARM and ARM-FM built a machine, FARM's machines are larger than ARM-FM's (Table~\ref{tab:res-size}), and the largest machine, that of a shop task with six hard rules, has more than ten thousand transitions (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). A machine of that size still follows its clauses exactly, but it is hard for a person to read. Section~\ref{sec:fw-size} takes it up.

\section{A rule alone does not make the agent act}
\label{sec:lim-rules}
Whether an agent has a reason to act depends on the kind of clause its task holds. Of the eight templates, only Existence and ExistenceTwo ask for something to happen. The other six are rules, hold while nothing happens (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).

A task made of rules alone is therefore kept by an agent that does nothing, unless the world sets the rules off by itself or produces some event the agent reacts to. Apart from such a world, only a goal makes the agent act. 

\keyidea{A person who wants the agent to act has to state a goal, or make the world cause the agent to react.}

This is intended, since a rule says what must not happen or how to answer an event, not that the agent must begin anything. It limits what such a task can test. The benchmark leaves out of training the tasks with no goal that an agent doing nothing passes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}), and it adds worlds that keep setting the rules off: the zombie lanes, where zombies enter three lanes by themselves, and the shop, that for example rings a siren the agent has to turn off.


\section{Ambiguous wording}
\label{sec:lim-ambiguous}
Everyday language is ambiguous: a sentence can read two ways, and the generator may take either. 

The task \textit{Try not to let zombies through both the top lane and the side lanes} can mean 
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``not the two together'', a soft NotCoExistence(\texttt{top\_\allowbreak breach}, \texttt{side\_\allowbreak breach}), $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{top\_breach} \rightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{side\_breach}$, 
    \item or ``neither one nor the other'', a soft Absence(\texttt{top\_\allowbreak breach}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{top\_breach}$, and a soft Absence(\texttt{side\_\allowbreak breach}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{side\_breach}$. 
\end{itemize}

\noindent FARM's generator takes the second reading, which the benchmark accepts for this task. 

\keyidea{The compiler builds the reading the generator takes, so the wording decides the machine.}
\noindent In the final run, the few clause sets that differed from the first accepted answer of their task were, all but one, judged afterwards to be fair readings of their wording (Section~\ref{sec:res-clauses}). They are cases, not a measure of how often the generator errs. 

The repository's guide to task wording (\texttt{docs/LTLF\_TASK\_LANGUAGE.md}) lists the readings found and how to write each one.

\section{Anomalies the model does not detect or cannot report}
\label{sec:lim-anomalies}
An anomaly is a fault in the task itself, which no choice of clauses can fix. The generator cannot report one. The prompt tells it to refuse a task that needs an undeclared fact, but its answer is a list of one to 25 clauses with no field for a refusal, so an answer that refuses is invalid and uses up an attempt, as a rejected candidate does (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). 

An anomaly is therefore caught only when a later check rejects the candidate, and when every attempt is rejected the task fails with no machine. 

Each of the three kinds of anomaly is caught only in part:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A task that cannot be done}: the critic rejects a clause whose event can never occur. It checks each event alone, so a task whose events cannot happen the way it asks still gets a machine.
    \item \textbf{A fact the environment does not declare}: a clause that names an undeclared fact is rejected. The generator can instead use the nearest declared facts and drop part of the task, and nothing in its answer says so.
    \item \textbf{Clauses that contradict each other}: the compiler rejects hard clauses that leave no way to reach a hard goal. A task without a hard goal is not checked, so its rules can contradict each other and it still builds. Soft clauses that conflict build too.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conditions the templates cannot state}
\label{sec:lim-conditions}
Some conditions cannot be written as clauses. A template argument is always one declared fact, never a negated condition or a combination of facts, and all the clauses of a task hold together: the generator cannot join them with ``or'' or make one optional. 

A condition such as \textit{serve no food while the siren rings} joins two facts, so it works only if the environment declares one event that carries both, \texttt{served\_while\_ringing}.

\section{Tasks the machines are not meant for}
\label{sec:lim-quantities}
Some tasks do not fit a Reward Machine at all:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{As much or as little as possible}: a clause is met or broken over a whole run, so it cannot ask for as many items as possible or for the shortest path. The templates that count stop at a fixed number and LTLf can not express the unbounded counting. A machine that paid every time something happens would need a loop that pays, which the loop check refuses (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). 
    
    A reward paid for each event (such as \texttt{kill\_zombie}) is simple to write in the environment instead if needed.
    \item \textbf{Behaviour no one can put into words}: something the user can not define with words, such a movement that looks natural, gives no clauses to write. It needs a reward learnt from human feedback \cite{christiano2017deep}.
    \item \textbf{Behaviour close to single moves}: a task such as \textit{Stay close to the obstacles on your way} can be stated only with a fact declared for it or with clauses over single moves. The machine then works at the level of moves, not at the level of the steps of the task, where it is meant to work and therefore out of scope.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Evaluation methodology}
\label{chap:evaluation}
The evaluation tests two claims: FARM builds a right Reward Machine from a task in plain language, and an agent trained on that machine learns the task. Two tests check them on one benchmark of test cases, the \textbf{cards} (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}). The \textbf{generation test} builds machines and checks them without training an agent (Section~\ref{sec:eval-machines}). The \textbf{training test} trains agents on the machines and judges what they do (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learning}). The \textbf{final run} is one pass of both tests, on the version of FARM frozen before it.

Every result belongs to one test and one set of cards. Numbers from different sets are never added or averaged. Counts are given as $x$ of $n$ ($p$\%), with a 95\% Wilson score interval where the record gives one.
% NOTE-OPEN: cite the Wilson score interval (Wilson 1927); no key in bib-keys.md.

Training results rest on two units, the row and the seed. A \textbf{row} is one card trained in one world under one setting (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}). A \textbf{seed} is one training run of a row, with its own random start. Every row but the two pilot rows, which repeat two base cards, has three seeds (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}). A training measure is the mean over the seeds of a row, given with a 95\% Student $t$ interval over those seeds. With so few seeds, that interval is wide.

\section{Empirical evaluation in a real environment}
\label{sec:eval-learning}
The training test checks the second claim, that an agent learns the task from its machine. It rests on four choices: which arms train, on which cards, under which setting, and when a card counts as learned.

\subsection{The three arms}
\label{sec:eval-arms}
The evaluation compares three ways of training, the \textbf{arms}. All three train the same learner and are scored on the same cards:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FARM}: trains on the reward of the Reward Machine that FARM builds.
    \item \textbf{ARM-FM}: trains on the reward of a machine built by the ARM-FM method.
    \item \textbf{Training with no Reward Machine} (``no RM'' in tables and figures): trains on the world's own reward.
\end{itemize}

The ARM-FM arm is a reconstruction, built without access to the official ARM-FM code. It follows the appendices of the paper and, for the MiniGrid tasks, the prompts and machines of the first author's public repository \cite{armfm_code}. Parts that neither source documents, such as how the later rounds of the generator and the critic are framed, are reconstructed. The rebuilt baseline may therefore differ from the original. The results compare FARM with this reconstruction.

FARM changes only how the machine is built. In FARM, the generator chooses clauses and the compiler builds the machine from them (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}). In ARM-FM, the generator writes the machine as text. In both, the critic accepts or rejects the machine. A second phase, again a generator followed by its critic, then writes the labelling function. Every machine state also gets a plain-language description. Both arms use the same models: \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} as generator and \texttt{glm-5.3-flash} as critic.

FARM and ARM-FM build a machine in rounds, in the same way. In a \textbf{round}, the generator proposes a candidate and the critic accepts or rejects it. A rejection, or a candidate that fails the arm's own checks, starts a new round. The phase that builds the machine, the \textbf{machine phase}, has up to 10 rounds. The phase that writes the labelling function has up to three. A machine is \textbf{built} when a candidate passes every check of its arm and then the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:eval-plan-check}). One that runs out of rounds or fails the plan check is not built.

\subsection{The benchmark cards}
\label{sec:eval-cards}
A machine can only be called right or wrong against stated answers, and an agent can only be judged against stated rules. The benchmark is a set of cards that state both. A card is one test case: a task written in plain language, together with the answers accepted for it.

Each answer is a list of clauses. One is the list the task is expected to give, the \textbf{expected clauses}, and the others are lists that are also right. Besides its task and its answers, a card holds four things:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{World}: the world the task runs in.
    \item \textbf{Rewordings}: the same task said in other words.
    \item \textbf{Goal flag}: whether the task has a goal.
    \item \textbf{Scripted plans}: one that solves the task, wrong ones that each break a named rule, and an idle one that only waits.
\end{itemize}
\noindent A \textbf{scripted plan} is a program of commands written in advance for the card's world, such as going to the key or picking it up. A command may find an object or call a fixed scripted policy of the world. The actions a plan takes therefore depend on the layout and on world events.

Cards come in groups, the \textbf{suites} of Table~\ref{tab:eval-suites}. Table~\ref{tab:eval-card} shows one card of the templates suite and the machine that FARM builds for it.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:eval-training}
Every row trains with PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) under the setting of its group of cards (Table~\ref{tab:eval-training}).
% NOTE-OPEN: cite PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) and MiniGrid; no keys in bib-keys.md.
In FARM and ARM-FM, the machine takes part in training in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Labels and reward}: the arm's labelling function reads the world at every step. The machine follows the propositions it returns and pays its reward.
    \item \textbf{End of the episode}: the episode also ends when the machine enters a final state or a rejecting state, such as FARM's well.
    \item \textbf{Input}: the agent sees the world's observation together with its current machine state. In most groups, the state is given as a text embedding of its description, as in ARM-FM (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}).
\end{itemize}
\noindent These two arms train on the machine's reward alone, except in the five repeated shop rows, which add the world's reward to the machine's.

The pilot rows play one judged episode, an evaluation episode after training (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}), because on their one fixed layout every judged episode would be the same.

\subsection{When a card counts as learned}
\label{sec:eval-learned}
The training results count, for each arm, the cards that its agents have \textbf{learned}. After training, each seed plays \textbf{judged episodes}, in which the agent takes its most likely action at every step.

A \textbf{judge} reads each judged episode through the benchmark's reference labelling, which is written with each world and kept separate from FARM and ARM-FM. It checks the card's expected clauses on the trace. The judge is the same for every arm and never uses an arm's labelling function or machine. A wrong machine therefore cannot turn a failed episode into a pass.

A judged episode \textbf{succeeds} when every hard clause of the card holds, the goal included when the card has one. It fails, whatever the rules say, when the arm's machine, not the world, ends it in a final state within $\max(1, \lceil 0.1L \rceil)$ actions. Here $L$ is the median length of the solving plan on the card's layouts, or 0 when there is none. The machine has then ended the episode before the agent could act on the task.

Soft rules never decide success. Every break of a soft rule is counted instead, each time it happens, in the \textbf{soft breaks per episode}.

The \textbf{judged success} of a seed is the share of its judged episodes that succeed. The seed \textbf{passes} at 0.8 or more.

\keyidea{A card counts as learned for an arm when at least two of its seeds pass: two of three, or both seeds of a pilot card, which trains only two.}

\noindent A card with no goal and only soft rules needs another test, since with no hard rule every judged episode succeeds. Its seeds are compared with an idle agent, which only waits, on the same layouts. A soft rule \textbf{left unmet} is one still waiting to be met when the episode ends. A seed passes when three things hold, per episode:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Its soft breaks and its soft rules left unmet are both no higher than the idle agent's;
    \item At least one of them is lower;
    \item The labels the judge reads change more often than the idle agent's, which shows that the agent acts.
\end{itemize}

A row whose machine was not built counts as not learned for that arm.

Besides the learned count, each row reports two means over its seeds, its judged success and its soft breaks per episode. A shop row adds a third, its orders served per episode. Learned counts are given over the 60 rows and over four kinds of subset, in this order:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item ARM-FM's own kind of task, the step-by-step cards, against the 49 rows of new kinds;
    \item The goal flag, outside the shop and the step-by-step cards;
    \item Three kinds of card by what the task asks: the step-by-step cards, cards with hard rules but no order of steps and no soft rule, and cards with at least one soft rule;
    \item Each suite.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Qualitative evaluation of the Reward Machines}
\label{sec:eval-machines}
The generation test builds and checks machines without training an agent. FARM and ARM-FM each build one machine for every card's own wording, which is its task text, and one for each of its rewordings. The exception is a card that reuses another card's machine.

\subsection{Built machines and rewordings}
\label{sec:eval-built}
The generation test counts the machines each arm builds, scores FARM's clauses and compares the machines of each card's wordings.

A machine of either arm goes through both phases of Section~\ref{sec:eval-arms}, one for the machine and one for its labelling function. A machine accepted at the first round of each phase therefore needs two rounds.

Every FARM machine also goes through a model review before it may train. A model, Claude Opus 5.5, reads one sheet per machine. Each sheet shows three things:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The machine's clauses beside the card's accepted answers;
    \item The results of the build checks and of the plan check;
    \item For every state, its role, its transitions, the clauses still owed there and its description.
\end{itemize}
\noindent The reviewer gives each machine one of three verdicts: ok, unsure or anomaly.

FARM's chosen clauses are also scored against the card's accepted answers. A FARM machine is \textbf{right} when its clauses match one of them, exactly or by an equivalent formula. ARM-FM stores no clauses, so its machines are not scored this way.

The \textbf{thinking-level study} repeats FARM's generation at each thinking level of its models. It runs 46 cases, picked around past mistakes of the generator, three times each. It counts the runs whose final clauses match an accepted answer of the case's card. On a case that asks for steps in order, the count is stricter: clauses that also make an earlier step a goal of its own pass the card but are not counted as right.

The rewordings test whether the same task, said in other words, gives the same machine. A card is \textbf{built on every wording} when its arm builds a machine for each wording, the plan check included. On the cards with at least two built wordings, the machines of each card are compared. The comparison has three outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The same machine in every built wording, with identical text;
    \item Machines of the same size that are not identical, with the same number of states and the same number of transitions;
    \item Different machines.
\end{itemize}

FARM's prompts were tuned on the same cards. The versions and their scores are in Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts}.

\subsection{From traces to rewards}
\label{sec:eval-plan-check}
The plan check runs the scripted plans of a card through a machine and reads what each plan pays. No language model takes part.

Each plan plays in the card's world. The arm's own labelling function reads every step, and the machine follows the labels. The run stops at the first final state or at the well. A machine passes when three kinds of plan behave as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The solving plan}: it does not enter the well. When the machine has final states, it ends in one.
    \item \textbf{A plan that breaks a hard rule}: it never reaches a final state.
    \item \textbf{A plan that breaks only soft rules}: it receives a negative reward before it stops, or it ends like the solving plan on its layout and is paid less in total. The total adds the rewards up without discount.
\end{itemize}
\noindent A machine with no final state must also meet three more conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An idle plan that keeps every rule does not enter the well, and both it and the solving plan end with nothing owed;
    \item A wrong or idle plan that breaks a hard rule for good ends in the well;
    \item Every wrong plan pays less than the solving plan, both with and without discount.
\end{itemize}

A machine that ends before the agent acts fails every plan. The check needs no clauses, so it applies to FARM and ARM-FM alike.

\subsection{Loops that pay}
\label{sec:eval-loops}
A loop pays when an agent can go round a cycle of transitions and collect a positive total. The agent then gains reward without doing the task. FARM's loop check runs on every build and refuses any machine with a loop whose pay adds up to more than zero (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). It finds such loops with a Bellman--Ford search over the undiscounted rewards.

To compare FARM and ARM-FM, a search of the same kind runs on every built machine of both. It looks only at the states reachable from the initial state without passing a final state. A loop it finds is searched again without the transitions that only a world event can take, such as a box that falls by itself.

A second measure looks at the trained agents. The \textbf{machine return} of an episode is the plain sum of the rewards that the arm's own machine pays over it. The machine return of each judged episode is compared with that of the \textbf{best scripted plan} of the card: the largest plain sum that a solving plan of the card earns on the same machine in the plan check. An episode that earned more would point to pay that the scripted plans do not reach.

\subsection{Machine size}
\label{sec:eval-size}
The size of a machine is its number of states and its number of transitions (Section~\ref{sec:output-format}). The sizes of FARM's and ARM-FM's machines are compared on the trained rows where both built a machine, counting each distinct pair of machines once. Each arm's sizes are summarised by their median.

\section{Comparison between ARM-FM and FARM}
\label{sec:res-comparison}

FARM and ARM-FM are compared on two things: the machines they build from the same cards, and what agents learn with those machines, beside training with no Reward Machine. Both are asked for the same 266 machines, 111 of them for a card's own wording and the rest for its rewordings. FARM builds its machines on the frozen pipeline with one setting: generator \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} and critic \texttt{glm-5.3-flash}, at thinking level max.

\subsection{Machines built with no hand edit}
\label{sec:res-built}

A machine can train only once it is built, so the first comparison counts the machines each arm builds and the rounds each build takes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-arms}).

No machine was edited by hand. FARM builds 265 of the 266 machines asked (99.6\%, 95\% Wilson interval 97.9\% to 99.9\%). Of these, 262 passed every model check at the first round. The other 3 needed a second round after one automatic rejection by a critic: 1 for the machine and 2 for its labelling function.

Every card's own wording is built. The one machine not built is a rewording of a shop card. It passes both phases and fails the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}).

The critic reads each machine together with the compiler clues (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). The clues raise the share of known defects it flags at its first call from 30\% to 56\% (Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts}).

The rebuilt ARM-FM builds 152 of the 266 (57\%, 95\% Wilson interval 51\% to 63\%; Figure~\ref{fig:res-built}). It builds 132 at the first try and 20 after further attempts. Of these 20, 19 come after a rejection by its critic and 1 after an invalid first output. The 114 machines not built pass both phases and fail the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:res-armfm-machines}).

A built FARM machine takes 2.01 rounds on average, and a built ARM-FM machine 2.16. The least possible is two (Section~\ref{sec:eval-built}).

\subsection{FARM's machines}
\label{sec:res-farm-machines}

A FARM machine is the product of the automata of the clauses the generator chooses. It keeps hard and soft rules apart. It pays whole units: \gain{$+1$} for each step of progress and \gain{$+10$} more for the finish. A hard break sends the run to the well. The edge into the well pays \loss{$-10$} when the task has no soft clause and more when it has one, about \loss{$-96$} for 300 steps (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}). It also takes back the progress already paid and ends the episode. Each soft break costs \loss{$-1$}, and the episode goes on.

The one machine FARM did not build is a rewording of a shop card. The shop world has no event for putting food down, so the pick-up that this wording asks for has no exact rule. The generator added a seventh clause for it. On a machine with no final state, the plan check needs every wrong plan to be paid less than the solving plan, both when the rewards are added up without discount and when they are discounted. With the added clause, one wrong plan fails this test, so the check refuses the machine (Section~\ref{sec:eval-plan-check}).

\subsubsection{Which clauses FARM chooses}
\label{sec:res-clauses}

The compiler builds exactly the clauses the generator chooses, so a FARM machine is as right as that choice. A candidate is the clause set the generator proposes for one wording. At the frozen setting, 9 of FARM's candidates match no answer their card listed before the final run. Eight are fair readings of the task. The ninth, the shop rewording above, was never built. The eight accepted ones differ from the listed answers in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Four add a soft Existence rule (``at some point'' in the task's words) beside a soft order;
    \item Two read ``not both'' as ``neither'' or as a ban;
    \item Two add a hard rule that the wording suggests.
\end{itemize}
\noindent These eight were accepted after the fact. The nine are therefore cases, not an accuracy rate (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).

A machine built from a later-listed answer, one that its card does not list first, can still teach the task. A study of clause choice checks FARM's final-run machines on 15 cards that accept more than one answer. Nine of these cards are among the 60 rows trained (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}). On these nine, the study finds three things:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Every trained machine matches a listed answer, and 6 of them match a later-listed one;
    \item FARM's agents learn 4 of these 6: UnlockPickup, its pilot copy, KeyCorridor and a soft not-both card (NotCoExistence) with no goal;
    \item FARM's agents do not learn the other two, both on the BlockedUnlockPickup level: the Blocked box card, a box task (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}), and a soft-order card (Precedence).
\end{itemize}
Whether a row is learned \textbf{follows the level}, not the order of the answers. The 4 rows not learned, two with each kind of answer, are all on the BlockedUnlockPickup level. The 5 rows on other levels are all learned.

The soft not-both card counts as learned under the rule for cards with only soft rules (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). Six of the 15 cards, four of them trained, received an answer only after their machines had been read. This part of the check is therefore not independent. The other 51 trained rows are outside the study.

Right runs of one wording almost always choose the same answer (Appendix~\ref{app:res-choice}).

\subsubsection{Loops that pay}
\label{sec:res-loops}

A loop that pays would let an agent collect reward by going round it without doing the task. The built machines of both arms are therefore searched for one (Section~\ref{sec:eval-loops}). FARM's loop check refuses any machine with such a loop, so no FARM machine has one (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}). ARM-FM has no such check. Run over its machines, the same check finds a loop that pays in 24 of the 152 built ARM-FM machines and in none of FARM's 265.

In each of the 24 machines, every loop that pays needs a world event, a box falling or a siren ringing. The world allows at most two of each per episode. With the world-event edges removed, none of the 24 machines has a loop that pays. In the final run no trained agent used one. Three of the 24 machines trained, all on box-carry cards. Their judged return, the machine return of Section~\ref{sec:eval-loops}, is 1.1 to 1.2 per episode, which is what one or two falls of the box pay.

In all trained runs of FARM and ARM-FM, no judged episode earned more on its machine than the best scripted plan of its card (run counts in Appendix~\ref{app:res-counts}). The scripted plan is not proven best, so the result is evidence, not a proof.

\section{The zombie lanes: reacting to events}
\label{sec:res-react}

A rule that answers an event holds while the event never happens, so an agent that does nothing passes it. The forcing worlds of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} set such rules off again and again. The zombie lanes are the main forcing world, and the box-carry worlds the second. Four things define the zombie lanes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Field}: 9 by 9 cells crossed by three lanes. The top lane is the one to protect, and plants grow off the lanes.
    \item \textbf{Zombies}: they enter at the right end of a random lane, with odds that rise from 4\% to 17\% per step, and walk one cell left every three steps.
    \item \textbf{Agent}: it starts on the middle lane and kills a zombie by moving onto it.
    \item \textbf{Episode}: it lasts 300 steps, and the world pays 1 for each zombie killed.
\end{itemize}

\section{The shop}
\label{sec:res-shop}

The shop merges many rules in one task. Clients come in and wait, and the agent has four jobs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serving}: it takes each order, fetches food from the pantry, cooks it at the stove and serves it;
    \item \textbf{Falls}: it picks up food that falls;

\subsection{A curriculum for the shop}
\label{sec:res-curriculum}

An agent starts with no knowledge of the world, so its first episodes are close to a random walk. It meets its first reward only by chance. The larger the world, the rarer that first reward, so agents learn worse as the world grows. A curriculum on the size of the world is one answer: the agent learns the task in a small shop, and the shop then grows. \tbd{shop size results: runs that learn at 3$\times$3, 5$\times$5 and 7$\times$7, and with the size curriculum}

The open question is whether a curriculum of shop tasks lets agents learn the shop cards on which no agent trained directly serves an order. \tbd{curriculum results for the shop}

\section{Thinking level of the generator}

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This thesis set out to build Reward Machines from tasks stated in plain language, with no machine edited by hand, and to find a curriculum for the shop. FARM addresses the first by changing one step of ARM-FM. A language model writes the task as clauses from a fixed catalogue of templates, each hard or soft. Fixed tools compile the clauses into the machine and price its transitions.

\keyidea{The machine follows the chosen clauses by construction, but whether the clauses say what the person meant rests on the generator and its critic.}

\noindent The first objective is \textbf{met}. No machine was edited by hand. FARM builds 265 of the 266 machines asked for (99.6\%, Section~\ref{sec:res-built}). On the 60 training cases, agents learn:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 43 of 60 (72\%, 95\% Wilson interval 59\% to 81\%) with FARM's machines;
    \item 29 of 60 (48\%, 36\% to 61\%) with the machines of the rebuilt ARM-FM;
    \item 23 of 60 (38\%, 27\% to 51\%) with no Reward Machine.
\end{itemize}
\noindent A case whose machine was not built counts as not learned for that arm (Section~\ref{sec:res-learning}). The claim is about cases learned, not about how fast they are learned.

% PICK H2H: from here to END PICK H2H, cut as one block.
FARM's lead over ARM-FM comes mostly from cases on which ARM-FM builds no machine. Of the 21 training cases that only FARM's agents learn, ARM-FM's machine was not built on 18. The 7 cases that only ARM-FM's agents learn all sit on the \mbox{BlockedUnlockPickup} level. Their tasks name one or two of the three steps the level needs before the box, never all three (Table~\ref{tab:res-h2h}). ARM-FM's machine pays for each of those steps, asked for or not, while FARM's keeps to the task and leaves little reward before the box. That difference in reward may explain these 7 cases, but no run tests it.
% END PICK H2H

Rewording a task seldom changes FARM's machine, and it changes ARM-FM's more often (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}). The rewordings were built and not trained, so this holds for the machines and says nothing about learning.

Two things can still go wrong. On one rewording of a shop task, the generator added a rule, and the plan check refused the machine (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}). Where the wording is ambiguous, the reading the generator takes decides the machine (Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}).

% PICK SHOP: framing to confirm with the author
In the shop, no trained agent serves an order, whether it trains on FARM's machine, on the world's reward or on both. ARM-FM, whose shop machines start in a final state, trains none of the shop tasks. An order nets nothing on FARM's shop machines in the frozen pipeline. But training on the world's reward, which pays each order served, serves none either. Whether an agent learns to serve depends on the world, the learner and how the machine pays each order. The results do not separate these causes (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}).

The second objective, a curriculum for the shop, is still \textbf{open}. No curriculum result is reported yet (Section~\ref{sec:res-curriculum}).

Machine size stays open as well. FARM's machines are larger than ARM-FM's and grow with the number of clauses, so the largest are hard to read. Making them smaller is work still to do (Chapter~\ref{chap:future-work}).

\chapter{Future work}
\label{chap:future-work}

Two studies of the Results chapter still wait for data: a curriculum for the shop (Section~\ref{sec:res-curriculum}) and the scoring of other language models on generation alone (Section~\ref{sec:res-models}). The five directions below go beyond this thesis. Each gives what is missing today, why it matters and a first step.

\section{Smaller machines}
\label{sec:fw-size}
A FARM machine is the product of one automaton per clause. It is minimised by merging the states that behave the same, and it still grows with the number of clauses (Sections~\ref{sec:step4} and~\ref{sec:lim-size}).

The boil task of the shop shows this growth. Each of its six hard rules is either waiting for its answer or not, so the machine holds one state for each combination of the six, plus the well. That gives 65 states and 10,662 transitions (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). The machine is already minimised, so none of its states can be merged without changing what it pays.

No way of making machines smaller is proposed here. The first step is to measure how the size grows with the number and the kind of rules, on tasks built for that purpose.

\section{A model that writes LTLf}
\label{sec:fw-ltlf}
The generator picks clauses from eight templates whose arguments are single declared facts. A task can therefore say no more than those templates state (Section~\ref{sec:lim-conditions}).

A generator that writes the LTLf formulas itself could state negated conditions, alternatives and nested conditions. In exchange, it would give up what the fixed catalogue gives: a known meaning, automaton and price for every clause. The rest of the pipeline should hold, but that needs testing. A first step is to ask such a model for formulas on tasks the catalogue cannot state, and to pass them through the same checks.

\section{A way to refuse}
\label{sec:fw-refusal}
The generator's answer is a list of clauses with no way to refuse. A task it should refuse therefore ends in failed attempts, or in clauses that come close and do not say what they leave out (Section~\ref{sec:lim-anomalies}).

A field for a refusal, with its reason, would let the model report a task the world cannot do, a fact the environment does not declare or a wording it cannot settle. A person could then fix the task or the environment description. The first step is to add the field and test it on tasks built to be refused.

\section{Counting}
\label{sec:fw-counting}
The templates count only to two: ExistenceTwo asks for an event at least twice (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). ``At least $n$ times'' needs an LTLf formula that grows with $n$. An unbounded count, such as one kill for each zombie that enters, cannot be stated in LTLf.

A count would let a machine pay for each kill up to a fixed number, with no loop that pays. Such a machine would come closer to the world's reward in the zombie lanes (Section~\ref{sec:res-react}). No run tests whether that helps the agents there. The first step is to find how a count would be written as a clause and priced.

\section{The parts inherited from ARM-FM}
\label{sec:fw-inherited}
FARM changes only how the Reward Machine is generated. The labelling function, the state descriptions and the training come from ARM-FM, and each can be improved.

The labelling function is one case. A model writes its code. Its critic reads that code without seeing the labels it gives in the world. The plan check catches some faults, such as the labelling function of one ARM-FM machine, which crashes (Table~\ref{tab:res-armfm-failures}). It catches them only on the plans it runs. A first step is a critic that also sees the labels on sample episodes.

\section{Reflection on the Sustainable Development Goals}
This thesis is a technical work in reinforcement learning. It proposes FARM, a method that builds Reward Machines from tasks written in plain language, and measures the machines and the agents that learn with them in simulated worlds. It does not address a social or environmental problem directly, so most goals do not apply. Its contribution is a research method, not a deployed system.

\subsection{SDG 9: Industry, innovation and infrastructure}
The work is research, so this is the goal it relates to most. Within it, the relation is highest with target 9.5, which asks to enhance scientific research and upgrade technological capabilities. The thesis contributes a method to one research problem, the writing of rewards for reinforcement learning. It also extends the open tools it builds on in public forks of IBM nl2ltl and FL-AT (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

The intended benefit is reward design that needs no expert in reinforcement learning. A developer, a person without technical knowledge or another agent could then state a task and get a machine that trains (Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}). The method also uses small and cheap language models on purpose. This keeps it within reach of groups without large computing budgets. These benefits are intended, not measured: no study with users was run.

\subsection{SDG 8: Decent work and economic growth}
Writing and tuning a reward function by hand takes time and needs expertise in both the task and reinforcement learning. Target 8.2 asks for higher productivity through technological upgrading and innovation. FARM builds the machine from the task with no edit by hand, so it could save part of that work. The relation is low because the time saved is not measured.

\subsection{SDG 12: Responsible consumption and production}
This goal relates to the computing that the method needs. Target 12.2 asks for the efficient use of natural resources. Of the five steps of FARM, only the generator and the critic call a language model, and both models are small. Steps 2 to 4, the compiler, call none: fixed tools build the machine from the clauses. The training runs of the evaluation still use computing power. The energy used by the work was not measured, so the relation is low.

\subsection{SDG 16: Peace, justice and strong institutions}
The goal includes accountable and transparent institutions. The work relates to it only indirectly, through the transparency of automated systems. In FARM each clause states its part of the task in one plain sentence. The machine follows the chosen clauses exactly, and a broken hard rule ends the episode in the well (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}). A person can therefore read what an agent is rewarded for, and check it against the task, before the agent trains. The model can still choose clauses that do not fit the task (Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}). This transparency therefore does not replace a review of the clauses.

\section{How the work started}
\label{app:dev-start}
The work goes through four periods, the last of which is the main development:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reward Machines in practice}: the work starts by studying what Reward Machines are, with examples built and tried by hand. A first implementation of Reward Machines with counterfactual reasoning (CRM) \cite{icarte2022reward} shows how they work and what can be done with them.
    \item \textbf{Aims}: the aim of bringing AI closer to what people want (Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal}) is set against what other work does with Reward Machines and what is feasible.
    \item \textbf{A first prototype}: with the goal framed and ARM-FM as the reference, a base prototype of the whole pipeline is built. It joins the tools of IBM and FL-AT, a small post-process and the critic.
    \item \textbf{Iteration}: from the prototype on, the work runs in cycles of investigating, changing and testing. AI agents take the largest part in this period.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The workflow with AI agents}
\label{app:ai-workflow}
In the iterative period the developer talks to one main agent, which orchestrates other agents behind it. A change goes through seven steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grounding}: the conversation first settles what is to be done. It covers the ideas behind the change, its aim, the tools and methods, what can and cannot be done, and where in the code it belongs. Mock-ups and first-hand tests come at this point.
    \item \textbf{Requirements that everyone understands}: planning sets down every requirement, in enough detail that the authors, student and supervisor, understand exactly what will happen.
    \item \textbf{Feasibility}: small ad hoc experiments show that the change is achievable, that the algorithms work and that the ideas hold. They are run by hand, or by an agent with temporary code.
    \item \textbf{Plan}: once everything is settled, an agent writes a plan and every reference is checked. The agent that codes then knows what to do, how to do it and what result it must reach.
    \item \textbf{Code, review and live tests}: every change is reviewed, tested live on real runs and compared with the references.
    \item \textbf{Reviewers of different providers}: several coding agents from different providers review the code independently, from different angles, and try to break it. They check that the code is robust and clean and gives the right results.
    \item \textbf{The human last}: a person checks the work last. The person checks the outputs rather than every line of the code, and asks the reviewers for clearer evidence where it is missing. Anything unclear is redone, checked again or compared with the standards and the goals.
\end{itemize}
The same workflow applies to this document: agents draft text and figures from the authors' notes and the measured results, and the authors review them.

\subsection{The Reward Machine viewer}
\label{app:viewer}
The tool that helps most is a viewer that makes Reward Machines easier to read (Figure~\ref{fig:dev-viewer}). It reads the text of a machine and draws it as an interactive graph. The graph appears in the web page, or in a stand-alone HTML or SVG file made by the \texttt{render-rm} command, and both use the same drawing code. The viewer compacts the machine in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One arrow per pair of states}: all transitions between the same two states share one arrow. Its label gives how many transitions it holds and their reward, or \textit{mixed} when the rewards differ. Its colour tells whether it costs, gains or does neither, as in the drawings of this thesis (Section~\ref{app:drawing}).
    \item \textbf{Propositions on demand}: the guards stay off the graph. Clicking an arrow opens a panel that lists each transition it holds, with its reward and its guard, the guard split into one chip per proposition.
    \item \textbf{A fixed layout}: the graph runs from the start at the top to the final state at the bottom. The well gets a lane of its own on the left, and states move off the arrows they are not part of.
\end{itemize}
Hovering an arrow highlights it, and hovering a state highlights the arrows that leave it. Unlike the drawings of this thesis, the viewer also draws the implicit stay of each state, the step that matches none of its transitions, as a loop labelled \texttt{else} with reward 0.

\chapter{Implementation details}
\label{app:implementation}

Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} describes the method, and this appendix holds the implementation behind it. It gives the contributions of FARM, how it uses each tool, the format of its machines, the models and settings of the runs, and the training values of the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:eval-training}).

\section{Contributions}
\label{app:contributions}
FARM makes fifteen additions to the tools it builds on, IBM nl2ltl, FL-AT and MONA, and to the generation step of ARM-FM:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prompt}: it offers all eight Declare templates, with the meaning, kind and formula of each. It serves any environment, because the propositions and their meaning come from the environment description (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item \textbf{Restricted answer}: each clause is a template, its propositions, a priority and one plain sentence. Only the first three reach the compiler, so tasks of one schema should get machines of one shape (Sections~\ref{sec:step1} and~\ref{sec:task-schemas}).
    \item \textbf{Priority}: each clause is hard or soft, as the preference cues of the task set it. IBM's templates have no such priority (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item \textbf{FL-AT fork}: it drops MONA's first state only when it is redundant, and it returns the state of every clause in each product state (Sections~\ref{sec:step2} and~\ref{sec:step3}).
    \item \textbf{Final states and well}: the hard clauses set the final states and one well, in place of the accepting states and the rewards of FL-AT (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Rewards}: they count the stages still owed. A step closer pays, a new thing to do costs, and reaching the final state pays \gain{$+10$} (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Re-arming and folding}: soft rules are re-armed, so that every new break is charged, and soft Absence clauses are folded and add no state to the machine (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
    \item \textbf{Hard-break price}: a hard break costs more than breaking soft rules at every step up to the step limit. This price applies when the task has a soft clause (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).
    \item \textbf{Take-back}: the edge into the well takes back the progress already paid (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).
    \item \textbf{Merge and minimisation}: MONA's start state is merged with its idle successor, and the priced machine is minimised (Sections~\ref{sec:start-merge} and~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Loop check}: it refuses any machine with a loop whose rewards add up to more than zero (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Pay order}: a pay order for the runs of a machine, from finished to hard break, is reported when FARM prepares a machine for training (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Critic}: it reviews the clauses, not the machine text, and never edits the machine. Its model must differ from the generator's (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
    \item \textbf{Compiler clues}: the critic gets each rule in plain words, the hard clauses that the others imply, and the events that every way to finish includes (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
    \item \textbf{Machine format}: ARM-FM's format is extended with explicit final and rejecting states and one event expression per transition (Section~\ref{sec:output-format}).
\end{itemize}

\section{IBM nl2ltl}
In Step~1 the LTLf formula of each clause comes from the templates of IBM's nl2ltl. FARM uses them through a fork (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/nl2ltl}) of \url{https://github.com/IBM/nl2ltl} \cite{nl2ltl_code}. It reuses only the template classes and the LTLf builders. The GPT engine of nl2ltl is bypassed. FARM supplies its own engine. That engine asks a language model for a list of clauses under a JSON schema. It then passes each clause to the Declare template classes of nl2ltl, which build the LTLf formula.

FARM talks to three model providers, OpenAI, OpenCode and Antigravity, through one engine each. Each engine returns the clauses under the same JSON schema. Generator and critic are configured separately.

The template classes of nl2ltl cover all eight Declare templates of Table~\ref{tab:templates}. The GPT prompt of nl2ltl, by contrast, lets the model choose only five: Existence, ExistenceTwo, Response, \mbox{ChainResponse} and RespondedExistence.

\section{FL-AT}
Steps 2 and 3 run on FL-AT. FARM uses it through a fork (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/Flat}) of \url{https://github.com/Jamidd/Flat} \cite{flat_code}, which makes four main changes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item MONA runs in a subprocess with temporary files, where the original wrote a file in the working folder through \texttt{os.system}. Its output is parsed strictly. An automaton that repeats a guard out of one state stops the build with an error.
    \item Proposition names are renamed with regular expressions, where the original split strings and evaluated labels.
    \item MONA's first state is removed when it is redundant (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}).
    \item The state tuples of the product are exported, so that each product state tells which state every clause is in.
\end{itemize}

\section{The generator prompt}
The prompt of the generator has three parts: a system message, a user message and a catalogue of the Declare templates. The catalogue gives, for each template, its arguments, its kind (goal or rule), its meaning in English and its LTLf formula in IBM's syntax. It states that only Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals and that every rule holds when nothing happens. A second table maps thirteen typical wordings to templates.

The instructions for the priority list the preference cues that make a clause soft: ``if possible'', ``preferably'', ``ideally'', ``try to'', ``when you can'', ``it would be nice'' and, for a ban, ``try not to''. Every other clause is hard. A cue covers the step it qualifies, not the order word that leads into that step.

A step the task asks for is \textbf{lone} when no hard order puts it before a later step that must happen, that is, when no hard Precedence chain links it to a later step that has a hard Existence. Every lone step gets its own hard Existence, and no other step does. Three kinds of step are lone:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A step asked for on its own;
    \item The last hard step of an order;
    \item A step whose later step is only preferred, since a soft Existence forces nothing.
\end{itemize}

An order is written as a hard Existence of its last step and one Precedence for each pair of consecutive steps. The \runex{sequence} example is built this way: Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}), Precedence(\texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open}) and Precedence(\texttt{door\_open}, \texttt{at\_goal}), with no \mbox{Precedence}(\texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{at\_goal}) (Table~\ref{tab:running}).

\section{The post-process}
Three parts of the post-process of Step~4 need more detail than Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} gives: the count of stages, the optional end charges and the minimisation of the machine. One more detail concerns the pay-order report of Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}: it needs the step limit. Without one, the report records the pay order as not checked.

\subsection{Stages}
The stages that a state $u$ owes, $\Phi(u)$ (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}), are counted in one of two ways, depending on whether the machine has a final state.

With a final state, the hard part of $\Phi(u)$ is the fewest events that take $u$ to a final state. Each step on the way counts the propositions that its guard needs true, and at least one.

Each soft clause, a soft goal included, adds its own local count. That count is the longest path from the clause's state to an accepting state in its own automaton, with every cycle collapsed into one node. Its length is then multiplied by the chain weight of the clause. The chain weight is one plus the weights of the clauses that the event closing the clause makes pending, so a rule that hands off to another is worth the chain behind it. A violated state counts zero.

Without a final state, every clause, hard or soft, uses this local count.

\subsection{End charges}
Two options of Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, both off in the frozen pipeline, read the count of stages: the end charges and the promise cost. The end charges of Section~\ref{sec:open-promises} are for the stages still open when an episode ends. They sum the local, chain-weighted count of every clause, hard clauses included. They read that count on the state that the edge into the well leaves or, when the environment ends the episode, on the state reached. The promise cost is for each step out of a state that still owes a hard stage, and it reads the hard part of the same count.

\subsection{Minimisation}
Minimisation makes the machine smaller. After pricing, states that pay the same, lead to the same states on every input and end an episode alike are merged into one. Minimisation is a setting, on by default.

\section{Retries and the critic}
A rejection by the critic starts a new attempt (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). Failures of providers and tools, and inputs too large for the critic, follow rules of their own:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generator provider}: a passing failure of the generator's provider, such as a timeout or a rate limit, uses no attempt. The same attempt is rerun, up to ten times in a row.
    \item \textbf{Invalid generator answer}: it uses an attempt, like a rejection.
    \item \textbf{Critic}: a failed critic request, or a critic answer that cannot be read, is repeated on the same candidate, up to ten times.
    \item \textbf{Limits and permanent errors}: when either limit of ten runs out, or when a provider gives a permanent error, the run ends.
    \item \textbf{MONA or FL-AT}: a failure of MONA, or of FL-AT's translation of the formula into MONA's input, also ends the run instead of using an attempt.
    \item \textbf{Large inputs}: a machine text longer than 200{,}000 characters reaches the critic as a summary. A task with more than twelve propositions gets no compiler clues.
\end{itemize}

\section{State descriptions}
A LARM, the output of ARM-FM, is a Reward Machine with the code of its labelling functions and a description of each state (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}). FARM builds these two parts the way ARM-FM does. After the machine is built, a \textbf{tagger} writes one description per state. It sends the states in batches of twenty, with up to three requests per batch. It also sees the information that the critic received.

\section{Machine format}
The format of FARM differs from that of ARM-FM (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}) in five points:
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are no \texttt{else} self-loops. A state with no matching transition stays where it is and pays \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD}, which is 0.
    \item The states with a special role are listed explicitly. \texttt{FINAL\_\allowbreak STATES} is always written and may be empty. \texttt{REJECTING\_\allowbreak STATES} is left out when the machine has no well.
    \item One event expression combines propositions with \texttt{!} and \texttt{\&}, such as \texttt{has\_key\&\allowbreak !door\_open}, instead of a separate predicate such as \texttt{not\_has\_key}.
    \item \texttt{REWARD\_FUNCTION} lists only the transitions with a reward other than 0.
    \item Four fields appear only when the optional charge at the end of an episode is on (Section~\ref{sec:open-promises}), and it is off by default. They are \texttt{ACCEPTING\_\allowbreak STATES}, \texttt{HARD\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD}, \texttt{SOFT\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD} and \texttt{PENDING\_\allowbreak PROMISES}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Drawing the machines}
\label{app:drawing}
The drawings of Reward Machines in this thesis colour each arrow by the sign of what it pays. In the simplified drawings, all transitions between two states share one arrow, which is drawn in one of three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{as a cost} when its rewards are negative or zero;
    \item \textbf{as a gain} when they are positive or zero;
    \item \textbf{neutral} only when it holds both signs or only zeros.
\end{itemize}
The label \textit{mixed} means that the rewards differ, not that the colour is neutral. The drawings follow the Reward Machine viewer of Section~\ref{app:viewer}, without its \texttt{else} loops.

\section{Prompt versions before and after}
\label{app:res-prompts}

Two changes made during development are measured here: the versions of the generator prompt, and the compiler clues added to the critic.

The generator prompt went through twelve versions, v0 to v11, measured over 2,430 runs on the card benchmark. From the first version to the last, the share of ordered tasks written in the chain form rises from 52\% (22 of 42, 95\% Wilson interval 38\% to 67\%) to 81\% (68 of 84, 71\% to 88\%). That is short of the 90\% set as its target. Wrong machines fall from 14\% (18 of 126 runs, 9\% to 22\%) to 12\% (31 of 252, 9\% to 17\%).

Adding the compiler clues to the critic raises the share of labelled defects that the critic flags at its first call from 30\% (74 of 246, 95\% Wilson interval 25\% to 36\%) to 56\% (23 of 41, 41\% to 70\%). The measure covers 38 items: 28 wrong clause sets holding 41 defects, and 10 right ones. Through the full loop of generator and critic on 46 cases, right machines rise from 77\% (213 of 276, 72\% to 82\%) to 85\% (39 of 46, 72\% to 92\%).

% PICK NOISE
By a rule of thumb, a move of less than about 10 points in the chain-form share counts here as noise. One prompt version scored 75.0\% and, run again an hour later, 70.2\% (63 and 59 of 84). \chapter{IBM template examples}

This appendix shows what the compiler builds from each template alone, once hard and once soft, sixteen machines in all. Each machine is the Reward Machine of a task with that one clause. The appendix goes with Table~\ref{tab:templates} of Step~1, which gives the eight Declare templates with their LTLf formulas and how to read them.

Each row shows one template, hard on the left and soft on the right, and its caption gives the plain sentence of each version. As in Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}, a double circle marks the final state, the well is grey and implicit stays are not drawn. These machines are small, so each arrow also carries its guard next to its reward. The colour of an arrow follows the sign of its reward.

Three rules of Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} account for the shapes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Only a hard goal gives a final state.} Existence and ExistenceTwo are the goals. Each gives a machine with a final state when it is hard, and neither does when it is soft. A hard rule alone gives a rule-only machine, with no final state (Section~\ref{sec:rule-only}).
    \item \textbf{A broken hard rule leads to the well.} Absence, Precedence, ChainResponse and NotCoExistence have a violated state, so their hard machines have a well. The hard Absence machine is drawn on the left of panel~(c) of Figure~\ref{fig:templates-alone-1}. \mbox{RespondedExistence} and Response have no violated state, so their hard machines have no well. In them the trigger costs at once, and the answer pays it back.
    \item \textbf{A soft clause never ends the episode.} No soft machine has a well. A soft rule that can break is re-armed, so every new break costs again. A soft Absence is folded: it adds no state, and its break is a loop that costs \loss{$-1$} (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What each step pays.} A step pays the change in stages, and the step that reaches the final state adds the completion reward:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dropping the key}, from u0 to u1, adds a stage and costs \loss{$-1$}.
    \item \textbf{Picking it up again}, from u1 back to u0, closes that stage and pays \gain{$+1$}.
    \item \textbf{Reaching the goal} pays \gain{$+10$} plus the progress of the step: \gain{$+11$} from u0, and \gain{$+12$} from u1 when the key comes back in the same step.
    \item \textbf{Reaching the goal in the step that drops the key}, from u0 to u3, closes the goal but owes the key, so it pays 0. Picking up the key in u3 then pays \gain{$+11$}.
    \item \textbf{A step without the key after a drop} breaks the hard rule. Every edge into the well u4 pays \loss{$-10$} and takes nothing back, because neither u1 nor u3 is ahead of the start (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}, \emph{Taking progress back}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Careful key}
\label{app:ex3}
The \runex{careful key} joins the goal with a preference, which costs reward when it is broken but never ends the episode.

\textbf{Task.} \textit{Reach the goal, ideally without ever losing the key.}

\textbf{Clauses.} Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}), hard, $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{at\_goal}$; Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}), soft, $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{key\_lost}$.

\textbf{Machine.} Two states and three transitions (Listing~\ref{lst:ex3-rm}). Figure~\ref{fig:ex3-rm} draws it, and Section~\ref{sec:run-step4} explains what each transition pays. The machine has no well.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=rm,caption={The Reward Machine of the \runex{careful-key} example.},label={lst:ex3-rm}]
REWARD_MACHINE:
STATES: u0, u1
INITIAL_STATE: u0
FINAL_STATES: u1
DEFAULT_REWARD: 0
TRANSITION_FUNCTION:

(u0, at_goal&!key_lost) -> u1
(u0, at_goal&key_lost) -> u1
