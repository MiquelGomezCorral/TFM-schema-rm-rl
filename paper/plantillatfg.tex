\begin{abstract}[english]
The automated construction of Reward Machines (RMs) in Reinforcement Learning has recently been explored using large foundation models, which generate RMs directly from natural language task descriptions. While promising, these methods lack formal guarantees and rely on qualitative, human-evaluated assessments of RM correctness. In this work, we propose FARM: A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning. A pipeline based on Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) for RM generation: natural language tasks are first translated into LTL formulas, which are then compiled into Reward Machines using automata-theoretic tools. This approach produces RMs that, by construction, are aligned with the desired temporal specification. We introduce a set of structural and behavioral metrics to evaluate RM quality, and assess reinforcement learning performance in terms of success rate and sample efficiency in task resolution. On a benchmark of temporally structured tasks, our LTL-based method produces RMs more faithful to the task specification than RMs generated by a state-of-the-art foundation model approach. \evidencegap{pending}{The learning runs of Chapter~\ref{chap:results}: success rate and sample efficiency against the rebuilt ARM-FM.}{Agents trained with these RMs learn faster and more reliably. These results suggest that logic-guided formal RM synthesis offers a robust and interpretable alternative to purely neural reward generation methods.}{Agents trained with these RMs, however, do not learn faster. These results suggest that logic-guided formal RM synthesis offers an interpretable and verifiable alternative to purely neural reward generation methods; its gain lies in the machines, not in learning speed.}
\end{abstract}

\chapter{State of the art}

\section{Related work}
\subsection{Reward Machines}
\label{sec:sota-rm}
In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent learns to act in a Markov decision process (MDP) by maximising the reward it receives \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Most methods treat the reward function as a black box: the agent observes the number returned after each step and nothing about how it is computed \cite{icarte2022reward}. In practice a person writes that function by hand, so its structure is known and can be exploited to learn faster.

Sparse rewards pose a second problem. When the reward arrives only on completing a long task, the agent receives almost no signal while it learns. Reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it. Ng et al.~\cite{ng1999policy} prove that shaping with the difference of a potential function over states, $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$, leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that no other form of shaping gives this guarantee in general.

Reward Machines (RMs) address both problems by exposing the structure of the reward to the learner. Toro Icarte et al.~\cite{toroicarte2018using} introduced them at ICML 2018 and gave a fuller account in the Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research (JAIR) \cite{icarte2022reward}. This thesis follows the JAIR article for the formalisation, counterfactual learning and reward shaping.

An RM is a finite-state machine, written $\langle U, u_0, F, \delta_u, \delta_r\rangle$ in the JAIR formulation. $U$ is a finite set of states, $u_0 \in U$ the initial state and $F$ the set of terminal states. The transition function $\delta_u$ takes the current state and the set of propositions that hold and returns the next state. The reward function $\delta_r$ assigns to each state a reward function over environment transitions. The ICML formulation \textbf{attaches the reward to the pair of states of a transition instead}, which is the convention of the RM text format used in this thesis.

Propositions come from outside the machine. A labelling function $L$ maps each environment transition to the set of propositions that hold in it, for example ``the agent holds the key''. At every step the machine reads that set and moves. The agent learns in the product of the MDP and the RM, whose states pair an environment state with an RM state; in the product the reward is Markovian even when the task is not.

Exposing the structure enables dedicated learning methods. Q-learning for RMs (QRM) \cite{toroicarte2018using} decomposes the machine and learns, off-policy, one sub-policy per RM state in parallel. The JAIR article adds counterfactual reasoning for RMs (CRM), which reuses each experience to update every RM state; a hierarchical method (HRM); and automated reward shaping. The shaping potential is the negative optimal value of the RM state, $\Phi(s,u) = -v^{*}(u)$, computed by value iteration on the machine (Section~4.4 of the article, restricted to simple RMs).

RMs have two costs. Someone has to design the machine, and the authors note that RMs do not remove the difficulty of designing reward functions, although they may make temporally extended behaviours easier to specify \cite{icarte2022reward}. The labelling function is also assumed to exist and to be correct: learning RMs from experience, one of the open problems listed in the article, needs a correct labelling function. Both costs motivate generating RMs automatically.

\subsection{ARM-FM}
\label{sec:sota-armfm}
ARM-FM (Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models), by Creus Castanyer et al.~\cite{creus2026armfm} and published at ICLR 2026, replaces the hand-built RM with one generated by a foundation model (FM) from a natural-language task description. The output is a Language-Aligned Reward Machine (LARM), made of three parts: an RM, the Python code of its labelling functions, and a natural-language description of each RM state. A text embedding of the description of the current state is given to the policy together with the environment observation, so that the policy knows which sub-goal is active. In this thesis \textbf{ARM-FM is the method and LARM its output}.

The RM is produced by a generator model and reviewed by a critic model over several rounds of self-improvement. A human may optionally approve the result or give corrective feedback. The generator prompt asks for the machine as plain text in a fixed format. The critic prompt asks, among other checks, that regressions are penalised and that no unintentional loops exist, and the critic answers either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED} \cite[pp.~26--27]{creus2026armfm}. The paper uses GPT-4o to generate all LARM components, except for the 1\,000 XLand-MiniGrid LARMs, which come from several open-source models.

The evaluation covers four environments: MiniGrid (DoorKey and the harder UnlockToUnlock, BlockedUnlockPickup and KeyCorridor), Craftium (a Minecraft-based environment where the agent mines a diamond after gathering wood, stone and iron), Meta-World manipulation tasks, and XLand-MiniGrid. On DoorKey the agent trained with the LARM outperforms DQN with ICM, ReAct and plain DQN; on the three harder MiniGrid tasks it is the only agent that solves all of them; on Craftium it learns where an agent without the LARM makes no progress. On XLand-MiniGrid a single policy trains on ten tasks at once and solves unseen combinations of known tasks without fine-tuning.

\subsection{Linear Temporal Logic}
\label{sec:sota-ltl}
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) is a logic for stating properties of sequences of states, called traces. Pnueli \cite{pnueli1977temporal} proposed it to reason about programs. It is a language rather than a catalogue of patterns: a formula is built from propositions, Boolean connectives and four temporal operators.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{X}\,\varphi$ (next): $\varphi$ holds at the next step.
    \item $\mathrm{F}\,\varphi$ (eventually): $\varphi$ holds at the current step or at a later one.
    \item $\mathrm{G}\,\varphi$ (always): $\varphi$ holds at every step from the current one on.
    \item $\varphi\ \mathrm{U}\ \psi$ (until): $\psi$ holds at some step from the current one on, and $\varphi$ holds at every step before it.
\end{itemize}
LTL is defined over infinite traces, whereas a reinforcement learning episode ends. LTLf, LTL over finite traces \cite{degiacomo2013linear}, keeps the syntax and reads a formula over a trace of finite length, where next requires a following step to exist. LTLf is as expressive as first-order logic over finite traces and corresponds to the star-free regular expressions.

In the DoorKey task of Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}, the propositions are \texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open} and \texttt{at\_goal}. ``The agent reaches the goal at some point'' is
\[ \mathrm{F}\ \texttt{at\_goal}, \]
and ``the door is not open before the key is held'' is
\[ (\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{has\_key}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}. \]

For every LTLf formula there is a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) that accepts exactly the traces satisfying it \cite{degiacomo2013linear}. In practice the formula is rewritten in monadic second-order logic over finite words, from which MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} builds the minimal DFA. This is the bridge used by Step~2 of FARM (Section~\ref{sec:step2}).

Camacho et al.~\cite{camacho2019ltl} bring this logic to RL. They show that RMs can express reward specifications written in LTL, LTLf and other formal languages, translate a specification into an RM and combine several specifications with a product construction.

Declare \cite{pesic2006declarative} defines a catalogue of templates, such as Existence, Response or Precedence, each with an LTL semantics, to specify business processes declaratively. nl2ltl \cite{fuggitti2023nl2ltl} translates natural language into LTL by letting a language model choose a Declare template and its arguments.

\subsection{Hard and soft constraints in planning}
\label{sec:sota-preferences}
In automated planning, a plan is a sequence of actions that takes a system from an initial state to a goal, and PDDL is the standard language to describe such problems. PDDL3 \cite{gerevini2006preferences} adds two things to it. State trajectory constraints are conditions on the whole sequence of states that the plan goes through, not only on the last one, written with operators such as \texttt{always}, \texttt{sometime}, \texttt{at-most-once} and \texttt{sometime-before}, close to those of LTL. Preferences are soft constraints: a valid plan must satisfy every hard constraint, while each preference it breaks only adds a cost to the plan. Goals and trajectory constraints can both be stated as preferences, so a condition over time can be either required or only preferred.

\subsection{Conclusions}
Each line of work covers part of the problem. Reward Machines expose the structure of a reward but are built by hand. ARM-FM generates them from language, without formal guarantees on the result. LTLf gives task statements a precise meaning and a route to automata, and planning separates the rules that must hold from the preferences that only cost. Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal} combines these pieces.

\section{Critique of the state of the art}
\label{sec:critique}
\subsection{Narrow task variety}
The tasks whose Reward Machines ARM-FM prints are all simple sequences of steps: first one thing, then the next, then the goal. DoorKey goes \texttt{key}, \texttt{door}, \texttt{goal}; UnlockToUnlock repeats \texttt{key} and \texttt{door} before the \texttt{goal}; Craftium gathers \texttt{wood}, \texttt{stone}, \texttt{iron} and \texttt{diamond} in order; Meta-World grasps, moves and succeeds \cite[pp.~30--32]{creus2026armfm}. Each machine is a chain of states, one per step. No task states a rule that must hold throughout the episode, such as keeping the key until the door is open, and none separates what must happen from what is only preferable. The closest the machines come to a rule is a penalty for losing a key, for example $-0.1$ in UnlockToUnlock.

\subsection{Inconsistent and unstructured generation}
Language models do not return the same output on every run, even with the temperature set to zero. Ouyang et al.~\cite{ouyang2025empirical} measured this for ChatGPT on 829 code-generation problems, and the same task can therefore yield different machines. ARM-FM places no schema on what the model writes: the machine is plain text in a format described by the prompt, and the rewards are free numbers.

Free rewards allow loops that pay without progress. Proposition~1 of ARM-FM guarantees that optimising the LARM also solves the sparse task, provided the machine has no positive reward cycle \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. The paper describes no automatic check of this condition; the critic prompt only asks for penalised regressions and no unintentional loops \cite[p.~27]{creus2026armfm}. The Meta-World machine printed in the paper breaks the condition: the cycle $u_0 \to u_1 \to u_2 \to u_0$ pays $+0.20$, $+0.40$ and $-0.40$, a total of $+0.20$ per lap \cite[p.~32]{creus2026armfm}, although the paper states that its LARMs satisfy the property. The DoorKey loop that pays $+0.2$ for picking the key up and $-0.2$ for dropping it sums to zero, which satisfies the condition only because two chosen values cancel.

A critic model can hallucinate as well \cite{huang2025survey}, so a critic, even with a human at its side, is not a guarantee.

\subsection{Verification by a human or another model}
ARM-FM states the limitation itself: human verification during RM generation is a trade-off that presupposes access to human verifiers, and reducing it by exploiting the automaton structure, for example through formal verification, is left as future work \cite[pp.~9--10]{creus2026armfm}. Humans took part in four of the seven settings of its Table~3. In DoorKey a human check confirmed the machine after three iterations; in UnlockPickup a human added the transition for a dropped key; in KeyCorridor a human suggested intermediate rewards; and in Meta-World a human rescaled the rewards. BlockedUnlockPickup and Craftium needed no intervention, and the 1\,000 XLand-MiniGrid machines were scored by a second language model instead.

\subsection{Reproducibility.}
The ARM-FM paper releases no code. Its appendices print the generator and critic prompts, six Reward Machines, the labelling functions of four MiniGrid tasks and the hyperparameters. A public repository of the first author \cite{armfm_code}, which the paper does not reference, adds prompts, generated machines and labelling code for four MiniGrid tasks only. This material allows the method to be rebuilt but not its exact outputs, so the ARM-FM baseline of this thesis is a reconstruction whose results may differ from the original ones. The authors did not answer the contact attempts made for further material.

\chapter{Proposal}
\label{chap:proposal}
\section{Human preferences}
An RL agent pursues whatever its reward pays for, so the reward is the way a person tells the agent what to achieve \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. AI alignment aims to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values \cite{ji2025alignment}. For an RL agent, one part of this aim is a reward that says everything the person means: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.

A task is rarely a single chain of steps. FARM lets the user state four things in natural language: what to do, in which order, what to do in response to an event, and what must never happen. The temporal relations of order, response and prohibition are the vocabulary of the LTLf templates of Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}.

Each statement is either hard or soft. A hard statement must hold for the task to count as done. A soft statement is a preference: the agent may give it up, at a cost. This split between constraints and preferences comes from planning (Section~\ref{sec:sota-preferences}) and is carried here into Reward Machines.

\section{No human expert}
ARM-FM relies on a human or on a second model to verify its machines (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). FARM aims at generation without a human expert. The language model only chooses clauses from a fixed catalogue, and a compiler builds the machine from them, so the properties of the machine follow from the construction and do not depend on a reviewer. A critic model still reads the result, but it can only flag clauses; the generator retunes them, and the critic never edits the machine.

\section{Formal and robust generation: Schemas}
\label{sec:schemas}
FARM limits the role of the language model. Its answer is bounded: instead of writing a Reward Machine, the model only picks patterns from a fixed catalogue, the Declare templates of Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}, and fills them with the propositions of the environment. Any other answer is rejected. Everything after that choice is formal and deterministic. The machine is built from the chosen patterns alone, without the meaning of the propositions, so tasks of the same shape, such as any ``first $a$, then $b$, then $c$'', receive the same machine with only the propositions exchanged. These task schemas (Section~\ref{sec:task-schemas}) make the result predictable and confine the variability of the language model to that one choice. The rewards are designed so that the agent cannot earn reward by going round in circles, and every machine is checked for such loops.

\subsection{Two kinds of templates: goals and rules}
A clause is a goal if a step in which no proposition holds leaves it unsatisfied, and a rule if that step leaves it satisfied. Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals: they ask for something to happen. The other six templates are rules, Precedence included: they hold while nothing happens, and a later event may break them. The compiler applies the test to the DFA of each clause, so no list of templates is needed.

\subsection{Adaptation to Reward Machine generation (prompt)}
The default prompt of IBM's engine is a few-shot list that allows five of the eight templates, with examples about business applications such as Slack or Salesforce, no definitions and no description of the environment. The prompt of FARM covers all eight templates. It gives the model the catalogue with a definition of each template, a table from typical phrases to templates, worked examples, the rules for choosing hard or soft, the environment description in Markdown, the task and, on a retry, the comments of the critic. The propositions the model may use come from the environment description, so the same prompt serves any environment.

\subsection{Hard and soft clauses}
Each clause carries a priority that the model chooses for that clause alone. The model marks a clause soft only when the task uses explicit preference language such as ``if possible'' or ``ideally''; every other clause is hard. The priority never changes the formula. A hard clause must hold for the task to be completed. A soft clause is a preference: breaking it costs reward but does not prevent finishing (Step~4). IBM's templates have no such notion, since ``never touch the lava'' and ``if possible, never touch the lava'' are the same Absence clause, so the priority has to come from the prompt.

\subsection{Task schemas}
\label{sec:task-schemas}
Steps 2 to 4 use only the template and the priority of each clause and the place each proposition fills, never what a proposition means. Tasks with the same clauses up to the propositions therefore receive the same machine with the names exchanged, and the clauses with the propositions left open form a task schema. Each running example stands for its schema: a task such as ``gather wood, then stone, then iron'' would have the clauses of example 1 with \texttt{has\_wood}, \texttt{has\_stone} and \texttt{has\_iron}, and the same machine. The priority belongs to the schema, so example 3 with a hard Absence would give a different machine, with a rejecting well. Two tasks of the same shape share a machine only when the generator gives them the same clauses.

\subsection{FL-AT and MONA}
FL-AT, the Formal Language--Automaton Transmogrifier \cite{transmogrifier2020}, writes the LTLf formula as a formula of monadic second-order logic over finite words, the input language of MONA, and reads back the automaton that MONA prints. MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} is a decision procedure for that logic: to decide a formula it builds the minimal DFA of its models. FARM uses a fork of FL-AT (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

\subsection{The product of the clause DFAs}
The product has no more states than the product of the sizes of the clause DFAs \cite{camacho2019ltl}. Its transitions carry Boolean conditions over the propositions, one per combination of clause transitions that can fire together.

\subsection{What was wrong with FL-AT's reward}
FL-AT also assigns rewards. Each product edge pays the reward of every clause whose DFA is accepting after the step, so a finished clause is paid again at every later step. Rules that merely hold pay for doing nothing, and Response and ChainResponse contain a rewarded cycle that can be farmed. FARM discards these rewards (Step~4).

\subsection{Final states}
FL-AT derives its accepting states from its own rewards: they are the destinations of the edges that pay the highest reward. FARM discards this marking and recomputes the final states from the hard clauses (Step~4).

\section{Step 4 · FARM's post-process: hard and soft clauses, and rewards}
\label{sec:step4}
The fourth step is specific to FARM and turns the product into a Reward Machine. For every clause, each product state is labelled as satisfied, pending (not yet satisfied but still possible) or violated (no longer satisfiable). The labels are computed exactly, by reachability on the DFA of each clause. Hard clauses prune the machine and decide the final states, soft clauses only change rewards, and the rewards are assigned last (Figure~\ref{fig:step4}).

\subsection{Merging MONA's start state}
\label{sec:start-merge}
MONA gives each rule clause a ``no step yet'' start that is left on the first step and never re-entered. When that start goes to the same state as its idle successor on every input, the successor becomes the start and the old start is dropped. Otherwise the machine is unchanged. Without the merge, an idle first step would still pay progress.
% What will go here: the merge conditions and their effect on the test tasks.

\subsection{Identifying final states}
A final state is a state in which every hard clause is satisfied, and only a task with a hard goal has one. Soft clauses never affect the final condition: a preference broken on the way still allows the task to finish, at a cost.
% What will go here: a worked example with one hard goal and one soft rule.

\subsection{Sending failed states to a rejecting sink}
An edge into a state where a hard clause is violated goes to a single shared well, a rejecting state that ends the episode with a penalty. The penalty adds to the $-1$ of the break the charge for every item open in the state the edge leaves (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}), so the edges into the one well differ in their reward. In a task with a hard goal, the same happens to states from which no final state can be reached. A hard clause that is only pending, such as a Response still waiting for its answer, blocks finishing but does not end the episode.
% What will go here: the well in the example machines and why one well is enough.

\subsection{Reward assignment}
The rewards follow one rule for every task: getting closer to finishing a task or a rule pays, and stepping back from it or getting a new thing to do costs. The new thing to do can come from the agent, which broke a rule, or from the environment, which changed so that the instructions now ask for a reaction. FARM treats the task as a to-do list. Every clause, goal or rule, hard or soft, is an item that counts the steps it still needs. Each step done pays $+0.1$, and each step back or step of new work costs $-0.1$. When the episode ends, each item still open is charged, $-1$ if it is hard and $-0.5$ if it is soft, and a goal is a hard item. A few constants complete the rewards (Table~\ref{tab:rewards}).

Progress follows the potential-based form of Ng et al.~\cite{ng1999policy}: a transition from $u$ to $u'$ adds
\[ F(u,u') = \Phi(u') - \Phi(u), \qquad \Phi(u) = -0.1 \sum_{i} \ell_i(u_i), \]
where $u_i$ is the state of clause $i$ in the product state $u$, the tuple that the fork of FL-AT returns (Section~\ref{sec:step3}), and $\ell_i(u_i)$ is the length of the longest path from $u_i$ to an accepting state in the DFA of clause $i$. An accepting state needs no more steps, and the loops of each clause DFA collapse into one node, so every $\ell_i$ is finite. A soft clause broken for good counts zero, because it paid its penalty when it broke. Hard and soft items weigh the same, and $\Phi = 0$ at the final states. A step that advances an item pays $0.1$ for each step gained, staying pays nothing, and an event that gives an item more to do costs $0.1$ for each step it adds. The longest path is used rather than the shortest because a DFA can have shortcut edges where two events happen in one step, which the environment may never produce, and these would make an item look closer to done than it is. Edges into the well, and into a state from which no target can be reached, pay no progress.

Measuring each item on its own DFA gives every event the same price wherever it happens. A potential measured on the whole product, as the longest path to a final state or to a state with nothing pending, has to collapse the loops of the product to stay finite, and a loop then pays nothing unless one of its ends is a target. Some rules would get no reward when they are triggered, although a clear reward exists. In example 2, dropping the key and picking it up at the next step moves the machine from u0 to u1 and back; the product-wide potential pays both edges 0, and the to-do list pays $-0.1$ for the drop and $+0.1$ for the pick-up. The product-wide potential also prices one event differently depending on what else is pending: in a rule-only task about an alarm, the same ring and switch-off paid $-0.3/{+0.3}$, $-0.1/{+0.1}$ or $0$ depending on the state, and the to-do list pays $-0.1/{+0.1}$ in all three (Appendix~\ref{app:alarm}).

\subsection{Recurrent soft-rule templates}
\label{sec:recurrent}
An LTLf formula gives one verdict per trace, so a soft rule would cost one penalty per episode however often it is broken. For the four templates whose DFA has a violated state (Absence, Precedence, ChainResponse and NotCoExistence), FARM re-arms a soft clause: its violated state takes the edges of the start state, so every new break costs $-0.5$ again. A step that breaks the rule while it is still broken is free, and the rule is armed again on the next step. Existence and ExistenceTwo are excluded, because paying again would invite reward farming.

\subsection{Rule-only machines}
\label{sec:rule-only}
A task with no hard goal has no final state: only the well or the end set by the environment ends the episode, and the compiler warns the user that the task has no goal. The to-do list needs no special case for such a task. Many rules wait for an event. When the event happens, caused by the agent or by the environment, the rule adds an item, and the step costs $-0.1$ at once. The reaction that the rule asks for removes the item and pays the $0.1$ back. In the rule ``Whenever a window breaks, fix it'', Response(\texttt{window\_broken}, \texttt{window\_fixed}), the window breaks while the agent does nothing, yet from that step the agent owes a repair. A window that breaks and is fixed totals 0, the same as a window that never breaks, whether the fix comes at the next step or three steps later. The penalty tells the agent that something needs a reaction, and the only reaction that earns it back is the one the user asked for. The same holds when the agent starts the wait itself: in example 4 (Appendix~\ref{app:ex4}), dropping the key costs $-0.1$ and picking it up again pays $+0.1$.

The graph gives a second reading. A state with an open item is a state to run away from: if the episode ends there, every open item is charged (Section~\ref{sec:open-promises}), and the other ways out cost too: the well charges the broken hard rule and every item still open, and a broken soft rule costs $-0.5$. The only free way out is the edge that does what the rule or the preference asks, so running away from these states and doing the task are the same thing.

\subsection{Open items at the end of an episode}
\label{sec:open-promises}
When the environment ends the episode, at the time limit or for any other reason, and the machine has not ended it itself, the machine charges every item still open: $-1$ per hard item and $-0.5$ per soft item, the values \texttt{HARD\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD} and \texttt{SOFT\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD}, counted for each state in \texttt{PENDING\_\allowbreak PROMISES}. A goal is a hard item, so a task that ends without its goal pays $-1$ per goal clause, plus $-1$ for every hard rule still waiting for its answer. The charge is added once, to the reward of the last step, and belongs to no edge of the machine, so no loop can collect it. The charge replaces the flat $-0.5$ penalty of the time limit; only machines without the list of open items, such as hand-written ones, keep the flat penalty. The well charges the same items, so that breaking a rule never ends the episode more cheaply (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).

A machine with a final state ends the episode itself when the task is done, and success means reaching that state. A machine without a final state marks as accepting the states where nothing is still owed (\texttt{ACCEPTING\_STATES}), and an episode that the environment ends in one of them is a success. This differs from LTLf acceptance: a soft rule broken for good counts as accepted, because it already paid $-0.5$ when it broke, and a hard rule broken for good leads to the well, which rejects on its own. Being in an accepting state never ends the episode.

\subsection{Breaking a rule never pays better than running out of time}
\label{sec:well-charge}
Charging the open items at the end of an episode opens a way to exploit the well. With several items open, running out of time can cost more than breaking a hard rule. In example 1, ``Pick up the key, then open the door, then reach the goal'', an episode that times out with nothing done pays $-3$ for its three open goals, while a step into the well pays only $-1$. An agent close to the time limit could then learn to break a rule on purpose to end the episode more cheaply.

FARM closes this on the edges into the well. Each edge into the well pays $-1$ plus the charge the episode would get if it ended in the state the edge leaves: $-1$ per hard item and $-0.5$ per soft item open there, the broken rule included when it was waiting. Breaking a rule is then always worse than running out of time in that state. In example 1, a break with nothing done costs $-4$ against $-3$ for the time limit, and a break from a state with nothing open still costs $-1$. In example 2, ``Reach the goal, and if you drop the key, pick it up right away'', the state u1, with the key dropped and the goal still open, costs $-2$ at the time limit and $-3$ on the edge into the well; with the flat penalty of the time limit and a plain well, the same two endings paid $-0.5$ and $-1$. From u3, the goal reached with the key still owed, the edge into the well costs $-2$. The same rule settles the case of two hard rules still waiting at the end of a rule-only episode, which would otherwise cost $-2$ against $-1$ for a break.

The machine keeps a single well, and the charge sits on each edge into it, so the runtime that executes the machine needs no change. The charge is paid once, on an edge into an absorbing state, so no loop can collect it.

Example 3 shrinks from five states to three. The start p0 and its idle twin p1 merge into u0, and the goal states p3 and p4 become the final state u2, since the only hard clause holds in both. The key is a soft clause, so no state leads to a well. Losing the key costs $-0.5$ and moves the machine to u1, the former p2. It returns to u0 at the next step without a loss, so each new loss is charged again (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}). The rewards of FL-AT are gone: idle steps pay nothing, and the goal pays 1.1, or 0.6 when the key is lost in the very step of the goal.

Example 2 shrinks from seven states to five. Again p0 and p1 merge into u0. The state p2, where the key is owed, becomes u1, and p4, the goal reached with the key still owed, becomes u3. Only p3 becomes final, as u2: in p4 the hard rule is still pending and blocks the end of the task. The two states where the rule is broken, p5 and p6, become the rejecting well u4, entered with reward $-1$ when the key is not back at the next step. The farmed cycle of Step~3 disappears, because the final state ends the episode. It pays 1.2 when the goal is reached with the key in hand and 1.1 when the key comes back after the goal.

Example 1 shrinks from 14 states to five. State u1 is reached after the key, u3 after the key and the door, and u4, the final state, after the goal. Every product state that breaks the order of the task becomes the well u2. Opening the door without the key or reaching the goal too early, which FL-AT still rewarded, now costs $-1$. Progress pays 0.1 or 0.2, and the goal pays 1.1 to 1.3: the 1 of the final state plus the progress of the step. \subsection{LLMs can hallucinate}
A critic model can hallucinate as well as the generator \cite{huang2025survey}. Its verdict therefore only decides whether the generator tries again; the guarantees of the machine come from the compiler (Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal}).

Generator and critic are configured separately. By default the generator is deepseek-v4.1-flash and the critic glm-5.3-flash, so the two roles use different models.

\subsection{Number of iterations}
The generator has three attempts by default, configurable from one to ten. Only rejections by the critic use up an attempt; a failure of the provider is retried without cost.

\subsection{Fixing mistakes}
When the critic flags a clause, the generator receives the comments in its prompt and proposes new clauses, from which the compiler rebuilds the machine.

\subsection{Flagging clauses whose states cannot occur in the environment}
Some clauses lead to machine states that the environment can never reach, for example a door that opens before the key is held. The critic flags these clauses, and the generator retunes them. The critic does not delete states.

\label{sec:output-format}
FARM writes each machine in the plain-text format of ARM-FM (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}) and extends it. Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1} shows the machine of the first running example.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\lstinputlisting[style=rm,caption={The Reward Machine FARM generates for the first running example.},label={lst:farm-example1}]{images/farm/rm-ex1.txt}
\end{minipage}

The format of FARM differs from that of ARM-FM in five points.
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are no \texttt{else} self-loops. A state with no matching transition stays where it is and pays the default reward.
    \item The states with a special role are listed explicitly: \texttt{FINAL\_STATES}, \texttt{REJECTING\_STATES} and \texttt{ACCEPTING\_STATES}.
    \item A single event expression combines propositions with \texttt{!} and \texttt{\&}, such as \texttt{has\_key \& !door\_open}, instead of a separate predicate such as \texttt{not\_has\_key}.
    \item \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD}, \texttt{HARD\_FAILED\_REWARD} and \texttt{SOFT\_FAILED\_REWARD} fix the reward of a transition without an entry and the charge for a failed promise (Section~\ref{sec:open-promises}).
    \item \texttt{PENDING\_PROMISES} lists, for each state, the hard and soft obligations still open.
\end{itemize}
Appendix~\ref{app:running} lists the machine of a task made only of rules, where the last two points apply.

\section{Details}

\subsection{Contributions}
FARM adds the following to the pipeline it builds on.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A prompt that covers all eight Declare templates and serves any environment, because the propositions and their meaning come from the environment description (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item A restricted answer: the language model can only choose a template, its propositions and a priority (Section~\ref{sec:schemas}).
    \item A fork of FL-AT that returns the state of every clause in each product state (Section~\ref{sec:step3}).
    \item A post-process with hard and soft clauses and a rejecting well, whose rewards treat the task as a to-do list: a step done pays, a new thing to do costs, and the items still open are charged when the environment ends the episode (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item A critic loop in which the critic flags clauses and the generator retunes them (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
\end{itemize}
The labelling functions and the state descriptions then follow ARM-FM, which turns the machine into a LARM.

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}
% TODO: conclusions on the pipeline as a whole (Steps 1-5 and the comparison with ARM-FM).
The rewards of FARM treat the task as a to-do list, so the transitions of the machine price what happens in the environment and what the instructions ask in return. On the machines of this thesis, a step towards the task or towards a rule pays, and a broken rule or a new thing to do costs. A step out of the order of the task leads to the well, the same event costs the same wherever it happens, a break that is repaired pays back what it cost, breaking a rule costs more than running out of time, and no loop pays (Section~\ref{sec:rewards-example} and Appendix~\ref{app:rewards}).

\evidencegap{pending}
{The learning runs of Section~\ref{sec:res-learning}.}
{Agents trained with the machines of FARM learn the tasks faster than with those of the rebuilt ARM-FM. Machines built from the logic of the task also teach it faster.}
{Agents trained with the machines of FARM learn the tasks no faster than with those of the rebuilt ARM-FM. The gain that this thesis shows lies in the machines: they follow the task by construction, and their rewards can be read and checked.}

\evidencegap{pending}
{The reaction runs of Section~\ref{sec:res-react}.}
{Agents also learn from these rewards how to react. The reward tells the agent what is good and what is wrong, what it must still do, how it must react to an event and in which order it must finish the task, and the agent follows it.}
{Whether a learner turns these rewards into reactions remains open: in the runs of this thesis, agents do not repair events more often than with the earlier design. The rewards are consistent and cannot be farmed, which the checks on the machines show, but that does not yet make the agent react.}

\chapter{Implementation details}
\label{app:implementation}

\section{IBM nl2ltl}
FARM uses a fork of IBM's nl2ltl (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/nl2ltl}), which is forked from \url{https://github.com/IBM/nl2ltl} \cite{nl2ltl_code}. The GPT engine of nl2ltl is bypassed. FARM supplies its own engine, which asks a language model for a list of clauses under a JSON schema and passes each clause to the Declare template classes of nl2ltl, which build the LTLf formula. Only the template classes and the LTLf builders are reused. The change in the fork concerns the build: it supports modern Python packaging and drops the pin on the OpenAI client, which the new engine does not need.

\section{FL-AT}
FARM uses a fork of FL-AT (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/Flat}), which is forked from \url{https://github.com/Jamidd/Flat} \cite{flat_code}. The fork makes five changes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Library packaging: the code installs as a package and uses package-relative imports.
    \item MONA runs in a subprocess with temporary files, where the original wrote a file in the working folder through \texttt{os.system}, and its output is parsed strictly.
    \item Proposition names are renamed with regular expressions, where the original split strings and evaluated labels.
    \item MONA's state 0, the do-nothing start state of Section~\ref{sec:redundant}, is removed.
    \item The state tuples of the product are exported, so that each product state tells which state every clause is in.
\end{itemize}

\section{Models and settings}
Unless stated otherwise, generation uses the defaults of the configuration: the OpenCode provider, deepseek-v4.1-flash as generator, glm-5.3-flash as critic, reasoning level none and at most three attempts. The running examples did not use these defaults. The monthly quota of the OpenCode plan was exhausted on 29 October 2026, so they were produced with the Antigravity provider (Section~\ref{sec:examples-produced}).

The only clause is a rule, so the machine has no goal and no final state (Listing~\ref{lst:ex4-rm} and Figure~\ref{fig:ex4-rm}). State u0 is accepting: nothing is owed there. State u1 owes the key, and \texttt{PENDING\_\allowbreak PROMISES} lists it with one hard promise. If the environment ends the episode in u1, the machine charges $-1$ once, the value \texttt{HARD\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD}. In u0 it charges nothing. Dropping the key costs $-0.1$ and picking it up returns $+0.1$, so the two shaping rewards cancel over the cycle.

\section{An alarm and a power cut}
\label{app:alarm}
\textbf{Task.} When the alarm rings, switch it off at the very next step. Preferably, if the power ever goes out, run the generator.

\noindent{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.2cm}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Clause} & \textbf{Priority} & \textbf{LTLf} \\ \midrule
ChainResponse(\texttt{alarm\_rings}, \texttt{alarm\_off}) & hard & $\mathrm{G}\,(\texttt{alarm\_rings} \rightarrow \mathrm{X}\,\texttt{alarm\_off})$ \\
RespondedExistence(\texttt{power\_cut}, \texttt{generator\_on}) & soft & $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{power\_cut} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,\texttt{generator\_on}$ \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

The task has no goal, so the machine has no final state (Listing~\ref{lst:alarm-rm}). This machine holds several cases at once: three loops on the same event, a well, and steps in which two or three events happen at once. Table~\ref{tab:alarm-todo} gives the to-do list of its seven states.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{The to-do list of each state of the alarm machine.}
\label{tab:alarm-todo}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{State} & \textbf{Still to do} & \textbf{Score} & \textbf{Charge at the end} \\ \midrule
u0, accepting & nothing & $0$ & $0$ \\
u1 & run the generator (soft) & $-0.1$ & $-0.5$ \\
u2, accepting & nothing, the generator ran & $0$ & $0$ \\
u3 & switch the alarm off (hard) & $-0.1$ & $-1$ \\
u4 & switch the alarm off, run the generator & $-0.2$ & $-1.5$ \\
u5 & switch the alarm off, the generator ran & $-0.1$ & $-1$ \\
u6, well & the alarm was not switched off in time & --- & --- \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The alarm can ring in u0, u1 and u2, and each time it adds one item. The ring costs $-0.1$ and switching the alarm off at the next step pays $+0.1$, in each of the three loops u0--u3, u1--u4 and u2--u5. The product-wide potential priced the same ring in three ways: $-0.3/{+0.3}$ in u0--u3, $-0.1/{+0.1}$ in u2--u5 and $0$ in u1--u4. Every loop nets $0$ under both designs, so no loop pays, but only the to-do list gives the ring one price.

Several events can happen in one step, and their prices add up (Table~\ref{tab:alarm-steps}). Switching the alarm off as the power goes out crosses one item off and adds another, and pays $0$; the product-wide potential paid $+0.1$ for it.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Steps of the alarm machine in which several events happen at once.}
\label{tab:alarm-steps}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{What happens} & \textbf{Items} & \textbf{Product-wide} & \textbf{To-do list} \\ \midrule
u3 $\to$ u1 & alarm off, power goes out & $-1$, $+1$ & $+0.1$ & $0$ \\
u3 $\to$ u4 & alarm off, rings again, power goes out & $-1$, $+2$ & $+0.1$ & $-0.1$ \\
u3 $\to$ u5 & alarm off, rings again, generator on & $-1$, $+1$ & $+0.2$ & $0$ \\
u3 $\to$ u2 & alarm off, generator on & $-1$ & $+0.3$ & $+0.1$ \\
u1 $\to$ u5 & alarm rings, generator on & $-1$, $+1$ & $+0.1$ & $0$ \\
u0 $\to$ u4 & alarm rings, power goes out & $+2$ & $-0.2$ & $-0.2$ \\
u4 $\to$ u2 & alarm off, generator on & $-2$ & $+0.2$ & $+0.2$ \\
u4 $\to$ u5 & alarm off, rings again, generator on & $-2$, $+1$ & $+0.1$ & $+0.1$ \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The edges into the well pay $-1$ plus the charge of the state they leave (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}): $-2$ from u3 and from u5, and $-2.5$ from u4, where the generator is also owed. With a plain well they all paid $-1$. Not switching the alarm off now costs more than letting the time run out in the same state.

\section{A door and a box}
\label{app:door-box}
\textbf{Task.} Open the door. Whenever the box falls off the shelf, preferably put it back.

\noindent{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.2cm}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Clause} & \textbf{Priority} & \textbf{LTLf} \\ \midrule
Existence(\texttt{door\_open}) & hard & $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{door\_open}$ \\
Response(\texttt{box\_fell}, \texttt{box\_on\_shelf}) & soft & $\mathrm{G}\,(\texttt{box\_fell} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,\texttt{box\_on\_shelf})$ \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

The machine has three states (Listing~\ref{lst:door-box-rm}): u0 owes the door, with score $-0.1$ and charge $-1$; u2 owes the door and the box, with score $-0.2$ and charge $-1.5$; and u1 is final. The loop u0--u2, where the box falls and is put back, paid nothing with the product-wide potential and pays $-0.1/{+0.1}$ with the to-do list. Table~\ref{tab:door-box-traces} adds up six traces.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Rewards of six traces of the door and box task, step by step. Idle steps are left out; when the environment ends the episode, the last reward includes the charge for the items still open.}
\label{tab:door-box-traces}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Product-wide}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{To-do list}} \\ \cmidrule(lr){2-3} \cmidrule(l){4-5}
\textbf{Trace} & \textbf{Steps} & \textbf{Total} & \textbf{Steps} & \textbf{Total} \\ \midrule
Nothing happens until the episode ends & $-0.5$ & $-0.5$ & $-1$ & $-1$ \\
The box falls and stays down & $0,\ -0.5$ & $-0.5$ & $-0.1,\ -1.5$ & $-1.6$ \\

When the door opens with the box still on the floor, the finishing edge pays $1 + 0.2 - 0.5 = 0.7$, where $-0.5$ is the charge of a soft clause still pending, and the trace totals $0.6$ under both designs.
