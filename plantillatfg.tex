\begin{abstract}
La construcció automàtica de màquines de recompensa (\foreign{english}{Reward Machines}, RM) en l'aprenentatge per reforç s'ha explorat recentment amb grans models fundacionals, que generen RM directament a partir de descripcions de tasques en llenguatge natural. Tot i ser prometedors, aquests mètodes no oferixen garanties formals de la correcció de les RM i depenen d'una persona o d'un segon model per a revisar-les. En aquest treball proposem FARM (\foreign{english}{A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning}), una cadena de processament basada en la lògica temporal lineal (LTL) per a generar RM: un model de llenguatge traduïx primer les tasques en llenguatge natural a fórmules LTL, que després es compilen en màquines de recompensa amb eines de la teoria d'autòmats. Aquest enfocament produïx RM que, per construcció, estan alineades amb l'especificació LTL, si bé la traducció mateixa encara pot ser errònia. L'avaluem amb dues proves: una construïx les RM i les comprova davant de la seua tasca sense entrenar cap agent, i l'altra hi entrena agents i jutja si aprenen la tasca. En un banc de proves de tasques amb estructura temporal, el nostre mètode basat en LTL produïx RM més fidels a l'especificació de la tasca que les d'una reconstrucció d'ARM-FM, un enfocament de l'estat de l'art basat en models fundacionals: sense editar a mà cap RM, FARM construïx el 99,6\,\% de les RM demanades i ARM-FM, el 57\,\%. Els agents entrenats amb les RM de FARM aprenen la tasca en el 72\,\% dels casos d'entrenament, enfront del 48\,\% amb les d'ARM-FM i del 38\,\% sense cap RM. Aquests resultats suggerixen que la síntesi formal de RM guiada per la lògica oferix una alternativa més fiable als mètodes de generació de recompenses purament neuronals.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}[english]
The automated construction of Reward Machines (RMs) in Reinforcement Learning has recently been explored using large foundation models, which generate RMs directly from natural language task descriptions. While promising, these methods lack formal guarantees of RM correctness and rely on a human or a second model to check the RMs. In this work, we propose FARM (A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning), a pipeline based on Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) for RM generation: natural language tasks are first translated by a language model into LTL formulas, which are then compiled into Reward Machines using automata-theoretic tools. This approach produces RMs that, by construction, are aligned with the LTL specification, though the translation itself can still be wrong. We evaluate it with two tests: one builds the RMs and checks them against their tasks without training an agent, and one trains agents on them and judges whether they learn the task. On a benchmark of temporally structured tasks, our LTL-based method produces RMs more faithful to the task specification than those of a rebuilt ARM-FM, a state-of-the-art foundation model approach: with no RM edited by hand, FARM builds 99.6\% of the RMs requested and ARM-FM 57\%. Agents trained on FARM's RMs learn the task in 72\% of the training cases, against 48\% with ARM-FM's and 38\% with no RM. These results suggest that logic-guided formal RM synthesis offers a more reliable alternative to purely neural reward generation methods.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Glossary}
\begin{description}
    \item[Arm] One way of training that the evaluation compares: FARM, ARM-FM, or training with no Reward Machine (``no RM'' in tables and figures).
    \item[ARM-FM] Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models: the method in which a foundation model writes a Reward Machine as text from a task in plain language. In this thesis it is also the arm that trains on the machines of its rebuilt version.
    \item[Card] One test case of the benchmark: a task written in plain language, together with the answers accepted for it. The word belongs to the evaluation; the other chapters say task.
    \item[Clause] One template chosen by the generator, with the propositions it applies to, a priority, hard or soft, and one plain sentence that states its part of the task.
    \item[Compiler] Steps 2 to 4 of FARM. They build the Reward Machine from the chosen clauses with fixed tools and call no language model.
    \item[Critic] The second language model, different from the generator. It accepts the result or rejects it with comments, and it never edits the machine.
    \item[DFA] Deterministic finite automaton: a machine with a finite set of states that reads a trace one step at a time and accepts or rejects it.
    \item[Final state] A state of the machine in which every hard clause is satisfied. Reaching it ends the episode, and only a task with a hard goal has one.
    \item[Generator] The language model that reads the task and writes it down: as clauses in FARM, as a machine in ARM-FM.
    \item[Goal and rule] A goal is a clause that asks for something to happen, so it stays unmet while nothing happens. Every other clause is a rule: it holds while nothing happens, and a later event may break it or make it wait for an answer.
    \item[Hard and soft] The priority of a clause. A hard clause must hold for the task to count as done; a soft clause is a preference, and each break costs reward but never prevents finishing. Hard rules and soft rules are the hard and soft clauses that are not goals; where the goal is included, the text says hard clauses.
    \item[Judged episode] One evaluation episode played after training, with the agent taking its most likely action at every step, and scored by the judge.
    \item[Judge] The scorer of the evaluation. It reads each judged episode through the benchmark's own labelling and checks the clauses expected for the task, in the same way for every arm.
    \item[Labelling function] The function that reads the world at every step and returns the propositions that hold, which the Reward Machine follows.
    \item[LARM] Language-Aligned Reward Machine, the output of ARM-FM: a Reward Machine, the code of its labelling function and a plain-language description of each state.
    \item[Learned] A training case counts as learned for an arm when at least two of its seeds succeed in 0.8 or more of their judged episodes. Shop tasks with a rule about serving orders also need enough orders served, and a task with no goal and only soft rules is compared with an idle agent (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). A case whose machine was not built counts as not learned.
    \item[Loop that pays] A cycle of transitions whose rewards add up to more than zero, so that an agent can collect reward by going round it without doing the task.
    \item[LTL and LTLf] Linear Temporal Logic, a logic of properties over sequences of states. LTLf reads the same formulas over finite sequences, such as an episode.
    \item[Plan check] The run of a task's scripted plans through a built machine, to read what each plan pays. No language model takes part.
    \item[Proposition] A fact about the world that is true or false at each step, such as \texttt{has\_key}. The labelling function reports which propositions hold.
    \item[Re-arming and folding] Two ways in which FARM handles a soft rule. A re-armed soft rule watches for the next break after each one, so every break costs again; a folded soft Absence adds no state to the machine, and its break becomes a loop that costs reward.
    \item[Reward Machine (RM)] A finite-state machine that reads which propositions hold at each step, moves between its states and pays a reward on each transition. Once the context is clear, the thesis says machine.
    \item[Rewording] The same task, said in other words.
    \item[Scripted plan] A fixed sequence of actions written for a task: one solves the task, others each break a named rule, and an idle one only waits.
    \item[Stage] One event still needed to finish the task. Every state of a FARM machine owes a number of stages, and progress pays as that number falls.
    \item[Task schema] The clauses of a task with the propositions left open. Tasks of one schema get machines of one shape, with the names exchanged.
    \item[Template] One of the eight fixed patterns of IBM nl2ltl, taken from Declare, such as Existence, Precedence or Absence. Each has a fixed LTLf formula in which only the propositions change.
    \item[Thinking level] How much a model reasons before it answers, from none to max. The frozen pipeline uses max.
    \item[Training case] One task trained under one setting. The training test has 60, on 53 different cards (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}).
    \item[Well] The one rejecting state of a FARM machine, which a machine may lack. Entering it ends the episode. An edge leads to it when a step breaks a hard clause or, in a task with a hard goal, leaves the task impossible to finish, and each such edge carries its own price.
\end{description}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
\section{Motivation}
In reinforcement learning (RL) an agent learns to act by maximising the reward it receives, so the reward decides what the agent learns \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Writing that reward is hard. When the reward arrives only at the end of a long task, the agent gets almost no signal while it learns, and reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it \cite{ng1999policy}. Shaping improves learning, but people write and tune reward functions by hand, which takes time and needs expertise in both the task and RL.

A tool that builds the reward from a statement of the task would save that time and open RL to people without that technical knowledge. Formal methods suit such a tool: a task stated in a formal language has one exact meaning, and fixed tools can turn it into a reward in the same way every time. Reward Machines, finite-state machines that pay reward as the steps of a task are completed, are a natural target, because they expose the structure of the task to the learner (Section~\ref{sec:sota-rm}).

Programming assistants built on large language models can help write code, so they could help with rewards as well. ARM-FM \cite{creus2026armfm} takes this route and lets a foundation model write the Reward Machine from a task in plain language (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}). A language model alone is not reliable, though: it can give different answers to the same input \cite{ouyang2025empirical} and state things that are false \cite{huang2025survey}, and ARM-FM relies on a person or on a second model to check its machines.

A second motivation is to bring AI closer to what people want. A task as a person states it may hold more than a goal: an order of steps, rules that must not be broken and preferences to keep when possible. A reward that captures all of them says more of what the person means. The aim is to build such rewards without a human expert, with machines robust enough not to depend on the uncertainty of the language model. Something to clarify, that robustness is an aim of the method, not a property of every part of it: the model's reading of the task can still be wrong.

This thesis presents FARM, a formal approach to Reward Machine generation. A language model writes the task as clauses from a fixed catalogue of temporal patterns, each marked hard or soft, and fixed tools build the Reward Machine from those clauses alone (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}).

\section{Objectives}
The main objective is to build Reward Machines from tasks stated in plain language, with no machine edited by hand, on which agents learn the tasks. In detail:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Capture the whole task as a person states it: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.
    \item Build the machine with fixed tools from a formal statement of the task, so that only the reading of the task is left to the language model.
    \item Need no human expert to check or repair the machines.
    \item Verify the correctness of the method both qualitatively and empirically and compare the results against ARM-FM and training with no Reward Machine.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Expected impact}
The intended benefit is reward shaping and task definition open to people who are not experts in RL, and better learning through machines that are built formally rather than written freely. Three kinds of user are in view: a developer who wants a machine that trains, a person without technical knowledge who states a task in plain language, and other agents that call the tool. The tool also brings AI closer to people: a person states a task, with its rules and preferences, in their own words, and the agent learns from a reward that carries all of them. These are intended benefits: no study with users was run.

\section{Structure of the thesis}
The thesis starts with the concepts it builds on, where current methods stand and what they leave open (Chapters~\ref{chap:sota} and~\ref{chap:proposal}). It then presents the new method, FARM, and its limits (Chapters~\ref{chap:farm} and~\ref{chap:limitations}), tests it and shows the results (Chapters~\ref{chap:evaluation} and~\ref{chap:results}). It ends with the conclusions and the open work (Chapters~\ref{chap:conclusions} and~\ref{chap:future-work}). A glossary precedes this chapter, and the appendices hold further examples and the implementation details.

\section{Scope and limits of the study}
\enlargethispage{3\baselineskip}% keeps the last three lines of the chapter off a page of their own
FARM changes only one step of ARM-FM, the generation of the Reward Machine. The labelling functions, the state descriptions and their embeddings follow ARM-FM and sit outside the main comparison, so the comparison with ARM-FM is isolated to the generation of the machine.

The ARM-FM baseline is a reconstruction. It is rebuilt from the paper and from a public repository of its first author, so its results may differ from the original ones; Section~\ref{sec:critique} gives the details.

The models are small and cheap on purpose: they are not the strongest available, but they are enough to test the method and they keep it accessible. Both methods use deepseek-v4.1-flash as generator and glm-5.3-flash as critic, where the ARM-FM paper used GPT-4o. Whether the results hold for other models is not tested.

FARM states tasks with the eight Declare templates of IBM nl2ltl, read as LTLf over finite traces. A task that these templates cannot state is outside its reach (Section~\ref{sec:lim-conditions}). A model that writes LTLf directly is future work (Section~\ref{sec:fw-ltlf}).

\chapter{State of the art}
\label{chap:sota}
\section{Related work}
\subsection{Reward Machines}
\label{sec:sota-rm}
In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent learns to act in a Markov decision process (MDP) by maximising the reward it receives \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Most methods treat the reward function as a black box: the agent observes the number returned after each step and nothing about how it is computed \cite{icarte2022reward}. In practice a person writes that function by hand, so its structure is known and can be exploited to learn faster.

Sparse rewards pose a second problem. When the reward arrives only on completing a long task, the agent receives almost no signal while it learns. Reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it. Ng et al.~\cite{ng1999policy} prove that shaping with the difference of a potential function over states, $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$, leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that no other form of shaping gives this guarantee in general.

Reward Machines (RMs) address both problems by exposing the structure of the reward to the learner. Toro Icarte et al.~\cite{toroicarte2018using} introduced them at ICML 2018 and gave a fuller account in the Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research (JAIR) \cite{icarte2022reward}. This thesis follows the JAIR article for the formalisation, counterfactual learning and reward shaping.

An RM is a finite-state machine, written $\langle U, u_0, F, \delta_u, \delta_r\rangle$ in the JAIR formulation. $U$ is a finite set of states, $u_0 \in U$ the initial state and $F$ the set of terminal states. The transition function $\delta_u$ takes the current state and the set of propositions that hold and returns the next state. The reward function $\delta_r$ assigns to each state a reward function over environment transitions. The ICML formulation \textbf{attaches the reward to the pair of states of a transition instead}, which is the convention of the RM text format used in this thesis.

Propositions come from outside the machine. A labelling function $L$ maps each environment transition to the set of propositions that hold in it, for example ``the agent holds the key''. At every step the machine reads that set and moves. The agent learns in the product of the MDP and the RM, whose states pair an environment state with an RM state; in the product the reward is Markovian even when the task is not.

Exposing the structure enables dedicated learning methods. Q-learning for RMs (QRM) \cite{toroicarte2018using} decomposes the machine and learns, off-policy, one sub-policy per RM state in parallel. The JAIR article adds counterfactual reasoning for RMs (CRM), which reuses each experience to update every RM state; a hierarchical method (HRM); and automated reward shaping. The shaping potential is the negative optimal value of the RM state, $\Phi(s,u) = -v^{*}(u)$, computed by value iteration on the machine (Section~4.4 of the article, restricted to simple RMs).

RMs have two costs. Someone has to design the machine, and RMs do not by themselves solve the problem of specifying a reward that leads to the intended behaviour, although they may make temporally extended behaviours easier to specify \cite[p.~199]{icarte2022reward}. The labelling function is also assumed to exist and to be correct: the methods that learn RMs from experience all assume a correct labelling function, and learning both at once is one of the open problems listed in the article \cite[p.~201]{icarte2022reward}. Both costs motivate generating RMs automatically.

An RM is also a graph, a form that most people are not used to reading, and it becomes hard to follow once it has more than a few states and transitions. This motivates rewards that a person can read, and simplified drawings of the machines.

\subsection{ARM-FM}
\label{sec:sota-armfm}
ARM-FM (Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models), by Creus Castanyer et al.~\cite{creus2026armfm} and published at ICLR 2026, replaces the hand-built RM with one generated by a foundation model (FM) from a natural-language task description. The output is a Language-Aligned Reward Machine (LARM), made of three parts: an RM, the Python code of its labelling functions, and a natural-language description of each RM state. A text embedding of the description of the current state is given to the policy together with the environment observation, so that the policy knows which sub-goal is active. In this thesis \textbf{ARM-FM is the method and LARM its output}.

The RM is produced by a generator model and reviewed by a critic model over several rounds of self-improvement. A human may optionally approve the result or give corrective feedback. The generator prompt asks for the machine as plain text in a fixed format. The critic prompt asks, among other checks, that regressions are penalised and that no unintentional loops exist, and the critic answers either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED} \cite[pp.~26--27]{creus2026armfm}. The paper uses GPT-4o to generate all LARM components, except for the 1\,000 XLand-MiniGrid LARMs, which come from several open-source models \cite[p.~4]{creus2026armfm}.

The evaluation covers four environments: MiniGrid (DoorKey and the harder UnlockToUnlock, BlockedUnlockPickup and KeyCorridor), Craftium (a Minecraft-based environment where the agent mines a diamond after gathering wood, stone and iron), Meta-World manipulation tasks, and XLand-MiniGrid. On DoorKey the agent trained with the LARM outperforms DQN with ICM, ReAct and plain DQN; on the three harder MiniGrid tasks it is the only agent that solves all of them; on Craftium it learns where an agent without the LARM makes no progress. On XLand-MiniGrid a single policy trains on ten tasks at once and solves unseen combinations of known tasks without fine-tuning.

\subsection{Linear Temporal Logic}
\label{sec:sota-ltl}
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) is a logic for stating properties of sequences of states, called traces. Pnueli \cite{pnueli1977temporal} proposed it to reason about programs. It is a language rather than a catalogue of patterns: a formula is built from propositions, Boolean connectives and four temporal operators.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{X}\,\varphi$ (next): $\varphi$ holds at the next step.
    \item $\mathrm{F}\,\varphi$ (eventually): $\varphi$ holds at the current step or at a later one.
    \item $\mathrm{G}\,\varphi$ (always): $\varphi$ holds at every step from the current one on.
    \item $\varphi\ \mathrm{U}\ \psi$ (until): $\psi$ holds at some step from the current one on, and $\varphi$ holds at every step before it.
\end{itemize}
LTL is defined over infinite traces, whereas a reinforcement learning episode ends. LTLf, LTL over finite traces \cite{degiacomo2013linear}, keeps the syntax and reads a formula over a trace of finite length, where next requires a following step to exist. LTLf is as expressive as first-order logic over finite traces and corresponds to the star-free regular expressions.

In the DoorKey task of Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}, the propositions are \texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open} and \texttt{at\_goal}. ``The agent reaches the goal at some point'' is
\[ \mathrm{F}\ \texttt{at\_goal}, \]
and ``the door is not open before the key is held'' is
\[ (\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{has\_key}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}. \]

For every LTLf formula there is a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) that accepts exactly the traces satisfying it \cite{degiacomo2013linear}. In practice the formula is rewritten in monadic second-order logic over finite words, from which MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} builds the minimal DFA. This is the bridge used by Step~2 of FARM (Section~\ref{sec:step2}).

Camacho et al.~\cite{camacho2019ltl} bring this logic to RL. They show that RMs can express reward specifications written in LTL, LTLf and other formal languages, translate a specification into an RM and combine several specifications with a product construction.

Declare defines a catalogue of templates, such as Existence, Response or Precedence, each with an LTL semantics, to specify business processes declaratively; Pesic and van der Aalst \cite{pesic2006declarative} introduced the language under the name ConDec. nl2ltl \cite{fuggitti2023nl2ltl} translates natural language into LTL by letting a language model choose a Declare template and its arguments.

\subsection{Hard and soft constraints in planning}
\label{sec:sota-preferences}
In automated planning, a plan is a sequence of actions that takes a system from an initial state to a goal, and PDDL is the standard language to describe such problems. PDDL3 \cite{gerevini2006preferences} adds two things to it. State trajectory constraints are conditions on the whole sequence of states that the plan goes through, not only on the last one, written with operators such as \texttt{always}, \texttt{sometime}, \texttt{at-most-once} and \texttt{sometime-before}, close to those of LTL. Preferences are soft constraints: a valid plan must satisfy every hard constraint, while each preference it breaks only adds a cost to the plan. Goals and trajectory constraints can both be stated as preferences, so a condition over time can be either required or only preferred.

\subsection{Conclusions}
Each line of work covers part of the problem. Reward Machines expose the structure of a reward but are built by hand. ARM-FM generates them from language, and its guarantee holds only if the generated machine meets conditions that no automatic step checks. LTLf gives task statements a precise meaning and a route to automata, and planning separates the constraints that must hold from the preferences that only cost. Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal} states the aims that follow from them, and Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} combines these pieces.

\section{Critique of the state of the art}
\label{sec:critique}
\subsection{Narrow task variety}
The tasks that ARM-FM evaluates are simple in what they ask: most are a list of steps that ends in one goal. Its MiniGrid tasks are long and hard to explore, as the paper stresses \cite[pp.~5, 14]{creus2026armfm}, but what they ask is still such a list. In DoorKey the agent `finds a key`, `unlocks a door` and `reaches the goal`; in BlockedUnlockPickup it `moves a ball out of the way`, `takes a key`, `unlocks a door` and `picks up a box`; in Craftium it gathers `wood`, `stone`, `iron` and `diamond` in this order; etc.

Each machine is a chain of states, one per step, with at most a few edges that skip a step or go back after a loss. No task states a rule that must hold throughout the episode, and none separates what must happen from what is only preferable. 

\subsection{Inconsistent  and unstructured generation}
Language models do not return the same output on every run, even with the temperature set to zero. Ouyang et al.~\cite{ouyang2025empirical} measured this for ChatGPT on 829 code-generation problems, and the same task can therefore yield different machines. ARM-FM puts no fixed structure on what the model writes: the machine is plain text in a format described by the prompt, and the rewards are free numbers.

Free rewards allow loops that pay without progress. Proposition~1 of ARM-FM guarantees that optimising the LARM also solves the sparse task, provided the machine has no loop that pays (a cycle whose rewards add up to more than zero) and its final reward is larger than what any run that does not finish collects \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. The paper describes no automatic check of these conditions; the critic prompt only asks the model to verify that regressions are penalised, that reward magnitudes do not allow reward hacking and that there are no unintentional loops \cite[p.~27]{creus2026armfm}. 

This means the check fully relies on LLM inference, and \textbf{thus is not robust}. The Meta-World machine printed in the paper breaks the first condition: the cycle $u_0 \to u_1 \to u_2 \to u_0$ pays $+0.20$, $+0.40$ and $-0.40$, a total of $+0.20$ per lap \cite[p.~32]{creus2026armfm}, although the paper states that its LARMs satisfy the property \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. The DoorKey loop that pays $+0.2$ for picking the key up and $-0.2$ for dropping it sums to zero, so it meets the condition because two chosen values cancel, and nothing in the method enforces it.

A critic is itself a language model, and language models can state things that are false \cite{huang2025survey}. The survey covers generated text in general, and it warns that methods that rely on a model's self-correction ``may be unreliable'' \cite[Section~5.3.1]{huang2025survey}. A critic's approval is therefore no guarantee either; this is an inference, not a measured result.

\subsection{Verification by a human or another model}
ARM-FM states the limitation itself: human verification during RM generation is a trade-off that presupposes access to human verifiers. The authors add that such verifiers are not strictly required, although they can improve the output, and leave reducing this dependence by exploiting the automaton structure, for example through formal verification, as future work \cite[pp.~9--10]{creus2026armfm}. 

In 3 of the 7 settings of its Table~3 a human corrected or extended the machine: in UnlockPickup a human asked for a transition for a dropped key, which the model then added; in KeyCorridorS3R3 a human advised intermediate rewards; and in Meta-World a human rescaled the reward values \cite[p.~20]{creus2026armfm}. Some others do not need any human intevertion, which is worth noting.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The ARM-FM paper releases no code. Its appendices print the generator and critic prompts, six Reward Machines, the labelling functions of four MiniGrid tasks and the hyperparameters. A public repository of the first author \cite{armfm_code}, which the paper does not reference, adds prompts, generated machines and labelling code for four MiniGrid tasks only. This material allows the method to be rebuilt but not its exact outputs, so the ARM-FM baseline of this thesis is a reconstruction whose results may differ from the original ones. The authors did not answer the contact attempts made for further material.

\chapter{Proposal}
\label{chap:proposal}
The methods of Chapter~\ref{chap:sota} leave three needs open: a reward that says everything a person means, machines that need no expert to check them, and the same kind of machine for the same kind of task.

\section{Understanding human preferences and temporal relations}
An RL agent pursues whatever its reward pays for, so the reward is the way a person tells the agent what to achieve \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. AI alignment aims to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values \cite{ji2025alignment}. For an RL agent, one part of this aim is a reward that says everything the person means: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.

A task is rarely a single chain of steps. A person says, in plain language, what to do, in which order, what to do in response to an event and what must never happen. The first aim is to catch these relations of order, response and prohibition, together with the person's preferences, in a shape from which a reward can be built. That needs a translator from human intent into a language that encodes such relations exactly, a part that a foundation model can play.

Each statement is either hard or soft. A hard statement must hold for the task to count as done. A soft statement is a preference: the agent may give it up, at a cost. This split between constraints and preferences comes from planning (Section~\ref{sec:sota-preferences}), and the aim is to carry it into Reward Machines.

\section{Machines without a human expert}
ARM-FM relies on a human or on a second model to verify its machines, and its authors name human verification as a trade-off of their approach (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). The second aim is generation without a human expert: a person states the task, and no one who knows Reward Machines or reinforcement learning has to check or repair the machine. The properties that make a machine safe to train on, such as having no loop that pays, should follow from how the machine is built, and not from a reviewer who may miss them.

\section{Same kind of task, same kind of machine}
\label{sec:schemas}
A language model that writes a machine freely may write a different one on every run (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). The third aim is that tasks of one kind share a schema, the form of the task with its objects left open, and that one schema gives one kind of machine. ``Gather wood, then stone, then iron'' and ``attend the client, prepare the order, then serve it'' share the schema ``first $a$, then $b$, then $c$''; Table~\ref{tab:schemas} gives both with their clauses and formulas. Their machines should then have the same shape, with only the objects exchanged, so that the result is predictable and the variability of the language model is confined to how it reads the task. This is an aim, not a guarantee: tasks of one kind get one kind of machine only when the model reads them into the same schema. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} measures the closest case, whether the rewordings of one task get the same machine (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}).

Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} presents a method built for these three aims and says how it meets each one.

\chapter{FARM: A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation}
\label{chap:farm}
\section{Introduction and motivation}
This chapter describes the method by which FARM turns a task written in plain language into a Reward Machine; Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} holds the implementation. FARM replaces only the generation of the Reward Machine in ARM-FM (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}); the rest of the ARM-FM pipeline stays as it is.

Each aim of Chapter~\ref{chap:proposal} rests on one tool:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A language model reads the task and the environment description, and states what the person means: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules and the preferences.
    \item LTLf encodes these statements as formulas over finite traces (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}).
    \item The Declare templates of IBM nl2ltl guide the model: it picks a template and fills it, and the template writes the formula.
    \item FL-AT and MONA convert each formula into an automaton and combine the automata into one machine.
    \item FARM's post-process builds the Reward Machine from the chosen clauses alone.
    \item A second language model, the critic, reviews the clauses against the task.
\end{itemize}

\section{Global overview of the pipeline}
\label{sec:overview}
FARM turns a natural-language task into a Reward Machine in five steps: task interpretation (Step~1), LTLf to DFA (Step~2), the product of the DFAs (Step~3), the post-process (Step~4) and the critic (Step~5). A language model chooses clauses, fixed tools compile them into automata, a post-process adds the rewards and the structure of the task, and a second model reviews the result. Steps 2 to 4 form the \textbf{compiler}: they call no language model and read only the chosen clauses and the run settings. The two language models of Steps 1 and 5 are therefore the only source of variability. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} draws the pipeline, and Table~\ref{tab:steps} gives the input, output and tool of each step.

\subsection{IBM nl2ltl}
IBM's nl2ltl \cite{fuggitti2023nl2ltl} goes from natural language to LTLf through templates. A language model chooses which templates to use and which propositions fill them, and each template maps to a fixed LTLf formula in which only the propositions change. All the work therefore lies in the choice: a template is a fixed pattern with holes, and the model only says which pattern to use and what goes in the holes. The choice is still hard, since the model has to understand the task, its order and the dependencies between its steps, rules and goals. FARM keeps the template classes of nl2ltl and replaces its engine (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

RespondedExistence$(a,b)$ & $\mathrm{F}\,a \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,b$ & if $a$ ever happens, $b$ happens too & rule \\
Response$(a,b)$ & $\mathrm{G}\,(a \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,b)$ & every $a$ is answered by $b$ at the same step or later & rule \\
Precedence$(a,b)$ & $(\neg b\ \mathrm{U}\ a) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg b$ & $b$ cannot happen before $a$ & rule \\
ChainResponse$(a,b)$ & $\mathrm{G}\,(a \rightarrow \mathrm{X}\,b)$ & every $a$ is followed by $b$ at the next step & rule \\
NotCoExistence$(a,b)$ & $\mathrm{F}\,a \rightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{F}\,b$ & if $a$ ever happens, $b$ never does & rule \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The eight Declare templates of IBM nl2ltl with their LTLf definition and kind.}
\label{tab:templates}
\end{table}

The formulas use four operators (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}): $\mathrm{F}\,a$ means that $a$ holds at some step from now on, $\mathrm{G}\,a$ that $a$ holds at every step from now on, $\mathrm{X}\,a$ that $a$ holds at the next step, and $a\ \mathrm{U}\ b$ that $b$ holds at some step and $a$ holds at every step before it.

Response and Precedence are easy to confuse, and both allow the same step. Response$(a,b)$ lets $b$ appear without $a$, but after an $a$ a $b$ must follow, at the same step or later. Precedence$(a,b)$ lets $a$ appear without $b$, but a $b$ needs an $a$ before it or at the same step.

\subsection{Goals and rules}
A clause is a \textbf{goal} if a step in which no proposition holds leaves it unsatisfied, and a \textbf{rule} otherwise. Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals: they ask for something to happen. The other six templates are rules, Precedence included: they hold while nothing happens, and a later event may break them or make them wait for an answer. The compiler applies the test to the DFA of each clause, so it needs no list of templates. The difference shapes Step~4: only a hard goal gives the machine a final state, and progress counts the stages still needed to reach it.

\subsection{The generator prompt}
IBM's default prompt is a few-shot list that offers five of the eight templates, with examples from business applications such as Slack or Salesforce, no definitions and no description of the environment. FARM's prompt offers all eight, adding Absence, Precedence and NotCoExistence. It gives the model the catalogue with the meaning, kind and formula of each template, a table from typical wording to template, worked examples, the instructions for hard and soft, the environment description in Markdown, the task and, on a retry, the comments of the critic. The propositions come from the environment description, so the same prompt serves any environment.

Three instructions shape the answer. First, a commanded step that no hard order links to a later step, such as a single ``do $a$'' or the last step of an order, gets its own hard Existence: a rule holds while nothing happens, and only a goal makes the step happen. Second, an order that the task states is kept as a Precedence even when the environment already forces it, so the machine follows the instruction and not one layout of the world. Third, an order that the task does not state is never added. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives the prompt text and the form of an ordered task.

\subsection{Hard and soft clauses}
Each clause carries a \textbf{priority that the model chooses for that clause alone}. Clauses are hard by default. The model marks a clause soft only when the task uses a preference cue such as ``if possible'', ``ideally'' or ``try to'', and the cue covers the phrase it qualifies, not the whole sentence or the task. The priority never changes the formula. A hard clause must hold for the task to be completed. A soft clause is a preference: breaking it costs reward but never prevents finishing (Step~4). IBM's templates have no such notion, since ``never touch the lava'' and ``if possible, never touch the lava'' are the same Absence clause, so the priority has to come from the prompt.

\subsection{FL-AT and MONA}
FL-AT, the Formal Language--Automaton Transmogrifier \cite{transmogrifier2020}, writes each LTLf formula in monadic second-order logic over finite strings, the input language of MONA. In that encoding a temporal operator becomes a quantifier over the positions of the trace, and a proposition becomes the set of positions where it holds. MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} decides a formula of this logic by building the minimal DFA of its models.

The fork of FL-AT that FARM uses runs MONA as a separate process and reads back the automaton that MONA prints: its initial state, its accepting states and its transitions, each with a guard over the propositions. The reading is strict, and a malformed automaton stops the build (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The fork changes two things: it drops MONA's first state when that state is redundant (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}), and it returns the clause states behind every product state (Step~3).

\subsection{Redundant states in MONA's automata}
\label{sec:redundant}
The automaton that MONA returns is minimal for the language of its formula, yet two of its states are redundant for a Reward Machine. Figure~\ref{fig:mona-stages} in Appendix~\ref{app:mona} shows both for the clause Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}).

The first is MONA's first state, S0 in panel~(a), which every input leaves for the same state. The fork drops it only when it is not accepting, has a single successor, is never re-entered and its transitions cover every input exactly once. The successor then becomes the start. Otherwise the DFA stays as MONA made it.

The second appears in rule clauses: a ``no step yet'' start that nothing re-enters and that moves on every input like the state that a step with no event leads to (its idle successor, Section~\ref{sec:start-merge}), S1 against S2 in panel~(b). Step~4 merges the two, which gives panel~(c).

Step~4 reads the tuples to label each clause in each state, to count the stages each state still owes and to fold a soft Absence.

The product starts in the tuple of the clause starts. From each tuple, every combination of one edge per clause gives a product edge, whose guard is the conjunction of the guards of those edges; a combination whose guard can never hold is dropped. Only the tuples reachable from the start become states.

The careful key shows the construction by hand. Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}) and Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}) both start in S1, so the product starts in (S1, S1). A step with neither event moves Existence to S1 and Absence to S2, so the product moves to (S1, S2). A step that loses the key reaches (S1, S3), and the goal reaches (S2, S2), or (S2, S3) when the key is lost in the same step. Table~\ref{tab:run-product} lists the five states. \subsection{Discarded features of FL-AT's product}
FL-AT also prices the product and marks its final states, and FARM discards both. FL-AT pays an edge one unit for every clause whose DFA is accepting after the step, and pays again at every later step that lands in an accepting state; other steps pay zero. Its accepting states are the destinations of the edges with the largest such reward, so which states are final follows from the rewards.

The careful-key product of Figure~\ref{fig:run-product3} shows both features. A step with neither event moves from p0 to p1 and pays 1, because the Absence clause accepts, and every later idle step in p1 pays 1 again: the agent is paid for doing nothing, and the loop on p1 keeps paying. Staying in p3 pays 2 per step. The largest reward is 2, so p3 is the only accepting state, although p4, the goal reached after a lost key, also completes the task, since the key is only a preference.

FARM keeps the states and edges of the product, sets the final states from the hard goals and prices every edge itself (Step~4). The careful-key machine of Section~\ref{sec:run-step4} pays nothing for idle steps and has one final state, reached with or without the key.

\section{Step 4 · Post-process}
\label{sec:step4}
The fourth step is FARM's own and turns the product into the Reward Machine. It first labels the states of every clause automaton and, before FL-AT multiplies the automata, re-arms soft rules so that each new break is charged and finds the states in which a soft Absence can be folded. On the product it then folds those states and marks the edges that break the Absence, sends every failure of a hard clause to one well and sets the final states, merges the start state with its idle successor, prices every edge, merges the states that behave alike, writes the machine and refuses it if a loop pays (Figure~\ref{fig:step4}). Hard clauses decide which states are final and which edges go to the well; soft clauses never do, and act through the rewards, the re-arming and the fold.

\subsection{Clause states}
Every state of a clause DFA gets one of three labels. It is \textbf{satisfied} when the DFA accepts there: the trace so far meets the clause. It is \textbf{pending} when the DFA does not accept yet but can still reach an accepting state, and \textbf{violated} when it can no longer reach one. The labels are exact, computed by reachability on the DFA of each clause, and through the tuples of Step~3 every product state knows the label of each of its clauses.

\subsection{Re-arming soft rules}
\label{sec:recurrent}
An LTLf formula gives one verdict per trace, so a broken soft rule would be charged once per episode however often it is broken again. FARM \textbf{re-arms} a soft rule instead: the violated state of its DFA drops its own edges and takes those of the start, so the rule watches for the next break, and every new break is charged again. Only the four templates whose DFA has a violated state can break: Absence, Precedence, ChainResponse and NotCoExistence. The other four, Existence, ExistenceTwo, RespondedExistence and Response, have no violated state, but a soft one still moves progress while it is pending: a soft Response trigger can cost $-1$ at once and its answer pay the $+1$ back, and finishing with the clause still pending costs $-5$.

A soft Absence is \textbf{folded} as well: in the product, the states in which it is violated merge into the matching states in which it is satisfied, and the break becomes a mark on the edge. The clause then adds no state to the machine, and its break is a loop that pays $-1$ (Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}). Re-arming and folding are settings, both on by default; with re-arming off, a soft rule costs one charge per episode.

\subsection{Final states and the well}
A product state in which a hard clause is violated can never complete the task, so every edge into such a state goes to one shared \textbf{well}, a rejecting state that ends the episode. When the task has a hard goal, the edges into states from which no final state can be reached go to the well too. One well is enough, because the price of a break sits on the edge into it, so each edge can pay its own price.

A \textbf{final state} is a state in which every hard clause is satisfied, and only a task with a hard goal has one. The edges out of a final state are dropped, so a final state absorbs and ends the episode, and the saved machine merges all final states into one. Soft clauses never affect the final condition: a preference broken on the way still lets the task finish, at a cost. In the careful key the goal is the only hard clause, so the goal is final with or without the key (Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}). A hard clause that is only pending, such as a Response still waiting for its answer, blocks finishing but does not end the episode. States that the start cannot reach are pruned. In a task with a hard goal, if no reachable edge enters a final state, the clauses conflict and the build fails; a rule-only task compiles with no final state.

\subsection{Merging MONA's start state}
\label{sec:start-merge}
MONA gives each rule a ``no step yet'' start (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}), and the product inherits it as a start that no edge re-enters. Its \textbf{idle successor} is the state it moves to on a step in which no proposition holds. The idle successor becomes the start, and the old start is dropped, when three conditions hold: no edge re-enters the start, the idle successor is neither the start, the well nor a final state, and both states move to the same state on every input. Otherwise the machine is unchanged.

The merge makes the machine smaller. It is safe because no template of the catalogue is broken by a step in which nothing happens, so the start and its idle successor are priced alike. On 25 sample machines and 3,512 clause sets, the machines built with and without the merge had the same rewards, final states and wells.

\subsection{Reward assignment}
The rewards follow one principle: getting closer to finishing the task or a rule pays, and stepping back from it or getting a new thing to do costs. A new thing to do can come from the agent, which triggered a rule, or from the environment, which changed so that the instructions now ask for a reaction. Table~\ref{tab:rewards} gives every value.

A \textbf{stage} is one event still needed, and every state owes a number of stages $\Phi(u)$. With a final state, $\Phi(u)$ counts the fewest events still needed to finish plus, for each soft clause, a soft goal included, the longest run of events its own automaton may still need; without one, every clause counts its own events (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). A step from $u$ to $u'$ pays
\[ r_{\mathrm{progress}}(u,u') = \Phi(u) - \Phi(u'), \]
one unit for each stage it closes and minus one for each stage it adds. This is potential-based shaping \cite{ng1999policy} with the potential $-\Phi$ and no discount, so around any loop the progress adds up to zero. A final state owes nothing, so the finishing edge pays every stage still owed. Three exceptions apply: a step that breaks a soft rule pays no progress for that rule, an edge into a state from which no state with nothing pending can be reached pays no progress, and an edge into the well takes progress back (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).

Completion pays on top of the progress of the finishing step. Each new break of a re-armed soft rule is charged again (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}), so a repeated break is never free. A soft clause still pending when the run finishes is charged once, on the finishing edge, so a preference that was never met costs reward without blocking the end. A soft break costs a tenth of completion, so one preference weighs less than finishing the task. A hard break costs more than a soft break, and more again when the task has a soft clause (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).

\phantomsection\label{sec:open-promises}
The last three rewards of Table~\ref{tab:rewards} are options, off in the frozen pipeline and worth trying in an experiment. Two of them price the stages still open when an episode ends, on the edge into the well or on the last step when the environment ends the episode; one setting turns both on (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). They count each clause on its own, so they can differ from the progress: at the start of the sequence machine they find one hard stage where progress owes three. The \textbf{promise cost} charges $-0.1$ on every step out of a state that still owes a hard stage, by the same count, finishing edges included, and an edge into the well pays it once for every step of the step limit, so a build with the promise cost needs the step limit of the environment.

\subsection{The price of a hard break}
\label{sec:well-charge}
In a task without a soft clause, an edge into the well pays the flat price of Table~\ref{tab:rewards}.

With a soft clause in the task, a hard break must cost more than breaking soft rules at every step until the episode ends; otherwise an agent facing many soft breaks could prefer the well. The edge into the well then pays
\[ -\left(\max\!\left(10,\; N c\,\frac{1-\gamma^{T}}{1-\gamma}\right) + 1\right), \]
where $c$ is the price of a soft break ($1$), $N$ the number of soft rules that can break in one step (a setting, $1$ by default), $\gamma$ the discount ($0.99$) and $T$ the step limit of the environment. The fraction is the discounted number of steps in an episode of $T$ steps. With a step limit of 300 steps, the discount $0.99$, one soft rule per step and a soft-break price of $1$, the price is about $-96$ ($-96.096$): the discounted cost of breaking a soft rule at every one of the 300 steps, plus one. The price grows with the step limit and is never milder than $-11$. The order holds only if no more than $N$ soft rules can break in one step, and the compiler does not check $N$ against the task. This price is a setting, on by default, and applies when re-arming and the soft-break charge are on; a build that needs it fails without the step limit.

The edge into the well also takes progress back, so that progress made before a hard break does not make the break cheaper. A state is ahead of the start when it owes fewer stages. The edge takes one unit for each stage by which the state it leaves is ahead, and nothing when that state is not ahead. The soft-rule charges paid on the way stay. In the sequence machine (Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1}), the edge from u0 into the well pays $-10$, and the edge from u1, one stage ahead of the start, pays $-11$, so a run that picks up the key and then reaches the goal with the door closed totals $-10$, the same as a break at the first step.

\subsection{Pay order and checks}
A machine should pay its runs in this order, best first: \textbf{finished}, \textbf{started}, \textbf{not started}, \textbf{soft break}, \textbf{hard break}. Finished is the best run to the final state. Started is the best run with a return of zero or more to a state closer to the final state than the start, measured in the fewest events still needed to finish; when the optional end charge is on, it is included. Not started is a run in which nothing happens, soft break the cost of breaking soft rules and hard break the mildest edge into the well. Not started may tie with a soft break.

Three checks look at a built machine. The loop check runs on every build: it searches the machine for a loop whose rewards add up to more than zero, counting every step without discount, and refuses the machine if it finds one. The pay order is a report, written when FARM prepares a machine for training and the step limit of the environment is known: a failed order prints a warning and stops nothing, and a machine built alone with the \texttt{generate-rm} command gets no report. The plan check plays scripted plans on each task of the benchmark (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}); a recorded failure stops that machine from training, while a missing record or an unchecked result does not.

\subsection{Rule-only machines}
\label{sec:rule-only}
A task with no hard goal has no final state. Only the well or the environment ends the episode, and the compiler warns that the task has no goal. Progress then counts every clause on its own and aims at the states where no clause is pending. A rule that waits for an event, such as a Response, owes a stage once its trigger happens, whether the agent or the environment caused it: the trigger costs one unit per stage at once, and the reaction that the rule asks for pays it back. A trigger that is answered therefore totals 0, the same as no trigger. With no final state, the pay order skips the comparison of started and not started.

\section{Step 5 · The critic}
\label{sec:step5}
The fifth step gives a second language model, the \textbf{critic}, six inputs: the environment description, the task, the clauses, the compiled machine, the compiler clues and the warnings. The \textbf{compiler clues} give each rule in plain words, the hard clauses that the others already imply, and the events that every way to finish includes. The \textbf{warnings} flag a task with no goal and a soft clause that changes no reward. The critic accepts the result or rejects it with comments, and on a rejection the generator tries again (Figure~\ref{fig:step5}). The critic never edits the machine.

\subsection{A review step for the final version}
Only the choice of clauses varies from run to run, so one review at the end covers the whole pipeline. The critic compares the clauses with the task. Each clause must come from a sentence of the task, use the right template and argument order, carry the priority that its wording asks for and use the declared propositions. The critic rejects a clause whose event can never occur in the environment and a hard Existence that the other clauses already imply. It does not judge the topology of the machine, its number of states or the size of its rewards, which follow from the clauses, and it treats the warnings, such as a task with no goal, as valid output. Its verdict is either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or a list of changes that starts with \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED}.

\subsection{Fixing mistakes}
On a rejection, the generator receives the comments of the critic in its prompt and proposes new clauses, from which the compiler rebuilds the machine (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). One stored case shows the repair. The task ``Try to move the ball off the door before you take the key. Pick up the box in the other room.'' first became a hard Existence(\texttt{target\_picked\_up}) and a soft Precedence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}, \texttt{has\_door\_key}). The critic asked for a soft Existence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}) as well, ``so the preferred move is expressed'', and accepted the second answer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Existence(\texttt{target\_picked\_up}), hard: $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{target\_picked\_up}$;
    \item Existence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}), soft: $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{blocker\_moved}$;
    \item Precedence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}, \texttt{has\_door\_key}), soft:\[(\neg\texttt{has\_door\_key}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{blocker\_moved}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\texttt{has\_door\_key}\]
\end{itemize}
Its machine has three states and six transitions: the start u0, u2 after the ball is moved, and the final state u1, with no well, since the only hard clause is the goal. Moving the ball pays $+1$, and the box then pays $+11$; moving the ball and taking the box in one step pays $+12$. Taking the key before moving the ball breaks the soft order: it costs $-1$ and stays in u0. Taking the box without having moved the ball pays $+7$: $+1$ for each of the two stages the start owes, the box and the move, because the final state owes none, $+10$ for completion and $-5$ for the soft Existence still pending. In the step that also takes the key it pays $+6$, since the broken order costs $-1$.

\subsection{Different LLMs for generation and critique}
Generator and critic are configured separately, and the build refuses a critic that resolves to the same model as the generator, so the review does not come from the model that wrote the clauses. The critic also sees none of the earlier attempts. The frozen pipeline uses deepseek-v4.1-flash as generator and glm-5.3-flash as critic.

\subsection{Clauses and environment facts}
The critic judges the clauses against the task, not against the layout of one environment. It rejects a clause set that drops an order the task states, even when the environment already forces that order, and it never asks for an order the task does not state. As a result, a machine can hold edges that the environment never takes. The sequence machine (Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1}) has edges out of u0 on an open door without the key and on the goal before the door, while in DoorKey only the key opens the locked door. These edges never fire, so they pay nothing in training. Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations} discusses what the compiler cannot know about the environment.

\subsection{Things to keep in mind}

\paragraph{LLMs can hallucinate}
The critic can hallucinate as well as the generator \cite{huang2025survey}. The compiler builds the machine exactly from the chosen clauses and adds no step that they do not state, so a wrong machine always comes from a wrong clause set: the generator proposed it and the critic missed it. In one study at the high thinking level, 3 of 46 runs gave a wrong machine that the critic accepted. What the compiler guarantees, the structure of the machine and the loop check, holds for any clause set; whether the clause set says what the person meant rests on the two models.

\paragraph{Number of attempts}
The generator has three attempts by default, configurable from one to ten, and the benchmark gives both FARM and ARM-FM ten (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). Every refused candidate uses one attempt. A candidate is refused when the critic rejects it, when the generator's answer is invalid, or when the compiler cannot build it: conflicting clauses, a loop that pays, or an automaton without exactly one transition per input. A passing failure of a model provider uses no attempt, and a permanent one ends the run (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

\paragraph{Thinking level of the models}
One thinking level, none, low, medium, high or max, applies to both models. The default, and the level of the frozen pipeline, is max. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} shows how the share of correct machines changes with the level.

\texttt{STATES} lists every state, and \texttt{INITIAL\_STATE} names the start. \texttt{FINAL\_STATES} names the final state, at most one, and stays empty when the task has no hard goal. \texttt{REJECTING\_\allowbreak STATES} names the well and is left out when there is none. \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD} is the reward of a step that matches no transition: the machine stays in its state and pays 0. \texttt{TRANSITION\_\allowbreak FUNCTION} gives one line per transition, \texttt{(source, event) -> destination}, where the event combines propositions with \texttt{!} for ``not'' and \texttt{\&} for ``and''. \texttt{REWARD\_FUNCTION} gives the reward of each transition, \texttt{(source, event, destination) -> reward}, and leaves out the transitions that pay 0.

In the listing, u0 is the start, u1 holds the key, u3 holds the key with the door open, u4 is the final state and u2 the well. Each stage closed pays $+1$ and the goal adds $+10$, so doing all three steps at once pays $+13$. Every way to break the order costs $-10$, or $-11$ from u1 (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).

A machine ends an episode when it enters its final state or the well, both absorbing. Otherwise the environment's own end or its time limit ends the episode. By default the agent is paid the reward of the machine alone, plus a flat time-limit penalty (default $-5$) on the last step of an episode that times out; the end-charge option of Table~\ref{tab:rewards} replaces that flat charge. A setting adds the reward of the environment to it (\texttt{both}) or trains on the environment alone, with no machine (\texttt{env}). Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} lists how the format differs from that of ARM-FM.

\section{Details}

\subsection{Contributions}
FARM adds the following to the tools it builds on, IBM nl2ltl, FL-AT and MONA, and to the generation step of ARM-FM.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A prompt that offers all eight Declare templates, with the meaning, kind and formula of each, and serves any environment, because the propositions and their meaning come from the environment description (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item A restricted answer: each clause is a template, its propositions, a priority and one plain sentence, and only the first three reach the compiler, so tasks of one schema should get machines of one shape (Sections~\ref{sec:step1} and~\ref{sec:task-schemas}).
    \item A priority for each clause, hard or soft, set by the preference cues of the task, which IBM's templates do not have (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item A fork of FL-AT that drops MONA's first state only when it is redundant and returns the state of every clause in each product state (Sections~\ref{sec:step2} and~\ref{sec:step3}).
    \item Final states and one well set by the hard clauses, in place of the accepting states and the rewards of FL-AT (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item Rewards that count the stages still owed: a step closer pays, a new thing to do costs, and reaching the final state pays $+10$ (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item Re-armed soft rules, so that every new break is charged, and folded soft Absence clauses, which add no state to the machine (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
    \item A price for a hard break that, with a soft clause in the task, is higher than the cost of breaking soft rules at every step up to the step limit, and a take-back of progress on the edge into the well (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).
    \item A merge of MONA's start state with its idle successor, and a minimisation of the priced machine (Sections~\ref{sec:start-merge} and~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item A loop check that refuses any machine with a loop whose rewards add up to more than zero (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item A pay order for the runs of a machine, from finished to hard break, reported when FARM prepares a machine for training (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item A critic that reviews the clauses, not the machine text, on a model that must differ from the generator, and that never edits the machine (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
    \item Compiler clues for the critic: each rule in plain words, the hard clauses that the others imply, and the events that every way to finish includes (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
    \item An extended machine format with explicit final and rejecting states and one event expression per transition (Section~\ref{sec:output-format}).
\end{itemize}

The labelling functions and the state descriptions that turn the machine into a LARM follow ARM-FM; Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} describes the tagger that writes one description per state.

\chapter{Limitations}
\label{chap:limitations}

FARM's compiler knows the task only through the clauses the generator chooses and the world not at all; the generator and the critic know the world only through the environment description. Each limit below is something the method does not do or does not guarantee today, given with a case, so that a person knows what to avoid when writing a task or describing an environment. Table~\ref{tab:lim-summary} says for each one whether later work could remove it.

\section{States the world rules out}
\label{sec:lim-impossible}
The compiler prunes the states that no path from the start reaches (Section~\ref{sec:step4}), but it knows nothing of the world, so it keeps the states and transitions that only the world rules out. This follows from a choice of the generator prompt: an order that the task states stays in the machine even when the world already forces it, so the machine follows the instruction and not one layout of the world (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). The sequence machine of Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1} shows the result. It has transitions out of u0 on an open door without the key and on the goal before the door, while in DoorKey only the key opens the locked door (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). Such a transition never fires, and a state that only such transitions enter is never visited. These states and transitions make the machine bigger and harder to read, and they do not make training worse.

FARM does not cut them, because a cut needs facts about the world that are certain. The constraints an environment description states rest on reading the code of the world and on random episodes, which can show a constraint false but never prove it, and a wrong cut would fail silently: if the world did take a cut transition, the machine would have no edge for it, so it would stay in its state and pay nothing. A step that cut states from those constraints was built and removed for this reason.

\section{Machines grow with the clauses}
\label{sec:lim-size}
A FARM machine is the product of one automaton per clause, so it can grow exponentially with the number of clauses: on the shop tasks, each added rule that waits for an answer nearly doubles the states (Table~\ref{tab:res-shop}). On the trained tasks where both machine arms built a machine, FARM's machines are larger than ARM-FM's (Table~\ref{tab:res-size}), and the largest machine, that of a shop task with six hard rules, has more than ten thousand transitions (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). A machine of that size still follows its clauses exactly, but it is hard for a person to read. Section~\ref{sec:fw-size} takes it up.

\section{A rule alone does not make the agent act}
\label{sec:lim-rules}
Of the eight templates, only Existence and ExistenceTwo ask for something to happen. The other six are rules, which hold while nothing happens (Section~\ref{sec:step1}): a Response, for instance, holds when its trigger never comes. A task made of rules alone is therefore kept by an agent that does nothing, unless the world sets the rules off by itself; apart from such a world, only a goal makes the agent act. This is intended, since a rule says what must not happen or how to answer an event, not that the agent must begin anything. It limits what such a task can test. The benchmark leaves out of training the tasks with no goal that an agent doing nothing passes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}), and it adds worlds that keep setting the rules off, the zombie lanes and the shop. A person who wants the agent to act has to state a goal.

\section{Ambiguous wording}
\label{sec:lim-ambiguous}
Everyday language is ambiguous: a sentence can read two ways, and the generator may take either. The task \textit{Try not to let zombies through both the top lane and the side lanes} can mean ``not the two together'', a soft NotCoExistence(\texttt{top\_\allowbreak breach}, \texttt{side\_\allowbreak breach}), $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{top\_breach} \rightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{side\_breach}$, or ``neither one nor the other'', a soft Absence(\texttt{top\_\allowbreak breach}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{top\_breach}$, and a soft Absence(\texttt{side\_\allowbreak breach}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{side\_breach}$. FARM's generator takes the second reading, which the benchmark accepts for this task. Its machine has a single state: a step with a breach in one kind of lane costs 1, and a step with breaches in both costs 2 (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}).

The compiler builds the reading the generator takes, so the wording decides the machine. In the final run, the few clause sets that differed from the first accepted answer of their task were, all but one, judged afterwards to be fair readings of their wording (Section~\ref{sec:res-clauses}); they are cases, not a measure of how often the generator errs. The prompt tells the generator to refuse an ambiguous task, but its answer has no way to refuse (Section~\ref{sec:lim-anomalies}). The repository's guide to task wording (\texttt{docs/LTLF\_TASK\_LANGUAGE.md}) lists the readings found and how to write each one; its rewrites were not tested.

\section{Anomalies the model does not detect or cannot report}
\label{sec:lim-anomalies}
A task can hold an anomaly: it may ask for something the world cannot do, need a fact the environment does not declare, or hold clauses that contradict each other. The prompt tells the generator to refuse a task that needs an undeclared fact, but its answer is a list of one to 25 clauses with no field for a refusal. An answer that refuses is invalid, and an invalid answer uses up an attempt, as a rejected candidate does (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). The three anomalies end differently.

\paragraph{A task that cannot be done.} FARM checks only whether single events can occur. The critic rejects a clause whose event can never occur, which sends the generator back for new clauses, and when every attempt is refused the task fails with no machine (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). A task whose events can each occur, but not in the way it asks, still gets a machine. On the benchmark, the scripted plan that solves each task shows that the task can be done; a task outside the benchmark has no such plan.

\paragraph{A fact the environment does not declare.} The generator may use only the propositions the environment declares. An answer that names another is rejected, uses up an attempt and is retried, and after the last attempt the task fails with no machine. When a task needs a fact the environment lacks, the generator can also answer with the nearest declared facts, although the prompt forbids leaving out part of a task, and nothing in its answer says so. The shop has no event for putting food down. Its boil task ends \textit{Whenever the pot boils over, put down what you hold next to the stove, turn the stove down, and pick your item up again}, and the generator turns that sentence into one hard Response(\texttt{pot\_boiling}, \texttt{stove\_fixed}), $\mathrm{G}\,(\texttt{pot\_boiling} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,\texttt{stove\_fixed})$, which leaves out the put-down and the pick-up, as the benchmark's expected answer does (Table~\ref{tab:res-shop}). On one rewording, ``pick the load back up'' became a seventh clause, Response(\texttt{stove\_fixed}, \texttt{holding\_food}), $\mathrm{G}\,(\texttt{stove\_fixed} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,\texttt{holding\_food})$, which asks for food in hand after every stove fix, whether or not the agent carried any before. The plan check refused that machine, the one FARM did not build (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}).

\paragraph{Clauses that contradict each other.} When the task has a hard goal and its hard clauses leave no way to reach it, the compiler stops with a conflicting-clauses error, the candidate is refused, and the generator tries again (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). The check needs a final state, so it applies only to tasks with a hard goal. A task made of rules alone can always be kept, as far as the compiler sees, by doing nothing, so it builds; whether the world lets the agent keep all its rules is what the compiler cannot know. Soft clauses that conflict also build, and each break costs its price.

\section{Conditions the templates cannot state}
\label{sec:lim-conditions}
A template argument is always one declared fact, never a negated condition or a combination of facts. The clauses of a task all hold together: the generator cannot join them with ``or'', mark one as an optional branch, or nest conditions inside an argument. The prompt tells it to refuse a task that needs them, and how such a task fares at run time was not tested. Where a task needs such a condition, the environment has to declare a fact that carries it. The shop's quiet rule shows this: \textit{Never give a client their food while the siren is ringing} joins two facts, so the shop declares one event for it, \texttt{served\_while\_ringing}, and the rule becomes a hard Absence(\texttt{served\_while\_ringing}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{served\_while\_ringing}$ (Table~\ref{tab:res-shop}).

\section{Soft rules in the zombie lanes}
\label{sec:lim-lanes}
In the zombie lanes the world sets the rules off by itself: zombies walk along three lanes, the agent kills one by moving onto it, and the top lane is the one to protect (Section~\ref{sec:res-react}). Where the top lane is a hard rule, FARM's agents learn the task and agents trained on the world's own reward, one point per kill, do not. On the lane tasks with only soft rules, FARM's agents break fewer of the rules than an agent that does nothing, so they do try to keep them, but the agents trained on the world's reward break fewer still and keep the top lane nearly clean.

FARM's machine charges a breach, which comes up to 27 steps after the move that let the zombie pass, and it charges both kinds of lane alike, while the world pays the kill itself. That this explains the gap is likely and not tested. A FARM machine cannot copy the world's reward: no template asks for every kill, and a machine that paid every kill, however many, would hold a loop that pays, which the build check refuses (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). A count of kills would pay each one up to a fixed number with no such loop (Section~\ref{sec:fw-counting}). These results count breaches and kills over whole episodes; no run measures how many steps an agent takes to answer an event.

\section{Approximations in the rewards}
\label{sec:lim-rewards}

Three small approximations remain where the machine's rewards meet the learner, and no run measures what they change. The learner treats the step limit as if the episode could go on, while FARM prices a hard break from that limit, so a hard break, meant to cost more than breaking a soft rule at every step, can cost slightly less in the learner's eyes: about $-96$ against $-100$ in the example of Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}. Progress pays without the learner's discount, so the guarantee of potential-based shaping \cite{ng1999policy} holds only approximately, and a loop repeated for ever could gain at most about 1, against 10 for finishing. The warning about a soft clause that changes no reward is a shortcut rather than an exact comparison, so it can miss such a clause or flag one that does pay; it only informs the critic (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).

\chapter{Evaluation methodology}
\label{chap:evaluation}
The evaluation tests two claims: FARM builds a right Reward Machine from a plain-language task, and an agent trained on that machine learns the task. Two tests check them on one benchmark of tasks. The generation test builds machines and checks them without training an agent (Section~\ref{sec:eval-machines}). The training test trains agents on the machines and judges what the agents do (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learning}). The final run is one pass of both tests, on the version of FARM frozen before it. Every result belongs to one test and one set of cards, and numbers from different sets are never added or averaged.

Counts, such as the rows learned out of 60 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}), are given as $x$ of $n$ ($p$\%) and, where the record gives one, with a 95\% Wilson score interval. A training measure, such as judged success, is the mean over the seeds of a row, three on every row but the two pilot rows, given with a 95\% Student $t$ interval over those seeds. With so few seeds this interval is wide.

\section{Empirical evaluation in a real environment}
\label{sec:eval-learning}

\subsection{The three arms}
\label{sec:eval-arms}
Three arms train the same learner and are scored on the same cards. FARM trains on the reward of the Reward Machine that FARM builds. ARM-FM trains on the reward of a machine built by the ARM-FM method. The third arm, training with no Reward Machine (``no RM'' in tables and figures), trains on the world's own reward. FARM and ARM-FM are the machine arms.

The ARM-FM arm is a reconstruction, built without access to official ARM-FM code. It follows the appendices of the paper and, for the MiniGrid tasks, the prompts and machines of the first author's public repository \cite{armfm_code}. Parts that neither source documents, such as the framing of later generator and critic rounds, are reconstructed. The rebuilt baseline may therefore differ from the original, and the results compare FARM with this reconstruction.

FARM changes only how the machine is built. In FARM the generator model chooses clauses and the compiler builds the machine from them (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}); in ARM-FM the generator writes the machine as text. In both arms the critic model accepts or rejects the machine. A second generator and critic stage then writes the labelling function, which reads the world and returns the propositions the machine follows, and every machine state gets a plain-language description. Both arms use the same models: deepseek-v4.1-flash as generator and glm-5.3-flash as critic.

Each machine arm has up to 10 attempts to build each machine, and every new candidate machine uses one attempt. A machine is built when a candidate passes every check of its arm and then the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:eval-plan-check}). A machine that runs out of attempts or fails the plan check is not built.

\subsection{The benchmark cards}
\label{sec:eval-cards}
The benchmark is a set of cards. A card is one test case: a task written in plain language, together with the answers accepted for it. The benchmark needs them because a machine can only be called right or wrong against stated answers, and an agent can only be judged against stated rules. Each answer is a list of clauses: the clauses the task is expected to give, and other lists that are also right. A card also names the world the task runs in and holds rewordings of the task, a flag that says whether the task has a goal, and scripted plans: one that solves the task, wrong ones that each break a named rule, and an idle one that only waits. A scripted plan is a program of commands written in advance for the card's world, such as going to the key or picking it up. A command may find an object or call a fixed scripted policy of the world, so the actions a plan takes depend on the layout and on world events. Table~\ref{tab:eval-card} shows one card of the templates suite and the machine FARM builds for it.

\subsection{When a card counts as learned}
\label{sec:eval-learned}
After training, each seed plays judged episodes in which the agent takes its most likely action at every step. A judge reads each judged episode through the benchmark's reference labelling, written with each world and separate from both machine arms, and checks the card's expected clauses on the trace. The judge is the same for every arm and never uses an arm's labelling function or machine, so a wrong machine cannot turn a failed episode into a pass.

A judged episode succeeds when every hard rule of the card holds, the goal included when the card has one. It fails, whatever the rules say, when the arm's machine, not the world, ends it in a final state within $\max(1, \lceil 0.1L \rceil)$ actions, where $L$ is the median length of the solving plan on the card's layouts, or 0 when there is none: the machine has then ended the episode before the agent could act on the task. Soft rules never decide success: every break of a soft rule is counted, each time it happens, in the soft breaks per episode.

The judged success of a seed is the share of its judged episodes that succeed, and the seed passes at 0.8 or more. A card counts as learned for an arm when at least two seeds pass: two of three, or both seeds of a pilot card, which trains only two.

The four shop cards with a rule about serving orders (\texttt{shop-serve}, \texttt{shop-full}, \texttt{shop-quiet-first} and \texttt{shop-boil}) also need 7.5 or more orders served per judged episode, on average, for a seed to pass (the served bar); the siren and pot cards have no serving rule and no such bar.

A card with no goal and only soft rules needs another test, because with no hard rule every judged episode succeeds. Its seeds are compared with an idle agent that only waits, on the same layouts. A soft rule left unmet is one still waiting to be met when the episode ends. A seed passes when, per episode, its soft breaks and its soft rules left unmet are both no higher than the idle agent's, at least one of them is lower, and the labels the judge reads change more often than the idle agent's, which shows that the agent acts.

A row whose machine was not built counts as not learned for that arm. ARM-FM was not trained on the shop, and its shop rows count as not learned under this rule.

Besides the learned count, each row reports its mean judged success, its soft breaks per episode and, on the shop, its orders served per episode, each averaged over the seeds. Learned counts are given over the 60 rows and over subsets of them, in this order: ARM-FM's own kind of task against the 49 new kinds; the goal flag, outside the shop and the step-by-step cards; three kinds of card by what the task asks, which are the step-by-step cards, cards with hard rules other than order and no soft rule, and cards with at least one soft rule; and each suite.

\section{Qualitative evaluation of the Reward Machines}
\label{sec:eval-machines}
The generation test builds and checks machines without training an agent. Each machine arm builds one machine for every card's own wording, which is its task text, and one for each of its rewordings, except on cards that reuse another card's machine. The FARM machines of this test come from the final version of FARM, frozen before the final run.

\subsection{Built machines and rewordings}
\label{sec:eval-built}
The model checks that decide whether a machine is built (Section~\ref{sec:eval-arms}) run in rounds, in the same way in both machine arms: the generator proposes a candidate, the critic accepts or rejects it, and a rejection, or a candidate that fails the arm's own checks, starts a new round. In the machine stage each round uses one of the 10 attempts, and the stage that writes the labelling function has its own limit of three rounds. A machine of either arm goes through both stages, so a machine accepted at the first round of each needs two rounds.

Every FARM machine also goes through a model review before it may train: Claude Opus 5.5 reads one sheet per machine, with its clauses beside the card's accepted answers, the results of the build checks and of the plan check, and, for every state, its role, its transitions, the clauses still owed there and its description. The reviewer's verdict on each machine is ok, unsure or anomaly. A machine judged unsure or anomaly is read by hand, and it does not train until that reading settles the doubt.

FARM's chosen clauses are also scored against the card's accepted answers, and a FARM machine is right when its clauses match one of them, exactly or by an equivalent formula. ARM-FM stores no clauses, so its machines are not scored this way. The thinking-level study repeats FARM's generation at each thinking level of its models. It runs 46 cases picked around past mistakes of the generator, three runs each, and counts the runs whose final clauses match an accepted answer of the case's card. On a case that asks for steps in order it is stricter: clauses that also make an earlier step a goal of its own pass the card but are not counted as right.

The rewordings test whether the same task, said in other words, gives the same machine. A card is built on every wording when its arm builds a machine for each wording, the plan check included. Over the cards with at least two built wordings, the machines of a card are compared: the same machine in every built wording (identical text), machines of the same size that are not identical (the same number of states and the same number of transitions), or different machines.

FARM's prompts were tuned on the same cards; the versions and their scores are in Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts}.

\subsection{From traces to rewards}
\label{sec:eval-plan-check}
The plan check runs the scripted plans of a card through a machine and reads what each plan pays. Each plan plays in the card's world: the arm's own labelling function reads every step, the machine follows the labels, and the run stops at the first final state or at the well. No language model takes part. A machine passes when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the solving plan does not enter the well and, when the machine has final states, ends in one;
    \item on a machine with no final state, an idle plan that keeps every rule does not enter the well either, and both it and the solving plan end with nothing owed;
    \item no plan that breaks a hard rule reaches a final state, and on a machine with no final state a wrong or idle plan that breaks a hard rule for good ends in the well;
    \item a plan that breaks only soft rules receives a negative reward before it stops, or ends in the same way as the solving plan on its layout and is paid less in total, summed plainly;
    \item on a machine with no final state, every wrong plan pays less than the solving plan, both summed plainly and discounted.
\end{itemize}
A machine that ends before the agent acts fails every plan. The check needs no clauses, so it applies to both machine arms. A machine whose build is marked failed trains in neither arm. FARM also refuses to train a machine whose plan-check record marks it failed; a missing record, or a check that tested nothing, does not by itself stop training.

\subsection{Loops that pay}
\label{sec:eval-loops}
A loop pays when an agent can go round a cycle of transitions and collect a positive total, gaining reward without doing the task. FARM's build check refuses any machine with a loop whose pay adds up to more than zero, found by a Bellman--Ford search over the undiscounted rewards (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). For the comparison, a search of the same kind runs on every built machine of both arms, restricted to the states reachable from the initial state without passing a final state. A loop it finds is searched again with the transitions removed that only a world event can take, such as a box that falls by itself; the world caps how often such events happen in an episode.

A second measure looks at the trained agents. The machine return of an episode is the plain sum of the rewards the arm's own machine pays over it, with any end charge or time-limit penalty the run turns on; it leaves out the world's reward, even on rows that train on both. The machine return of each judged episode is compared with the largest plain sum that a solving plan of the card earns on the same machine in the plan check (the best scripted plan of the card). An episode that earned more would point to pay that the scripted plans do not reach. The comparison covers scripted plans only, and the scripted plan is not proven to be the best possible.

\subsection{Shape, number of states and useless states}
\label{sec:eval-size}
The size of a machine is its number of states, those on its \texttt{STATES} line, and its number of transitions, the entries of its \texttt{TRANSITION\_\allowbreak FUNCTION} (Section~\ref{sec:output-format}). The sizes of the two machine arms are compared on the trained rows where both built a machine, counting each distinct pair of machines once, and are summed up by their median: the middle value once the sizes are sorted. Useless states are left to Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}.

\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the final run: first the Reward Machines that FARM and the rebuilt ARM-FM build from the same cards, then what agents learn with them, over all cards, in the zombie lanes and in the shop, and last the smaller studies. Card, rewording, plan check, judged episode and learned keep the meaning Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} gives them, and a card an arm could not train counts as not learned.

\section{Comparison between ARM-FM and FARM}
\label{sec:res-comparison}

Both arms are asked for the same 266 machines, 111 of them for a card's own wording and the rest for its rewordings. FARM builds its machines on the frozen pipeline with one setting: generator \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} and critic \texttt{glm-5.3-flash}, at thinking level max.

\subsection{Machines built with no hand edit}
\label{sec:res-built}

Each machine goes through two stages, each a generator followed by a critic: one writes the machine and one its labelling function. No machine was edited by hand: 265 of the 266 asked were built (99.6\%, 95\% Wilson interval 97.9\% to 99.9\%). Of these, 262 passed every model check at the first round, and 3 needed a second round after one automatic rejection by a critic, 1 for the machine and 2 for its labelling function. Every card's own wording is built; the one machine not built is a rewording of a shop card, which passes both stages and fails the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}).

The critic reads each machine together with plain-language clues from the compiler: each rule in plain words, the hard clauses the others already imply, and the events that every way to finish includes. With them a critic model can reject a wrong machine. Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts} measures what the clues change.

The rebuilt ARM-FM builds 152 of the 266 (57\%, 95\% Wilson interval 51\% to 63\%): 132 at the first try and 20 after further attempts, 19 of them after a rejection by its critic and 1 after an invalid first output. The other 114 pass both stages and fail the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:res-armfm-machines}). FARM's build check works on clauses; ARM-FM stores none, so its machine counts as built when it passes the plan check (Section~\ref{sec:eval-plan-check}). Figure~\ref{fig:res-built} sets the two counts side by side.

Counted as Section~\ref{sec:eval-built} counts them, a built FARM machine takes 2.01 rounds on average, 1.004 for the machine and 1.008 for its labelling function, against the least possible of two, and no stage takes more than two rounds. A built ARM-FM machine takes 2.16, 1.151 and 1.007, and its machine stage takes up to four. The frozen FARM run has no provider retries.

\subsection{FARM's machines}
\label{sec:res-farm-machines}

A FARM machine is the product of the automata of the clauses the generator chooses, so it keeps hard and soft rules apart. It pays whole units: 1 for each step of progress and 10 more for the finish. Breaking a hard rule enters the well, which pays $-10$, takes back the progress already paid and ends the episode; each soft break costs 1 and the episode goes on.

The one machine FARM did not build is a rewording of the shop's boil card (\texttt{shop-boil\textasciitilde p2}), whose last sentence reads \textit{Each time the pot boils over, put your load down beside the stove, turn the stove down and pick the load back up.} From ``pick the load back up'' the generator added a seventh clause, Response(\texttt{stove\_fixed}, \texttt{holding\_food}). With it, on two of the check's layouts the plan that serves the clients but never turns the siren off does not pay less than the solving plan on both counts. On one, both plans pay $-1$ summed plainly, though the wrong plan pays less when discounted. On the other, the wrong plan pays less summed plainly, $-2$ against $0$, but more when discounted. On a machine with no final state the plan check needs every wrong plan to pay less on both counts (Section~\ref{sec:eval-plan-check}), so it refuses the machine. The shop world has no event for putting food down, so the pick-up the wording asks for has no exact rule.

Other facts about FARM's machines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The largest is the boil card's, with 65 states and 10,662 transitions from six hard clauses (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). The smallest have one state: the zombie-lane cards whose only rules are two soft Absence clauses, where the machine charges 1 for a step with a breach in one kind of lane and 2 for a step with both.
    \item On the lane card \textit{Try not to let zombies through both the top lane and the side lanes}, the generator reads ``both'' as ``neither'', a reading the card accepts, so its machine is the same as that of the card that asks to keep every lane clear.
    \item A machine can end in a final state on a card marked as having no goal: for \textit{Kill at least two zombies.} the card expects a hard ExistenceTwo, so FARM's machine has a final state.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Which clauses FARM chooses}
\label{sec:res-clauses}

The compiler builds exactly the clauses the generator chooses, so a FARM machine is as right as that choice. At the frozen setting, 9 of FARM's candidates, the clause sets the generator proposes for one wording, match no answer their card listed before the final run. Eight were accepted as fair readings of the task after their outputs had been read, and the ninth, the shop rewording above, was never built. The eight accepted differ in three ways: four add a soft ``at some point'' rule beside a soft order, two read ``not both'' as ``neither'' or as a ban, and two add a hard rule that the wording suggests. Because those eight were accepted after the fact, the nine are listed as cases and give no accuracy rate (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).

A machine built from a later-listed answer can still teach the task. A study of clause choice checks FARM's final-run machines on 15 cards that accept more than one answer. Nine are among the 60 rows trained (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}), and on each the trained machine matches a listed answer, on 6 of them a later-listed one. FARM's agents learn 4 of these 6: UnlockPickup, its pilot copy, KeyCorridor and a soft not-both card with no goal. They do not learn the Blocked box card of Table~\ref{tab:res-ex-machines} and a soft-order card on the same level.
On the 3 rows whose machine matches the first-listed answer, they learn 1 (95\% Wilson interval 6\% to 79\%, against 30\% to 90\% for 4 of 6): too few rows to compare. The gap follows the level, not the order of the answers. The 4 rows not learned, two with each kind of answer, are all on the BlockedUnlockPickup level and among the seven rows of Section~\ref{sec:res-learning} that only ARM-FM learns, whose tasks skip steps their level needs; the 5 rows on other levels are all learned. The soft not-both card counts as learned by the rule for cards with only soft rules (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). Six of the 15 cards, four of them trained, received an answer only after their machines had been read, so this part of the check is not independent. The other 51 trained rows are outside the study.

How the generator chooses among the accepted answers is in Appendix~\ref{app:res-choice}. Right runs of one wording almost always choose the same answer, and ordered tasks take the chain form the prompt teaches: the Existence of the last step plus one Precedence for each pair of steps in a row. The first-listed answer is chosen in more than half of the machines, with an interval above one half, only once the six added answers are counted. On not-both and order cards with rewordings the answer changes between wordings, each wording run once.

\subsubsection{Loops that pay}
\label{sec:res-loops}

FARM's build check refuses any machine with a loop that pays, a cycle whose rewards add up to more than zero (Section~\ref{sec:eval-loops}), so no FARM machine has one (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}). ARM-FM has no such check. Run over its machines, the same check finds a loop that pays in 24 of the 152 built ARM-FM machines and in none of FARM's 265. Each of the 24 loops needs a world event, a box falling or a siren ringing, and the world allows at most two of each per episode; with the world-event edges removed, none of the 24 loops pays. ARM-FM's Box carry machine is one of them (Table~\ref{tab:res-ex-machines}). In the final run no trained agent used one: three of the 24 machines trained, all on box-carry cards, and their judged return of 1.1 to 1.2 per episode is what one or two falls of the box pay.

In all trained runs of FARM and ARM-FM, no judged episode earned more on its machine than the best scripted plan of its card (run counts in Appendix~\ref{app:res-counts}). This compares the agents with scripted plans only. The scripted plan is not proven best, so the result is evidence, not a proof.

During development, before the pipeline was frozen, agents trained on ARM-FM machines of four tasks farmed a loop (repository entry \texttt{docs/paper/dev-reward-farming/}). On two of the tasks the agents picked the key up and dropped it again 206 to 306 times per episode and never opened the door; on the other two they looped until the episode's step limit. The machines those agents trained on were earlier versions, which sent a key loss back to an earlier state, so taking the key paid again. Three of the four tasks have a frozen ARM-FM machine, without such a loop and not among the 24 above; on the fourth, its machine was not built. The risk is a possibility these machines leave open, which the final run does not show.

\section{The zombie lanes: reacting to events}
\label{sec:res-react}

A rule that answers an event, such as picking a dropped box up again, holds while the event never happens, so an agent that does nothing passes it. In the forcing worlds of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} the world sets such rules off again and again. The main one is the zombie lanes: a field of 9 by 9 cells crossed by three lanes, of which the top lane is the one to protect. Zombies enter at the right end of a random lane, with odds that rise from 4\% to 17\% per step, and walk one cell left every three steps. The agent starts on the middle lane and kills a zombie by moving onto it, and plants grow off the lanes. An episode lasts 300 steps, and the world pays 1 for each zombie killed. An idle agent lets 26.2 zombies through per episode, 8.7 in the top lane and 17.5 in the side lanes. Table~\ref{tab:res-lanes} gives the seven lane cards.

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This thesis set out to build Reward Machines from tasks stated in plain language, with no machine edited by hand, and to find a curriculum for the shop. FARM takes up the first by changing one step of ARM-FM: a language model writes the task as clauses from a fixed catalogue of templates, each hard or soft, and fixed tools compile the clauses into the machine and price its transitions. The machine follows the chosen clauses by construction; whether the clauses say what the person meant rests on the generator and its critic.

The first objective is met. No machine was edited by hand: FARM builds 265 of the 266 machines asked (99.6\%, Section~\ref{sec:res-built}). Agents trained on its machines learn 43 of the 60 training cases (72\%, 95\% Wilson interval 59\% to 81\%), against 29 (48\%, 36\% to 61\%) with the machines of the rebuilt ARM-FM and 23 (38\%, 27\% to 51\%) with no Reward Machine, on the same 60 cases (a case whose machine was not built counts as not learned for that arm; Section~\ref{sec:res-learning}). The claim is about cases learned, not about how fast they are learned.

% PICK H2H: from here to END PICK H2H, cut as one block.
FARM still needs tuning. Its lead over ARM-FM comes mostly from cases on which ARM-FM builds no machine: of the 21 training cases that only FARM's agents learn, ARM-FM's machine was not built on 18. On 7 cases only ARM-FM's agents learn (Table~\ref{tab:res-h2h}). All 7 sit on the BlockedUnlockPickup level, ask for the box with one rule beside it, and name one or two of the three steps the level needs before the box, never all three. ARM-FM's machine pays for each of those steps, asked for or not. FARM's machine keeps to the task, as it is built to, and leaves little reward before the box. The likely reason for the gap, not tested, is that ARM-FM's pay for the level's steps guides the agent through the level. Where a task on the same level names every step, FARM's agents learn it (Section~\ref{sec:res-learning}).
% END PICK H2H

Rewording a task seldom changes FARM's machine, and it changes ARM-FM's more often (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}). The rewordings were built and not trained, so this holds for the machines and says nothing about learning. The compiler builds exactly the clauses chosen, so a machine is as right as that choice, and the model can still choose clauses that do not fit the task. On one rewording of a shop task the generator added a rule, and the plan check refused the machine (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}); where the wording is ambiguous, the reading the generator takes decides the machine (Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}).

% PICK SHOP: framing to confirm with the author
Not every task is learned. In the shop no trained agent serves an order, whether it trains on FARM's machine, on FARM's machine and the world's reward together, or on the world's reward alone; ARM-FM's shop machines start in a final state, so ARM-FM trains none of the shop tasks (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). The world allows serving, since a scripted plan serves orders on every shop task with a serving rule. Whether an agent learns to serve depends on the world, the learner and how the machine pays each order, taken together. On FARM's shop machines an order nets nothing in the frozen pipeline, and training on the world's reward, which pays each order served, serves none either. The results do not separate these causes.

\chapter{Future work}
\label{chap:future-work}

Two studies of the Results chapter still wait for data: a curriculum for the shop (Section~\ref{sec:res-curriculum}) and the scoring of other language models on generation alone (Section~\ref{sec:res-models}). The directions below go beyond this thesis, each with what is missing today, why it matters and a first step.

\section{Smaller machines}
\label{sec:fw-size}
A FARM machine is the product of one automaton per clause, minimised after the product is built (Section~\ref{sec:step4}), and it grows with the number of clauses (Section~\ref{sec:lim-size}). On the shop's boil task each of the six hard rules is either waiting for its answer or not, so the machine holds one state for each combination of the six, plus the well. That gives 65 states and 10,662 transitions (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}), and since the machine is already minimised, none of them can be merged without changing what it pays. A machine of that size still follows its clauses, but a person can hardly read it. No way of making machines smaller is proposed here. The first step is to measure how the size grows with the number and the kind of rules, on tasks built for that purpose.

\section{A model that writes LTLf}
\label{sec:fw-ltlf}
The generator picks clauses from eight templates whose arguments are single declared facts, so a task can say no more than those templates state (Section~\ref{sec:lim-conditions}). A generator that writes the LTLf formulas itself could state negated conditions, alternatives and nested conditions. It would give up what the fixed catalogue gives: a known meaning, automaton and price for every clause. The rest of the pipeline should hold, but that needs testing. A first step is to ask such a model for formulas on tasks the catalogue cannot state and pass them through the same checks.

\section{A way to refuse}
\label{sec:fw-refusal}
The generator's answer is a list of clauses with no way to refuse, so a task it should refuse ends in failed attempts or in clauses that come close and do not say what they leave out (Section~\ref{sec:lim-anomalies}). A field for a refusal, with its reason, would let the model report a task the world cannot do, a fact the environment does not declare or a wording it cannot settle, and a person could then fix the task or the environment description. The first step is to add the field and test it on tasks built to be refused.

\section{Counting}
\label{sec:fw-counting}
The templates count only to two: ExistenceTwo asks for an event at least twice (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). ``At least $n$ times'' needs an LTLf formula that grows with $n$, and an unbounded count, such as one kill for each zombie that enters, cannot be stated in LTLf. A count would let a machine pay for each kill up to a fixed number, closer to the world's reward in the zombie lanes, with no loop that pays (Section~\ref{sec:lim-lanes}); whether that helps the agents there is untested. Counting beyond twice cannot be stated with these templates and needs investigation, starting with how a count would be written as a clause and priced.

\section{The parts inherited from ARM-FM}
\label{sec:fw-inherited}
FARM changes only how the Reward Machine is generated. The labelling function, the state descriptions and the training come from ARM-FM, and each can be improved. The labelling function is one case: a model writes its code, and its critic reads that code without seeing the labels it gives in the world. The plan check catches some faults, such as the labelling function of one ARM-FM machine that crashes (Table~\ref{tab:res-armfm-failures}), but only on the plans it runs. A first step is a critic that also sees the labels on sample episodes.

\section{IBM nl2ltl}
FARM uses a fork of IBM's nl2ltl (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/nl2ltl}), which is forked from \url{https://github.com/IBM/nl2ltl} \cite{nl2ltl_code}. The GPT engine of nl2ltl is bypassed. FARM supplies its own engine, which asks a language model for a list of clauses under a JSON schema and passes each clause to the Declare template classes of nl2ltl, which build the LTLf formula. Only the template classes and the LTLf builders are reused. The change in the fork concerns the build: it supports modern Python packaging and drops the pin on the OpenAI client, which the new engine does not need.

FARM talks to three model providers, OpenAI, OpenCode and Antigravity, through one engine each. Generator and critic are configured separately, and each engine returns the clauses under the same JSON schema. In the experiments FARM and ARM-FM use the same models, deepseek-v4.1-flash as generator and glm-5.3-flash as critic, not the GPT-4o of the ARM-FM paper.

The template classes of nl2ltl cover all eight Declare templates of Table~\ref{tab:templates}, but its GPT prompt lets the model choose only five: Existence, ExistenceTwo, Response, ChainResponse and RespondedExistence. The prompt of FARM allows all eight, so it adds Absence, Precedence and NotCoExistence to what the model may choose.

\section{FL-AT}
FARM uses a fork of FL-AT (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/Flat}), which is forked from \url{https://github.com/Jamidd/Flat} \cite{flat_code}. The fork makes five changes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Library packaging: the code installs as a package and uses package-relative imports.
    \item MONA runs in a subprocess with temporary files, where the original wrote a file in the working folder through \texttt{os.system}, and its output is parsed strictly.
    \item Proposition names are renamed with regular expressions, where the original split strings and evaluated labels.
    \item MONA's first state is removed when it is redundant: not accepting, with one successor, never re-entered, and with transitions that cover every input exactly once (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}). Otherwise the automaton stays as MONA made it.
    \item The state tuples of the product are exported, so that each product state tells which state every clause is in.
\end{itemize}

The logic that the fork writes for MONA is monadic second-order logic over finite strings (the \texttt{m2l-str} header). The parse of MONA's output is strict: an automaton that repeats a guard out of one state stops the build with an error.

\section{The generator prompt}
The prompt of the generator has three parts: a system message, a user message and a catalogue of the Declare templates. The catalogue gives, for each template, its arguments, its kind (goal or rule), its meaning in English and its LTLf formula in IBM's syntax, and states that only Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals and that every rule holds when nothing happens. A second table maps thirteen typical wordings to templates.

The instructions for the priority list the preference cues that make a clause soft: ``if possible'', ``preferably'', ``ideally'', ``try to'', ``when you can'', ``it would be nice'' and, for a ban, ``try not to''. Every other clause is hard. A cue covers the phrase it modifies, not the whole sentence or the task, and it covers the step it qualifies, not the order word that leads into that step.

A commanded step is \textbf{lone} when no hard Precedence chain links it to a later step that has a hard Existence. Every lone step gets its own hard Existence, and no other step does. Lone steps are a single ``do $a$'', the last hard step of an order, and a step whose later step is only preferred, since a soft Existence forces nothing.

An order is written as a hard Existence of its last step and one Precedence for each pair of consecutive steps: ``$a$, then $b$, then $c$'' becomes Existence$(c)$, Precedence$(a,b)$ and Precedence$(b,c)$, with no Precedence$(a,c)$. The sequence example is built this way (Table~\ref{tab:running}). An order the task states is kept even when the environment guarantees it, and no order the task does not state is added.

\section{The post-process}
\paragraph{Stages.} With a final state, the hard part of $\Phi(u)$ is the fewest events that take $u$ to a final state, where a step costs the propositions that its guard needs true, and at least one. Each soft clause, a soft goal included, adds its own count: the longest path from its state to an accepting state in its own automaton, with every cycle collapsed into one node, times its chain weight. The chain weight of a clause is one plus the weights of the clauses that the event closing it makes pending, so a rule that hands off to another is worth the chain behind it. A violated state counts zero, and a final state owes nothing. Without a final state, every clause, hard or soft, uses this local count.

\paragraph{End charges.} The optional end charges of Section~\ref{sec:open-promises} sum the local, chain-weighted count of every clause, hard clauses included, on the state that the edge into the well leaves or, when the environment ends the episode, on the state reached. The promise cost reads the hard part of the same count.

\paragraph{Minimisation.} After pricing, states that pay the same, lead to the same states on every input and end an episode alike are merged into one. Minimisation is a setting, on by default.

\paragraph{Pay order.} The pay order of Section~\ref{sec:step4} is a report, written when FARM prepares a machine for training and the step limit is known; without a step limit it is recorded as not checked. A failed order prints a warning and does not stop training, and the \texttt{generate-rm} command, which builds a machine alone, does not write the report. A machine with no final state skips the comparison of started against not started.

\section{Retries and the critic}
On a retry, the prompt of the generator carries the comments of the critic and the refused candidates, each with who refused it and at which step, the feedback, the proposed clauses and the compiled machine. The critic keeps no history and starts fresh on every attempt.

A passing failure of the generator's provider, such as a timeout or a rate limit, uses no attempt: the same attempt is rerun, up to ten times in a row. An invalid generator answer uses an attempt like a rejection. A failed critic request, or a critic answer that cannot be read, is repeated on the same candidate, up to ten times. When either runs out, or when a provider gives a permanent error, the run ends. A failure of MONA or of the translator also ends the run instead of using an attempt.

A machine text longer than 200{,}000 characters reaches the critic as a summary, and a task with more than twelve propositions gets no compiler clues.

\section{State descriptions}
After the machine is built, a tagger writes one text per state, as ARM-FM does. It sends the states in batches of twenty, with up to three requests per batch, and it also sees the information that the critic received.

\section{Machine format}
The format of FARM differs from that of ARM-FM (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}) in these points.
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are no \texttt{else} self-loops. A state with no matching transition stays where it is and pays \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD}, which is 0.
    \item The states with a special role are listed explicitly. \texttt{FINAL\_\allowbreak STATES} is always written and may be empty; \texttt{REJECTING\_\allowbreak STATES} is left out when the machine has no well.
    \item A single event expression combines propositions with \texttt{!} and \texttt{\&}, such as \texttt{has\_key\&\allowbreak !door\_open}, instead of a separate predicate such as \texttt{not\_has\_key}.
    \item \texttt{REWARD\_FUNCTION} lists only the transitions with a reward other than 0.
    \item \texttt{ACCEPTING\_\allowbreak STATES}, \texttt{HARD\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD}, \texttt{SOFT\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD} and \texttt{PENDING\_\allowbreak PROMISES} appear only when the optional charge at the end of an episode is on (Section~\ref{sec:open-promises}), which it is not by default.
\end{itemize}

\section{Drawing the machines}
In the simplified drawings, all transitions between two states share one arrow. The arrow is drawn as a cost when its rewards are negative or zero, as a gain when they are positive or zero, and neutral only when it holds both signs or only zeros. The label \textit{mixed} means that the rewards differ, not that the colour is neutral.

\section{How the examples were produced}
\label{sec:examples-produced}
The four running examples (Section~\ref{sec:running-examples} and Appendix~\ref{app:running}) were generated on 29 October 2026 with the Antigravity provider (\texttt{agy} 1.4.10). The generator was gemini-3.8-flash-medium and the critic gpt-oss-120b-medium. The effort level is part of the model name, so the reasoning setting stays at none. Each task ran with at most three attempts, and the critic accepted every proposal at the first one. A second run of each task gave an identical Reward Machine.

The code is commit a56bbed of the branch \texttt{feat/\allowbreak rule-only-\allowbreak rewards}, plus a fix to the Antigravity engine that was not yet committed (branch \texttt{fix/\allowbreak antigravity-\allowbreak structured-\allowbreak output}). The fix is needed because \texttt{agy} enforces a JSON schema only through its built-in \texttt{finish} tool, which the agent without tools cannot call. The engine now places the schema in the instructions and validates the reply itself.

\section{Prompt versions before and after}
\label{app:res-prompts}

The generator prompt went through twelve versions, v0 to v11, measured over 2,430 runs on the card benchmark. From the first version to the last, the share of ordered tasks written in the chain form rises from 52\% (22 of 42, 95\% Wilson interval 38\% to 67\%) to 81\% (68 of 84, 71\% to 88\%), short of the 90\% set as its target, and wrong machines fall from 14\% (18 of 126 runs, 9\% to 22\%) to 12\% (31 of 252, 9\% to 17\%).

Adding the compiler's plain-language clues to the critic (each rule in plain words, the hard clauses the others already imply, and the events that every way to finish includes) raises the labelled defects the critic flags at its first call from 30\% (74 of 246, 95\% Wilson interval 25\% to 36\%) to 56\% (23 of 41, 41\% to 70\%). The measure covers 38 items: 28 wrong clause sets holding 41 defects, and 10 right ones. Through the full loop of generator and critic on 46 cases, right machines rise from 77\% (213 of 276, 72\% to 82\%) to 85\% (39 of 46, 72\% to 92\%).

\section{Sequence}
\textbf{Task.} Pick up the key, then open the door, then reach the goal.

\textbf{Clauses.} All three are hard: Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}), $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{at\_goal}$; Precedence(\texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open}), $(\neg\texttt{door\_open}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{has\_key}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\texttt{door\_open}$; Precedence(\texttt{door\_open}, \texttt{at\_goal}), $(\neg\texttt{at\_goal}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{door\_open}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\texttt{at\_goal}$.

\section{An alarm and a power cut}
\label{app:alarm}
\textbf{Task.} When the alarm rings, switch it off at the very next step. Preferably, if the power ever goes out, run the generator.

\noindent{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6.2cm}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Clause} & \textbf{Priority} & \textbf{LTLf} \\ \midrule
ChainResponse(\texttt{alarm\_rings}, \texttt{alarm\_off}) & hard & $\mathrm{G}\,(\texttt{alarm\_rings} \rightarrow \mathrm{X}\,\texttt{alarm\_off})$ \\
RespondedExistence(\texttt{power\_cut}, \texttt{generator\_on}) & soft & $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{power\_cut} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,\texttt{generator\_on}$ \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

The task has no goal, so the machine has no final state (Listing~\ref{lst:alarm-rm}). This machine holds several cases at once: three loops on the same event, a well, and steps in which two or three events happen at once. Table~\ref{tab:alarm-todo} gives the to-do list of its seven states.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\label{tab:alarm-todo}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{State} & \textbf{Still to do} & \textbf{Score} & \textbf{Charge at the end} \\ \midrule
u0, accepting & nothing & $0$ & $0$ \\
u1 & run the generator (soft) & $-0.1$ & $-0.5$ \\
u2, accepting & nothing, the generator ran & $0$ & $0$ \\
u3 & switch the alarm off (hard) & $-0.1$ & $-1$ \\
u4 & switch the alarm off, run the generator & $-0.2$ & $-1.5$ \\
u5 & switch the alarm off, the generator ran & $-0.1$ & $-1$ \\
u6, well & the alarm was not switched off in time & --- & --- \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The alarm can ring in u0, u1 and u2, and each time it adds one item. The ring costs $-0.1$ and switching the alarm off at the next step pays $+0.1$, in each of the three loops u0--u3, u1--u4 and u2--u5. The product-wide potential priced the same ring in three ways: $-0.3/{+0.3}$ in u0--u3, $-0.1/{+0.1}$ in u2--u5 and $0$ in u1--u4. Every loop nets $0$ under both designs, so no loop pays, but only the to-do list gives the ring one price.

Several events can happen in one step, and their prices add up (Table~\ref{tab:alarm-steps}). Switching the alarm off as the power goes out crosses one item off and adds another, and pays $0$; the product-wide potential paid $+0.1$ for it.

\label{app:door-box}
\textbf{Task.} Open the door. Whenever the box falls off the shelf, preferably put it back.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:example-pseudocode} shows a block of pseudocode.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Example of a pseudocode block}
\textbf{Input:} list of items $L$, threshold $\theta$ \\
\textbf{Output:} list of kept items $L'$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{Filter}{$L, \theta$}
    \State $L' \gets \emptyset$
    \For{each $x \in L$}
        \If{$f(x) > \theta$}
            \State $L' \gets L' \cup \{x\}$ \Comment{keep the item}
        \Else
            \State \textbf{continue} \Comment{skip the item}
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $L'$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:example-pseudocode}
\end{algorithm}
