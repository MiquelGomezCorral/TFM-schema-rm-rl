\begin{abstract}
La construcció automàtica de màquines de recompensa (\foreign{english}{Reward Machines}, RM) en l'aprenentatge per reforç s'ha explorat recentment amb grans models fundacionals, que generen RM directament a partir de descripcions de tasques en llenguatge natural. Tot i ser prometedors, aquests mètodes no oferixen garanties formals de la correcció de les RM i depenen d'una persona o d'un segon model per a revisar-les. En aquest treball proposem FARM (\foreign{english}{A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning}), una cadena de processament basada en la lògica temporal lineal (LTL) per a generar RM: un model de llenguatge traduïx primer les tasques en llenguatge natural a fórmules LTL, que després es compilen en RM amb eines de la teoria d'autòmats. Aquest enfocament produïx RM que, per construcció, estan alineades amb l'especificació LTL, si bé la traducció mateixa encara pot ser errònia. L'avaluem amb dos experiments: un construïx les RM i les comprova davant de la seua tasca sense entrenar cap agent, i l'altre hi entrena agents i jutja si aprenen la tasca. En un banc de proves de tasques amb estructura temporal, el nostre mètode basat en LTL produïx RM més fidels a l'especificació de la tasca que les d'una reconstrucció d'ARM-FM, un enfocament de l'estat de l'art basat en models fundacionals: sense editar a mà cap RM, FARM construïx el 99,6\,\% de les RM demanades i ARM-FM, el 57\,\%. En l'experiment d'entrenament, els agents entrenats amb les RM de FARM aprenen el 72\,\% de les tasques, enfront del 48\,\% amb les d'ARM-FM i del 38\,\% sense cap RM. Aquests resultats suggerixen que la síntesi formal de RM guiada per la lògica oferix una alternativa més fiable als mètodes de generació de recompenses purament neuronals.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}[english]
The automated construction of Reward Machines (RMs) in Reinforcement Learning has recently been explored using large foundation models, which generate RMs directly from natural language task descriptions. While promising, these methods lack formal guarantees of RM correctness and rely on a human or a second model to check the RMs. In this work, we propose FARM (A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation in Reinforcement Learning), a pipeline based on Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) for RM generation: natural language tasks are first translated by a language model into LTL formulas, which are then compiled into RMs using automata-theoretic tools. This approach produces RMs that, by construction, are aligned with the LTL specification, though the translation itself can still be wrong. We evaluate it with two experiments: one builds the RMs and checks them against their tasks without training an agent, and one trains agents on them and judges whether they learn the task. On a benchmark of temporally structured tasks, our LTL-based method produces RMs more faithful to the task specification than those of a rebuilt ARM-FM, a state-of-the-art foundation model approach: with no RM edited by hand, FARM builds 99.6\% of the RMs requested and ARM-FM 57\%. In the training experiment, agents trained on FARM's RMs learn 72\% of the tasks, against 48\% with ARM-FM's and 38\% with no RM. These results suggest that logic-guided formal RM synthesis offers a more reliable alternative to purely neural reward generation methods.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Glossary}
\begin{description}
    \item[ARM-FM] Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models: the method in which a foundation model writes an RM as text from a task in plain language. In this thesis it is also the method that trains on the RMs of its rebuilt version.
    \item[Clause] One template chosen by the generator, with the propositions it applies to, a priority, hard or soft, and one plain sentence that states its part of the task.
    \item[Compiler] Steps 2 to 4 of FARM. They build the RM from the chosen clauses with fixed tools and call no language model.
    \item[Critic] The second language model, different from the generator. It accepts the result or rejects it with comments, and it never edits the RM.
    \item[DFA] Deterministic finite automaton: an automaton with a finite set of states that reads a trace one step at a time and accepts or rejects it.
    \item[Experiment] One of the two parts of the evaluation. The generation experiment builds RMs and checks them against their tasks, and the training experiment trains agents on them and judges what they learn (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).
    \item[Final state] A state of the RM in which every hard clause is satisfied. Reaching it ends the episode, and only a task with a hard goal has one.
    \item[Generator] The language model that reads the task and writes it down: as clauses in FARM, as an RM in ARM-FM.
    \item[Goal and rule] A goal is a clause that asks for something to happen, so it stays unmet while nothing happens. Every other clause is a rule: it holds while nothing happens, and a later event may break it or make it wait for an answer.
    \item[Group] One of the eight groups into which the benchmark sorts its tasks, such as the base group, which holds the tasks of ARM-FM, or the shop (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}).
    \item[Hard and soft] The priority of a clause. A hard clause must hold for the task to count as done; a soft clause is a preference, and each break costs reward but never prevents finishing. Hard rules and soft rules are the hard and soft clauses that are not goals; where the goal is included, the text says hard clauses.
    \item[Judged episode] One evaluation episode played after training, with the agent taking its most likely action at every step, and scored by the judge.
    \item[Judge] The scorer of the evaluation. It reads each judged episode through the benchmark's own labelling and checks the clauses expected for the task, in the same way for every method.
    \item[Key level] One of the four MiniGrid levels of ARM-FM's tasks, DoorKey, UnlockPickup, BlockedUnlockPickup and KeyCorridor. In each, the agent must pick up a key to open a locked door.
    \item[Labelling function] The function that reads the environment at every step and returns the propositions that hold, which the RM follows.
    \item[LARM] Language-Aligned Reward Machine, the output of ARM-FM: an RM, the code of its labelling function and a plain-language description of each state.
    \item[Learned] A task counts as learned for a method when at least two of its runs pass, which a run does when 0.8 or more of its judged episodes succeed. A task with no goal and only soft rules is compared with an idle agent (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). A task whose RM was not built counts as not learned.
    \item[Loop that pays] A cycle of transitions whose rewards add up to more than zero, so that an agent can collect reward by going round it without doing the task.
    \item[LTL and LTLf] Linear Temporal Logic, a logic of properties over sequences of states. LTLf reads the same formulas over finite sequences, such as an episode.
    \item[Method] One of the ways of building RMs or of training that the evaluation compares, FARM and ARM-FM in the generation experiment, and these two and training with no RM (shown as ``no RM'' in tables and figures) in the training experiment.
    \item[Proposition] A fact about the environment that is true or false at each step, such as \texttt{has\_key}. The labelling function reports which propositions hold.
    \item[Re-arming and folding] Two ways in which FARM handles a soft rule. A re-armed soft rule watches for the next break after each one, so every break costs again; a folded soft Absence adds no state to the RM, and its break becomes a loop that costs reward.
    \item[Reward Machine (RM)] A finite-state machine that reads which propositions hold at each step, moves between its states and pays a reward on each transition.
    \item[Rewording] The same task, said in other words.
    \item[Run] One instance of an experiment on a task. In the generation experiment it is one build of an RM for one wording of the task, and in the training experiment one training of an agent on the task from its own random start.
    \item[Scripted plan] A program of commands written in advance for a task: one solves the task, others each break a named rule, and an idle one only waits.
    \item[Scripted-plan check] Playing a task's scripted plans through a built RM, to read what each plan pays. No language model takes part.
    \item[Stage] One event still needed to finish the task. Every state of an RM that FARM builds owes a number of stages, and progress pays as that number falls.
    \item[Task] In the evaluation, a task of the benchmark, stored with the answers accepted for it, its rewordings and its scripted plans. A task trained again under a second setting counts as a task of its own.
    \item[Task schema] The clauses of a task with the propositions left open. Tasks of one schema get RMs of one shape, with the names exchanged.
    \item[Template] One of the eight fixed patterns of IBM nl2ltl, taken from Declare, such as Existence, Precedence or Absence. Each has a fixed LTLf formula in which only the propositions change.
    \item[Thinking level] How much a model reasons before it answers, from none to max. FARM uses max by default.
    \item[Well] The one rejecting state of an RM that FARM builds, which an RM may lack. Entering it ends the episode. An edge leads to it when a step breaks a hard clause or, in a task with a hard goal, leaves the task impossible to finish, and each such edge carries its own price.
\end{description}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
\section{Motivation}
In reinforcement learning (RL) an agent learns to act by maximising the reward it receives, so the reward decides what the agent learns \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Writing that reward is hard. When the reward arrives only at the end of a long task, the agent gets almost no signal while it learns, and reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it \cite{ng1999policy}. Reward shaping improves learning, but people write and tune reward functions by hand, which takes time and needs expertise in both the task and RL.

A tool that builds the reward from a statement of the task would save time and open RL to people without that technical knowledge. Formal methods suit such a tool: a task stated in a formal language has one exact meaning, and fixed tools can turn it into a reward in the same way every time. Reward Machines (RMs), finite-state machines that pay reward as the steps of a task are completed, are a natural target, because they expose the structure of the task to the learner (Section~\ref{sec:sota-rm}).

Programming assistants built on large language models (LLMs) can help write code, so they could help with rewards as well. Recent work follows this line and lets an LLM write the RM from a task in plain language \cite{creus2026armfm} (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}). A language model alone is not reliable, though. It can give different answers to the same input \cite{ouyang2025empirical} and state things that are false \cite{huang2025survey}. These approaches rely on a person or on a second model to check the RMs they generate.

A second motivation is to bring AI closer to what people want. A task as a person states it may hold more than a goal: an order of steps, rules that must not be broken and preferences to keep when possible. A reward that captures all of them carries the whole task, not only its goal. The aim is to build such rewards without a human expert, with RMs robust enough not to depend on the uncertainty of the language model. Something to clarify, that robustness is an aim of the method, not a property of every part of it. The model's reading of the task can still be wrong.

This thesis presents FARM, a formal approach to Reward Machine generation that rewards the agent's activities as they align with the user's specifications. The user specifies a task, an LLM transforms the task description into a formalisation of temporal patterns, and fixed tools then build the RM from that formalisation alone (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}).

\section{Objectives}
The main objective is a fully automated generation of RMs from tasks stated in plain language, on which agents learn the tasks. In detail:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Capture the whole task as a person states it: the goal, the order of the steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.
    \item Build the RM with fixed tools from a formal statement of the task, so that only the reading of the task is left to the language model.
    \item Need no human expert to check or repair the RMs.
    \item Verify the correctness of the method both qualitatively and empirically and compare the results against an approach that generates the RMs with an LLM \cite{creus2026armfm} and against training with no RM.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Expected impact}
The intended benefit is reward shaping and task definition open to people who are not experts in RL, and better learning through RMs that are built formally rather than written freely. The tool is meant for two groups of people, set apart by what they know. The first group knows RL, as the developers and researchers who train agents do. These people can write a reward, and the tool saves them the time of writing and tuning it by hand. The second group knows the task but not RL. These people cannot define the states and rewards of an RM, and the tool asks them only to state the task in plain language. The tool also brings AI closer to people, because a person can state a task in their own words, with its rules and preferences, and the agent then learns from a reward that carries all of them. These are the benefits the tool is meant to bring. The thesis includes no study with users, so they are not measured.

\section{Structure of the thesis}
The thesis starts with the concepts it builds on, where current methods stand, what they leave open and the approach it proposes (Chapter~\ref{chap:sota}). It then presents the new method, FARM, and its limits (Chapters~\ref{chap:farm} and~\ref{chap:limitations}), tests it and shows the results (Chapters~\ref{chap:evaluation} and~\ref{chap:results}). It ends with the conclusions and the open work (Chapters~\ref{chap:conclusions} and~\ref{chap:future-work}). A glossary precedes this chapter, and the appendices hold further examples and the implementation details.

\chapter{State of the art}
\label{chap:sota}
This chapter presents the work that this thesis builds on and where that work stands today. It first reviews the related work, and then examines what the current methods leave open, most of all the approach that generates RMs with an LLM. It ends by drawing the conclusions of the review and by proposing the approach of this thesis to meet the needs left open.

\section{Related work}
Four lines of work meet in this thesis. The first two concern the reward itself, with RMs and with ARM-FM, the approach that generates RMs with an LLM from a task in plain language. The other two concern how a task is stated, through temporal logic and through the hard and soft constraints of planning. The subsections below present them in this order.

\subsection{Reward Machines}
\label{sec:sota-rm}
In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent learns to act in a Markov decision process (MDP) by maximising the reward it receives \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Most methods treat the reward function as a black box. The agent observes the number returned after each step and nothing about how it is computed \cite{icarte2022reward}. In practice, however, a person writes that function by hand, so its structure is known and can be exploited to learn faster.

Sparse rewards pose a second problem. When the reward arrives only on completing a long task, the agent receives almost no signal while it learns. Reward shaping adds intermediate reward to guide it. Ng et al.~\cite{ng1999policy} prove that shaping with the difference of a potential function over states, $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$, leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that no other form of shaping gives this guarantee in general.

RMs address both problems by exposing the structure of the reward to the learner. Toro Icarte et al.~\cite{toroicarte2018using} introduced them at the International Conference on Machine Learning in 2018 and gave a fuller account in the work published at the Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research \cite{icarte2022reward}. This thesis follows that work for the formalisation, counterfactual learning and reward shaping.

An RM is a finite-state machine, written $\langle U, u_0, F, \delta_u, \delta_r\rangle$ in the formulation of \cite{icarte2022reward}. $U$ is a finite set of states, $u_0 \in U$ the initial state and $F$ the set of terminal states, called final states in this thesis. The transition function $\delta_u$ takes the current state and the set of propositions that hold and returns the next state. The reward function $\delta_r$ assigns to each state a reward function over environment transitions. The formulation introduced in \cite{toroicarte2018using} \textbf{attaches the reward to the pair of states of a transition instead}, which is the convention of the RM text format used in this thesis (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}).

Propositions come from outside the RM. A labelling function $L$ maps each environment transition to the set of propositions that hold in it, for example ``the agent holds the key''. At every step the RM receives that set, and its transition function $\delta_u$ either keeps it in its current state or moves it to another one. The agent learns in the product of the MDP and the RM, whose states pair an environment state with an RM state. At each step the environment state changes with the agent's action, and the RM state changes with the propositions that the step makes true. In the product the reward is Markovian even when the task is not.

Exposing the structure of the reward function through the states and transitions of the RM enables dedicated learning methods, which exploit them at each learning step. Q-learning for RMs (QRM) \cite{toroicarte2018using} decomposes the RM and learns, off-policy, one sub-policy per RM state in parallel. Toro Icarte et al.~\cite{icarte2022reward} add three more methods:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual experiences for RMs (CRM).} After every step, an off-policy learner receives one experience per RM state, the one the agent would have had if the RM had been in that state, so that one step updates every RM state.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical RL for RMs (HRM).} The agent learns one sub-policy for each edge of the RM, and a higher-level policy that chooses which of them to follow from the current RM state.
    \item \textbf{Automated reward shaping.} The shaping potential is the negative optimal value of the RM state, $\Phi(s,u) = -v^{*}(u)$, computed by value iteration on the RM (Section~4.4 of the article, restricted to simple RMs, in which $\delta_r$ returns a number instead of a reward function).
\end{itemize}

Using RMs has two costs. The first is their design. Someone has to build the RM, and RMs do not by themselves solve the problem of specifying a reward that leads to the intended behaviour, although they may make temporally extended behaviours easier to specify \cite[p.~199]{icarte2022reward}. The second is the labelling function, which is assumed to exist and to be correct. The methods that learn RMs from experience all assume a correct labelling function, and learning both at once is one of the open problems listed in the article \cite[p.~201]{icarte2022reward}. Both costs motivate generating RMs automatically.

An RM is also a graph, a form that most people are not used to reading, and it becomes hard to follow once it has more than a few states and transitions. This motivates the generation of rewards that are readable and easily interpretable by humans.

\subsection{ARM-FM}
\label{sec:sota-armfm}
ARM-FM (Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models) is the work of Creus Castanyer et al.~\cite{creus2026armfm}, published at the International Conference on Learning Representations in 2026. It replaces the hand-built RM proposed by Toro Icarte et al.~\cite{toroicarte2018using} with one generated by a foundation model (FM) from a natural-language task description. The output is a Language-Aligned Reward Machine (LARM), made of three parts: an RM, the Python code of its labelling functions, and a natural-language description of each RM state. A text embedding of the description of the current state is given to the policy together with the environment observation, so that the policy knows which sub-goal is active. In this thesis \textbf{ARM-FM is the method and LARM its output}.

The RM is produced by a generator model and reviewed by a critic model over several rounds of self-improvement. A human may optionally approve the result or give corrective feedback. The prompt given to the generator model asks it to write the RM as plain text in a fixed format. In turn, the critic prompt asks, among other checks, that regressions are penalised and that no unintentional loops exist, and the critic answers either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED} \cite[pp.~26--27]{creus2026armfm}. The paper uses GPT-4o to generate all LARM components, except in one environment of its evaluation, where several open-source models are used \cite[p.~4]{creus2026armfm}.

The evaluation covers four environments: MiniGrid (DoorKey and three harder tasks), Craftium (a Minecraft-based environment where the agent mines a diamond after gathering wood, stone and iron), Meta-World manipulation tasks, and XLand-MiniGrid. On DoorKey the agent trained with the LARM outperforms the baselines of the paper. On the three harder MiniGrid tasks, the agent trained with the LARM is the only one of the compared agents that solves all three. On Craftium it learns where an agent without the LARM makes no progress. In these three comparisons, the agent trained with the LARM does better than the agents it is compared with. On XLand-MiniGrid a single policy trains on ten tasks at once and solves unseen combinations of known tasks without fine-tuning.

Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey} reproduces the DoorKey RM printed in the appendix of the paper, in the fixed text format that the generator writes.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=rm,caption={The DoorKey RM printed by ARM-FM (page 30 of the paper).},label={lst:armfm-doorkey}]
REWARD_MACHINE:
STATES: u0, u1, u2, u3
INITIAL_STATE: u0
TRANSITION_FUNCTION:
(u0, has_key) -> u1
(u0, else) -> u0
(u1, is_door_in_env_open) -> u2
(u1, not_has_key) -> u0
(u1, else) -> u1
(u2, at_goal) -> u3
(u2, else) -> u2
(u3, else) -> u3
REWARD_FUNCTION:
(u0, has_key, u1) -> 0.2
(u1, is_door_in_env_open, u2) -> 0.3
(u1, not_has_key, u0) -> -0.2
(u2, at_goal, u3) -> 1.0
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}

The RM has four states and starts in u0. Taking the key moves it to u1 and pays $0.2$, opening the door then moves it to u2 and pays $0.3$, and reaching the goal moves it to u3 and pays $1.0$. Dropping the key before the door is open costs $0.2$ and sends the RM back to u0. Any other step, written \texttt{else}, keeps the RM in its state.

\subsection{Linear Temporal Logic}
\label{sec:sota-ltl}
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) is a logic for stating properties of sequences of states, called traces. Pnueli \cite{pnueli1977temporal} proposed it to reason about programs. Its formulas are built from propositions, Boolean connectives and four temporal operators:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathrm{X}\,\varphi$ (next): $\varphi$ holds at the next step.
    \item $\mathrm{F}\,\varphi$ (eventually): $\varphi$ holds at the current step or at a later one.
    \item $\mathrm{G}\,\varphi$ (always): $\varphi$ holds at every step from the current one on.
    \item $\varphi\ \mathrm{U}\ \psi$ (until): $\psi$ holds at some step from the current one on, and $\varphi$ holds at every step before it.
\end{itemize}
LTL is defined over infinite traces, whereas RL tasks in this work are defined over finite episodes. LTLf, LTL over finite traces \cite{degiacomo2013linear}, fits such episodes. It keeps the syntax and reads a formula over a trace of finite length, where next requires a following step to exist. LTLf is as expressive as first-order logic over finite traces, which is less than what regular expressions can describe.

For example, in the DoorKey task, where the agent takes a key, opens a door and reaches a goal, the propositions are \texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open} and \texttt{at\_goal}. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item  \textit{The agent reaches the goal at some point} is
    \[ \mathrm{F}\ \texttt{at\_goal}, \]
    \item \textit{the door is not open before the key is held} is
    \[ (\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{has\_key}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}. \]
\end{itemize}

For every LTLf formula, such as these two, there is a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) that accepts exactly the traces satisfying it \cite{degiacomo2013linear}, and tools exist that build this DFA from the formula.

On this basis, Camacho et al.~\cite{camacho2019ltl} bring LTLf to RL. They show that RMs can express reward specifications written in LTL, LTLf and other formal languages, translate a specification into an RM and combine several specifications with a product construction.\footnote{The product of two graphs has one state for each pair of states, one from each graph, and follows both graphs at once. At each step, each graph moves along its own edge, and the product moves to the pair of their new states.}

LTL formulas can also be built from a catalogue of fixed patterns. For instance, \texttt{Declare} is a language to specify business processes declaratively. It defines a catalogue of templates, such as Existence, Response or Precedence, each with a meaning in LTL. Pesic and van der Aalst introduced it under the name ConDec \cite{pesic2006declarative}.

\subsection{Hard and soft constraints in planning}
\label{sec:sota-preferences}
In automated planning, a plan is a sequence of actions that takes a system from an initial state to a goal, and the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) is the standard language to describe such problems. PDDL3 \cite{gerevini2006preferences} adds two elements to it. The first element is the state trajectory constraints, which are conditions on the whole sequence of states that the plan goes through, not only on the last one. They are written with operators such as \texttt{always}, \texttt{sometime}, \texttt{at-most-once} and \texttt{sometime-before}, close to those of LTL. The second element is the preferences, which are soft constraints. A valid plan must satisfy every hard constraint, while each preference it breaks only adds a cost to the plan. Goals and trajectory constraints can both be stated as preferences, so a condition over time can be either required or only preferred.

\section{Critique of the state of the art}
\label{sec:critique}
The methods reviewed above leave several problems open. The four that this section examines concern ARM-FM, the method that this thesis compares against. 

The tasks that ARM-FM is evaluated on are narrow, its generation is neither consistent nor structured, its RMs partially depend on a human to verify them, and its exact outputs cannot be reproduced.

\subsection{Narrow task variety}
The tasks that ARM-FM evaluates are simple in what they ask: most are a list of steps that ends in one goal. Its MiniGrid tasks are long and hard to explore, as the paper stresses \cite[pp.~5, 14]{creus2026armfm}, but what they ask is still such a list. For instance, in DoorKey the agent ``finds a key'', ``unlocks a door'' and ``reaches the goal''; in BlockedUnlockPickup it ``moves a ball out of the way'', ``takes a key'', ``unlocks a door'' and ``picks up a box''; in Craftium it gathers ``wood'', ``stone'', ``iron'' and ``diamond'' in this order; etc.

Each RM the paper prints is a chain of states, one per step, with at most a few edges that skip a step or go back after a loss. Beyond the steps, no task states a rule that must hold throughout the episode, and none separates what must happen from what is only preferable. 

\subsection{Inconsistent and unstructured generation}
Language models do not return the same output on every run, even with the temperature set to zero. Ouyang et al.~\cite{ouyang2025empirical} measured this for ChatGPT on 829 code-generation problems, and the same task can therefore yield different RMs. ARM-FM also puts no fixed structure on what the model writes. The RM is plain text in a format described by the prompt, and the rewards are free numbers.

Free rewards allow loops that pay without progress. Proposition~1 of ARM-FM guarantees that optimising the LARM also solves the sparse task, provided the RM has no loop that pays (a cycle whose rewards add up to more than zero) and its final reward is larger than what any run that does not finish collects \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. The paper describes no automatic check of these conditions. Instead, the critic prompt only asks the model to verify that regressions are penalised, that reward magnitudes do not allow reward hacking and that there are no unintentional loops \cite[p.~27]{creus2026armfm}. 

This means the check fully relies on LLM inference, and \textbf{thus is not robust}. In fact, the Meta-World RM printed in the paper breaks the first condition: the cycle $u_0 \to u_1 \to u_2 \to u_0$ pays $+0.20$, $+0.40$ and $-0.40$, a total of $+0.20$ per lap \cite[p.~32]{creus2026armfm}, although the paper states that its LARMs satisfy the property \cite[p.~21]{creus2026armfm}. By contrast, the DoorKey loop that pays $+0.2$ for picking the key up and $-0.2$ for dropping it sums to zero, so it meets the condition because two chosen values cancel, and nothing in the method enforces it.

A critic is itself a language model, and language models can state things that are false \cite{huang2025survey}. The survey covers generated text in general, and it warns that methods that rely on a model's self-correction ``may be unreliable'' \cite[Section~5.3.1]{huang2025survey}. A critic's approval is therefore no guarantee either. This is an inference, not a measured result.

\subsection{Verification by a human or another model}
ARM-FM states the limitation itself: human verification during RM generation is a trade-off that presupposes access to human verifiers. The authors add that such verifiers are not strictly required, although they can improve the output, and leave reducing this dependence by exploiting the automaton structure, for example through formal verification, as future work \cite[pp.~9--10]{creus2026armfm}. 

In practice, in 3 of the 7 settings of its Table~3 a human corrected or extended the RM. In one MiniGrid task a human asked for a transition for a dropped key, which the model then added. In another a human advised intermediate rewards, and in Meta-World a human rescaled the reward values \cite[p.~20]{creus2026armfm}. Some others needed no human intervention.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The ARM-FM paper releases no code. Instead, its appendices print the generator and critic prompts, six RMs, the labelling functions of four MiniGrid tasks and the hyperparameters. A public repository of the first author \cite{armfm_code}, which the paper does not reference, adds prompts, generated RMs, labelling code and a training script for four MiniGrid tasks only. This material allows the method to be rebuilt but not its exact outputs, so the ARM-FM baseline of this thesis is a reconstruction whose results may differ from the original ones. The authors did not answer the contact attempts made for further material.

\section{Conclusions and proposed approach}
\label{sec:proposal}
Each line of work reviewed in this chapter covers part of the problem. RMs expose the structure of a reward but are built by hand. ARM-FM generates them from language, and its guarantee holds only if the generated RM meets conditions that no automatic step checks. LTLf gives task statements a precise meaning and a route to automata, and planning separates the constraints that must hold from the preferences that only cost.

Taken as they are, these methods leave three needs open. The first is a reward that captures the whole task as the person states it, with its goal, the order of its steps, its rules and its preferences. The second is that RMs need no expert to check them, and the third is that tasks of the same kind get the same kind of RM. The three subsections below state the aims that follow from these needs. Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} then combines the pieces reviewed above into a method built for these three aims, and says how it meets each one.

\subsection{Understanding preferences and temporal relations}
An RL agent pursues whatever its reward pays for, so the reward is the way a person tells the agent what to achieve \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. More broadly, AI alignment aims to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values \cite{ji2025alignment}. For an RL agent, one part of this aim is a reward that captures the whole task as the person states it, with its goal, the order of its steps, the rules that must not be broken and the preferences to keep when possible.

Such a task is rarely a single chain of steps. A person says, in plain language, what to do, in which order, what to do in response to an event and what must never happen. The first aim is to catch these relations of order, response and prohibition, together with the person's preferences, in a shape from which a reward can be built. That needs a translator from human intent into a language that encodes such relations exactly, a part that a foundation model can play.

Each of these statements is either hard or soft. A hard statement must hold for the task to count as done. A soft statement is a preference: the agent may give it up, at a cost. This split between constraints and preferences comes from planning (Section~\ref{sec:sota-preferences}), and the aim is to carry it into RMs.

\subsection{RMs without a human expert}
ARM-FM relies on a human or on a second model to verify its RMs, and its authors name human verification as a trade-off of their approach (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). The second aim is generation without a human expert: a person states the task, and no one who knows RMs or reinforcement learning has to check or repair the RM. The properties that make an RM safe to train on, such as having no loop that pays, should follow from how the RM is built, and not from a reviewer who may miss them.

\subsection{Same kind of task, same kind of RM}
\label{sec:schemas}
A language model that writes an RM freely may write a different one on every run (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). The third aim, instead, is that tasks of one kind share a schema, the form of the task with its objects left open, and that one schema gives one kind of RM. For example, \textit{gather wood, then stone, then iron} and \textit{attend the client, prepare the order, then serve it} share the schema ``first $a$, then $b$, then $c$''. Table~\ref{tab:schemas} gives both with their formulas. Their RMs should then have the same shape, with only the objects exchanged, so that the result is predictable and the variability of the language model is confined to how it reads the task. This is an aim, not a guarantee. Tasks of one kind get one kind of RM only when the model reads them into the same schema. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} measures the closest case, whether the rewordings of one task get the same RM (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}).

\chapter{FARM}
\label{chap:farm}
% \section{Introduction and motivation}
This chapter describes the process by which FARM (A Formal Approach to Reward Machine Generation) turns a task written in plain language into an RM. The implementation details, with the list of what FARM adds to the tools it builds on, are in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}. FARM replaces only the generation of the RM in ARM-FM (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}). The rest of the ARM-FM pipeline stays as it is.

Each aim of Section~\ref{sec:proposal} rests on one tool:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A language model reads the task and the environment description, from which captures the whole task as the person states it, with its goal, the order of its steps, its rules and its preferences.
    \item The Declare templates of nl2ltl, an IBM tool that goes from natural language to LTLf, guide the LLM. It picks a template and fills it to form a clause, and the template writes the formula.
    \item LTLf encodes these statements as formulas over finite traces (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}).
    \item The Formal Language--Automaton Transmogrifier (FL-AT) and MONA, two tools for logic and automata, convert each formula into an automaton and combine the automata into one product.
    \item FARM's post-process builds the RM from the chosen clauses alone.
    \item A second language model, the critic, reviews the clauses against the task.
\end{itemize}
Together these tools form the pipeline of FARM, whose five steps use them in this order.

\section{Global overview of the pipeline}
\label{sec:overview}
FARM turns a natural-language task into an RM in five steps: task interpretation (Step~1), LTLf to DFA (Step~2), the product of the DFAs (Step~3), the post-process (Step~4) and the critic (Step~5). A language model chooses clauses, fixed tools compile them into one automaton, a post-process adds the rewards and the structure of the task, and a second model reviews the result.

Steps 2 to 4 form the \textbf{compiler}. They call no language model and read only the chosen clauses and the run settings, which makes the compiler the deterministic part of the pipeline. The two language models of Steps 1 and 5 are therefore the only source of variability.

\section{Step 1 · Task interpretation and formalisation}
\label{sec:step1}
In the first step a language model, the \textbf{generator}, reads the task and the environment description and writes the task as clauses (Figure~\ref{fig:step1}). A \textbf{clause} names one of eight \textbf{templates}, the \textbf{propositions} it applies to and a \textbf{priority, hard or soft}, and states its part of the task in one plain sentence. The answer is restricted to this form, with at most 25 clauses, and only the template, the propositions and the priority reach the compiler. The template of each clause then writes its LTLf formula.

FARM assumes that the task it receives can be done. Everything the task speaks of is a proposition that the environment description declares and that can become true in the environment, and the clauses that state the task do not contradict each other. Section~\ref{sec:lim-anomalies} describes what happens to a task that breaks this assumption, and letting the generator refuse such a task is future work (Section~\ref{sec:fw-refusal}).

\subsection{IBM nl2ltl}
IBM's nl2ltl \cite{fuggitti2023nl2ltl} goes from natural language to LTLf through templates. A language model chooses which templates to use and which propositions fill them, and each template maps to a fixed LTLf formula in which only the propositions change. 

All the work therefore lies in the choice. A template is a fixed pattern with holes, and the model only says \textbf{which pattern to use and what goes in the holes}. The choice is still hard, since the model has to understand the task, its order and the dependencies between its steps, rules and goals. 

FARM keeps the template classes of nl2ltl, improves its performance with prompting, and replaces its engine (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}) so other LLMs can be used. FARM's prompts were tuned on a set of tasks kept apart from the ones the evaluation scores (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), and Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts} gives the prompt versions and their scores.

The formulas use four operators (Section~\ref{sec:sota-ltl}): $\textbf{F}\,a$ means that $a$ holds at some step from now on, $\textbf{G}\,a$ that $a$ holds at every step from now on, $\textbf{X}\,a$ that $a$ holds at the next step, and $a\ \textbf{U}\ b$ that $b$ holds at some step and $a$ holds at every step before it.

Response and Precedence are easy to confuse, and both allow the same step. Response$(a,b)$ lets $b$ appear without $a$, but \text{after} an $a$ a $b$ must follow, at the same step or later. Precedence$(a,b)$ lets $a$ appear without $b$, but a $b$ needs an $a$ \text{before} it or at the same step.

\subsection{Goals and rules}
A clause is a \textbf{goal} if it asks for something to happen, and a \textbf{rule} if it only limits what may happen. One test tells them apart: after a step in which \textbf{nothing happens}, that is, no proposition holds, a goal is not satisfied yet, while a rule is. 

For example, Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals since by doing nothing, they cannot be satisfied. The other six templates are rules, Precedence included. They hold while nothing happens, and a later event may break them or make them wait for an answer. For example, if $b$ happens before $a$, Precedence$(a,b)$ is broken, but if nothing happens, it still holds.

The compiler applies the test to the DFA of each clause, so it needs no list of templates. The difference shapes Step~4. Only a hard goal gives the RM a final state, and the RM pays the agent as it gets closer to that state.

\subsection{The generator prompt}
IBM's default prompt is a few-shot list that offers five of the eight templates, with examples from business applications such as Slack or Salesforce, no definitions and no description of the environment. \textbf{FARM's prompt offers all eight}, adding Absence, Precedence and NotCoExistence. It gives the model the catalogue with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The meaning, kind and formula of each template;
    \item A table from typical wording to template;
    \item Worked examples;
    \item The instructions for hard and soft;
    \item The \textbf{environment description} in Markdown;
    \item The \textbf{task};
    \item On a retry, the comments of the critic.
\end{itemize}

The propositions come from the environment description, so the same LLM prompt serves any environment.
    
Three instructions shape the answer. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A step the task asks for gets its own goal, a hard Existence, unless a hard order already puts it before a later step. A single ``do $a$'' gets one, and so does the last step of an order; the earlier steps need none, since the order makes them come first. Without that goal nothing makes the agent take the step, because a rule holds while nothing happens.
    \item When the task states an order, the order stays as a Precedence even if the environment already forces it, so the RM follows the instruction and not one layout of the environment.
    \item When the task states no order, none is added. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives the prompt text and the form of an ordered task.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hard and soft clauses}
Each clause carries a \textbf{priority that the model chooses for that clause alone}. Clauses are hard by default. The model marks a clause soft only when the task uses a preference cue such as ``if possible'', ``ideally'' or ``try to'', and the cue covers the phrase it qualifies, not the whole sentence or the task. 

The priority never changes the formula. A hard clause must hold for the task to be completed. A soft clause is a preference. Breaking it costs reward but never prevents finishing (Step~4). IBM's templates have no such notion, since \textit{never touch the lava} and \textit{if possible, never touch the lava} are the same Absence clause, so the priority has to come from the prompt. For FARM, the first would be a hard Absence and the second a soft one.

\subsection{FL-AT and MONA}
FL-AT, the Formal Language--Automaton Transmogrifier \cite{transmogrifier2020}, writes each LTLf formula in monadic second-order logic over finite strings, the input language of MONA. In that encoding a temporal operator becomes a quantifier over the positions of the trace, and a proposition becomes the set of positions where it holds. MONA \cite{henriksen1995mona} decides a formula of this logic by building the smallest DFA for it.

The fork of FL-AT that FARM uses runs MONA as a separate process and reads back the automaton that MONA prints: its initial state, its accepting states and its transitions, each with a guard over the propositions. The reading is strict, and a malformed automaton stops the build (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The fork changes two things: it drops MONA's first state when that state is redundant (see next at Section~\ref{sec:redundant}), and it returns, for every state of the product built in Step~3, the clause states it combines.

\subsection{Redundant states in MONA's automata}
\label{sec:redundant}
MONA returns the smallest automaton for each formula, but two of its states do no work in an RM. Figure~\ref{fig:mona-stages} in Appendix~\ref{app:mona} shows both for the clause Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{key\_lost}$, but they are described here.

\paragraph{A pass-through first state.} MONA's automaton can begin with a state that every input leaves for the same next state. In panel~(a), S0 moves to S1 whatever happens. The FL-AT fork removes such a state and starts the automaton at its successor, which gives panel~(b). To be safe, it removes the state only when it is not accepting, has one successor, is never entered again and has transitions that cover every input exactly once. Otherwise the automaton stays as MONA made it.

\paragraph{A start for a run with no steps.} In a rule clause, MONA keeps a separate start for the moment before the first step. In panel~(b), S1 is that start. It moves exactly like S2, the state that a step without \texttt{key\_lost} leads to, and differs from S2 only in that it does not accept, because the automaton does not accept a run with no steps. An episode always has at least one step, so the difference never matters, and Step~4 merges S1 into S2 (Section~\ref{sec:start-merge}), which gives panel~(c).

\subsection{The product of the clause DFAs}
The idea of the product is to run the clause automata side by side. Each automaton follows its own clause, and after every step each one is in one of its states. A state of the product records where all of them are at that moment. In the \runex{careful key} it is a pair, written as (S1, S2): the state of Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}) first and the state of Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}) second.

A step of the agent moves every automaton at once, each along the edge that the events of the step choose in it. An edge of the product is therefore one edge of each automaton, taken together in the same step. Its guard asks for all of their guards to hold, so it joins them with \textit{and}, and it leads to the product state made of their destinations. When two of these guards contradict, as when one asks for \texttt{door\_open} and the other for $\neg\,\texttt{door\_open}$, no step can take both edges, and the product has no such edge.

FL-AT builds the product from its start state, which holds the start state of every automaton. It adds the edges out of that state, then the edges out of every new state they reach, until no new state appears. The product therefore contains only the combinations of states that some run can reach.

The \runex{careful key} example shows each part. Both automata start in S1, so the product starts in p0 = (S1, S1). In S1, Existence has two edges: $\neg\,\texttt{at\_goal}$ stays in S1 and \texttt{at\_goal} goes to S2. Absence has two as well: $\neg\,\texttt{key\_lost}$ goes to S2 and \texttt{key\_lost} to S3. One edge from each gives four combinations, so p0 has four edges. A step that reaches the goal and keeps the key, for instance, takes \texttt{at\_goal} in Existence and $\neg\,\texttt{key\_lost}$ in Absence, so its product edge has the guard $\texttt{at\_goal} \land \neg\,\texttt{key\_lost}$ and leads to p3 = (S2, S2). The other three edges lead to p1 = (S1, S2), where nothing has happened yet, p2 = (S1, S3), where the key is lost, and p4 = (S2, S3), where the goal is reached and the key lost. No guards contradict here, since the two clauses share no proposition. The edges out of p1 to p4 reach no new pair, so the product has the five states of Table~\ref{tab:run-product}. The two automata could form $2 \times 3 = 6$ pairs, but (S2, S1) never occurs, because Absence leaves S1 on the first step and never comes back.

\subsection{Discarded features of FL-AT's product}
\label{sec:flat-discard}
FL-AT also prices the product and marks its final states, and FARM replaces both. FARM needs rewards that pay for each step of progress, never pay for going round a loop, and price a broken hard rule apart from a broken preference, and it needs final states that only the hard clauses decide. FL-AT's pricing gives none of these.

FL-AT pays each edge one unit for every clause whose DFA accepts after the step. This rewards the agent before the task is done, a simple form of shaping, but it falls short in four ways.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{It pays for staying, not for progress.} A clause pays on every step it spends accepting, not once when it is met, so a loop through an accepting state keeps paying, and a rule that holds from the start pays the agent for doing nothing. Such a reward is not potential-based shaping, so it can change which behaviour is best \cite{ng1999policy}. The agent may earn more by staying than by finishing. FARM pays for the change in the stages still owed, where a stage is one event still needed (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}), so every loop adds up to zero, and its loop check refuses any RM with a loop that pays (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Progress that completes no clause pays nothing.} A clause pays only once its DFA accepts. In the \runex{sequence} example, taking the key pays the same as a step in which nothing happens, because the Precedence of key and door accepts both before and after it. FARM pays $+1$ for each stage a step closes.
    \item \textbf{A break has no price of its own.} FL-AT has no hard or soft clauses. Each clause is worth one unit. A broken clause just stops paying, one unit less on that step and on every later one, so the cost of a break depends on the steps left, not on what was broken, and an early break costs more than a late one. Nothing ends the episode either, so after a broken hard rule the agent still collects the units of the other clauses. FARM sends a hard break to the well, a shared rejecting state (Section~\ref{sec:final-well}) that ends the episode with a set charge (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}), and charges $-1$ for each soft break when it happens (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
    \item \textbf{The final states follow from the rewards.} FL-AT marks as final the destinations of the edges with the largest reward. A broken preference then stops the task from finishing, and in a task made only of rules every clause already accepts after a step in which nothing happens, so that step would finish the task. FARM marks a state final when every hard clause holds, ignores the soft ones, and gives a task with no hard goal no final state (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Clause states}
Every state of a clause DFA gets one of three labels. It is \textbf{satisfied} when the DFA accepts there, that is, when the trace so far meets the clause. It is \textbf{pending} when the DFA does not accept yet but can still reach an accepting state, and \textbf{violated} when it can no longer reach one. The labels are exact, computed by reachability on the DFA of each clause, and since each product state of Step~3 records where every clause automaton is, it knows the label of each of its clauses.

\subsection{Re-arming soft rules}
\label{sec:recurrent}
An LTLf formula gives one verdict per trace, so a broken soft rule would be charged \textbf{only once} per episode however often it is broken again. FARM \textbf{re-arms} a soft rule instead: 

\keyidea{The violated state of its DFA drops its own edges and takes those of the start, so the rule watches for the next break, and every new break is charged again.} 

This only applies to the four templates whose DFA has a violated state: Absence, Precedence, ChainResponse and NotCoExistence. The other four, Existence, ExistenceTwo, RespondedExistence and Response, have no violated state, but a soft one still moves progress while it is pending. For example, the event that sets off a soft Response can cost $-1$ at once and its answer pay the $+1$ back, and finishing with the clause still pending costs $-5$.

A soft Absence is \textbf{folded} as well. In the product, the states in which it is violated merge into the matching states in which it is satisfied, and the break becomes a mark on the edge. The clause then adds no state to the RM, and its break is a loop that pays $-1$ (Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}). Re-arming and folding are settings, both on by default. With re-arming off, a soft rule costs one charge per episode.

To accomplish this, FARM uses no table of template conversions. It computes reachability on the DFA, a deterministic process. The list above is only the result for the eight templates of the catalogue, and any new templates added by other developers, would be processed automatically by the same algorithm.

\subsection{Final states and the well}
\label{sec:final-well}
FARM drops the final states that FL-AT marks on the product (Section~\ref{sec:flat-discard}), so the RM still has to say where an episode ends. Two kinds of state end it: the well, entered when the task can no longer be completed, and the final states, reached when it is done. Only the hard clauses decide which states these are.

\keyidea{A product state in which a hard clause is violated can never complete the task, so every edge into such a state goes to one shared \textbf{well}, a rejecting state that ends the episode.}

\noindent When the task has a hard goal, the edges into states from which no final state can be reached go to the well too. The final RM can work with one well, because the price of a break sits on the edge into it, so each edge can pay its own price (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).

\keyidea{A \textbf{final state} is a state in which every hard clause is satisfied, and only a task with a hard goal has one.}

The edges out of a final state are dropped, so reaching it ends the episode, and the saved RM merges all final states into one.

\paragraph{Soft clauses} They never affect the final condition. A preference broken on the way still lets the task finish, at a cost. In the \runex{careful key} the goal is the only hard clause, so the goal is final with or without the key (Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}). By contrast, a hard clause that is only pending, such as a Response still waiting for its answer, blocks finishing but does not end the episode.

\paragraph{Unreachable states} States that the start cannot reach are pruned. In a task with a hard goal, if no reachable edge enters a final state, the clauses conflict and the build fails. A rule-only task compiles with no final state.

\subsection{Merging MONA's start state}
\label{sec:start-merge}
MONA gives each rule a ``no step yet'' start (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}), and the product inherits it as a start that no edge re-enters. Its \textbf{idle successor} is the state it moves to on a step in which no proposition holds. The idle successor becomes the start, and the old start is dropped, when three conditions hold: no edge re-enters the start, the idle successor is neither the start, the well nor a final state, and both states move to the same state on every input. Otherwise the product is unchanged.

The merge makes the RM smaller. It is safe because no template of the catalogue is broken by a step in which nothing happens, so the start and its idle successor are priced alike. On 25 sample RMs and 3,512 clause sets, the RMs built with and without the merge had the same rewards, final states and wells.

\subsection{Reward assignment}
\label{sec:reward-assignment}
With the final states and the well in place, every edge of the RM still needs a price. FARM sets these prices itself, in place of FL-AT's one unit per accepting clause (Section~\ref{sec:flat-discard}), so that the agent is paid for progress rather than for staying, and every break has a price of its own.

Every price serves one order between the runs an agent can take. A \textbf{finished} run is the best run to the final state. A \textbf{started} run is the best run with a return of zero or more to a state closer to the final state than the start, measured in the fewest events still needed to finish. A \textbf{not started} run is one in which nothing happens, \textbf{soft break} is the cost of breaking soft rules and \textbf{hard break} the mildest edge into the well. An RM must pay them in this order:

\keyidea{\centering\upshape\bfseries\boldmath finished $>$ started $>$ not started $\geq$ soft break $>$ hard break}
\noindent Not started may tie with a soft break. Every reward of this section is set to keep this order. To keep it, the rewards follow one principle: getting closer to finishing the task or a rule \textbf{pays}, and stepping back from it, breaking a rule, or getting a new thing to do \textbf{costs}. A new thing to do can come from the agent, which triggered a rule, or from the environment, which changed so that the instructions now ask for a reaction.

Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, at the end of the section, gives every value. To clarify the rewards with an example, Appendix~\ref{sec:rewards-example} follows them step by step on one task, the \runex{dropped key}: what each state still owes, what each step pays and what five runs total.

\subsubsection{Progress}
Each step is paid first for its progress, measured by how much less it leaves to do. Progress is what puts a started run above a not started one. 

A \textbf{stage} is one event still needed, and every state owes a number of stages $\Phi(u)$. With a final state, $\Phi(u)$ counts the fewest events still needed to finish plus, for each soft clause (a soft goal included), the longest run of events its own automaton may still need; without one, every clause counts its own events (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). A step from $u$ to $u'$ pays
\[ r_{\mathrm{progress}}(u,u') = \Phi(u) - \Phi(u'), \]
\gain{one unit} for each stage it closes and \loss{minus one} for each stage it adds. This is potential-based shaping \cite{ng1999policy} with the potential $-\Phi$ and no discount, so around any loop the progress adds up to zero. The learner does discount its rewards, so the guarantee that shaping leaves the best behaviour unchanged holds only approximately. A final state owes nothing, so the finishing edge pays every stage still owed. 

Two exceptions apply: a step that breaks a soft rule pays no progress for that rule, and an edge into the well takes progress back (see \emph{Taking progress back} below).

\subsubsection{Completion and charges}
Besides progress, a few events have a fixed price. Completion puts a finished run at the top of the order, and the charges keep a soft break no better than a not started run. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Completion pays \gain{$+10$} on top of the progress of the finishing step. 
    \item Each new break of a re-armed soft rule is charged again, \loss{$-1$} per break (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}), so a repeated break is never free. 
    \item A soft clause still pending when the run finishes is charged \loss{$-5$} once, on the finishing edge, so a preference that was never met costs reward without blocking the end. 
    \item A soft break costs \loss{$-1$}, a tenth of completion, so one preference weighs less than finishing the task.
    \item A hard break costs \loss{$-10$} when the task has no soft clause. When the task also has soft clauses, the price grows with the step limit, about \loss{$-96$} for 300 steps, so that a hard break still costs more than breaking soft rules at every step and stays at the bottom of the order (see \emph{The price of a hard break} below).
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{The price of a hard break}
\phantomsection\label{sec:well-charge}
A hard break sends the run to the well, and its price must keep the hard break at the bottom of the order. In a task \textbf{without} a soft clause, an edge into the well pays the flat price of Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, \loss{$-10$}. Now, in the other case:

\keyidea{\textbf{With} a soft clause in the task, a hard break must cost more than breaking soft rules at every step until the episode ends; otherwise an agent facing many soft breaks could prefer the well.}
\noindent The edge into the well then pays
\[ -\left(\max\!\left(10,\; N c\,\frac{1-\gamma^{T}}{1-\gamma}\right) + 1\right), \]
where $c$ is the price of a soft break ($1$), $N$ the number of soft rules that can break in one step (a setting, $1$ by default), $\gamma$ the discount ($0.99$) and $T$ the step limit of the environment. The fraction is the discounted number of steps in an episode of $T$ steps. 

With a step limit of 300 steps, the discount $0.99$, one soft rule per step and a soft-break price of $1$, the price is about \loss{$-96$} ($-96.096$), which is the discounted cost of breaking a soft rule at every one of the 300 steps, plus one. The price grows with the step limit and is never milder than \loss{$-11$}. The order holds only if no more than $N$ soft rules can break in one step, and the compiler does not check $N$ against the task. 

\paragraph{Taking progress back}
Progress pays as the run gets closer to the end, so a run that breaks a hard rule late has already been paid for the stages it closed. Without a correction, the same break would cost less the later it came, and the hard break could climb up the order.

\keyidea{The edge into the well takes back the progress the run made, so a hard break late in the run costs no less than one at the first step.}
\noindent A state is \textbf{ahead} of the start when it owes fewer stages than the start. The edge into the well takes \loss{one unit} for each stage by which the state it leaves is ahead, and nothing when that state is not ahead. The soft-rule charges paid on the way are not taken back.

In the \runex{sequence} RM (Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1}), the edge from u0 into the well pays \loss{$-10$}, and the edge from u1, one stage ahead of the start, pays \loss{$-11$}. An episode in which the agent picks up the key and then reaches the goal with the door closed therefore totals \loss{$-10$}, the same as a break at the first step.

\subsubsection{Checks}
The rewards are set to keep the order of the start of this section, and three checks look at each built RM to catch one that does not pay as it should. They differ in what they look for, in when they run and in what a failure does:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loop check.} It searches the RM for a loop whose rewards add up to more than zero, counting every step without discount. An agent could circle such a loop for ever and earn more than by finishing, which is known as Reward Farming. It runs on every build and refuses the RM if it finds one.
    \item \textbf{Pay order.} It checks that the RM pays its five runs in the order of the start of this section; when the optional end charge is on (see \emph{Summary of the rewards} below), the started run includes it. It is a report, written when FARM prepares an RM for training and the step limit of the environment is known. A failed order prints a warning and stops nothing, and an RM built alone with the \texttt{generate-rm} command gets no report.
    \item \textbf{Scripted-plan check.} It plays the scripted plans of each task of the evaluation benchmark (sequences of commands written in advance that solve the task or break one of its rules) through the RM and reads what each plan pays (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}). A recorded failure stops that RM from training, while a missing record or an unchecked result does not.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rule-only RMs}
\phantomsection\label{sec:rule-only}
Some tasks only set rules, such as \textit{never touch the lava}, and ask for nothing to happen. A task with no hard goal has no final state. Only the well or the environment ends the episode, and the compiler warns that the task has no goal. 

\keyidea{Progress then counts every clause on its own and aims at the states where no clause is pending rather than at the goal.}

A step into a state from which no state with nothing pending can be reached pays no progress. The rules can no longer all be met, so there is nothing left to get closer to.

\label{sec:checks}\paragraph{Rule triggers} A rule that waits for an event, such as a Response, owes a stage once its trigger happens, whether the agent or the environment caused it. The trigger costs \loss{one unit} \textbf{per stage at once}, and the reaction that the rule asks for pays it back. A trigger that is answered therefore totals 0, the same as no trigger, but it lets no other choice to the agent but to follow the rule for maximizing its reward.

The task \card{shop-siren}, \textit{Keep the shop quiet: whenever the siren rings, turn it off}, shows these rewards at work with one hard rule, Response(\texttt{siren\_ringing}, \texttt{switch\_toggled}). In the shop environment (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}), a siren also starts ringing by itself at random steps. The proposition \texttt{siren\_ringing} holds while the siren rings, and \texttt{switch\_toggled} holds at the step the agent turns it off. The task has no goal and asks for none of this work, so the agent has nothing else to do. Only an event of the environment, the siren starting, gives it something to do. 

Its RM has two states and no final state (Figure~\ref{fig:rule-only-rm}), and no well either, since the siren can still be turned off at any later step. A step in which the siren rings moves the RM from u0, where nothing is owed, to u1 for \loss{$-1$}, the charge of one stage. Turning the siren off pays the \gain{$+1$} back on the way to u0. A siren left ringing keeps the \loss{$-1$}. With the discount, the \gain{$+1$} is worth more the sooner it comes, so the agent does best by turning the siren off as soon as it rings.

\section{Step 5 · The critic}
\label{sec:step5}
The fifth step reviews the generator's work before the RM leaves FARM. A second language model, the \textbf{critic}, reads the task, the clauses and the RM built from them, and accepts the result or rejects it with comments; on a rejection the generator tries again (Figure~\ref{fig:step5}). The critic never edits the RM.

Like the generator's, the critic's prompt was tuned on that separate set of tasks, never on the tasks the evaluation scores (Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts}).

\subsection{What the critic reads and checks}
Only the choice of clauses varies from run to run, because Steps~2 to~4 turn the same clauses into the same RM every time. One review at the end therefore covers the whole pipeline, and it looks at the clauses.

\keyidea{The critic judges whether the clauses say what the task says (if they are the right selection). It reads the RM only to see what the clauses mean in practice, and does not judge its shape or its rewards, which follow from the clauses.}
\noindent The critic reads six inputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The environment description and the task, the same two that the generator read;
    \item The clauses and the RM compiled from them, which shows what the clauses do in practice: an empty set of final states, for instance, shows that a hard goal is missing;
    \item The \textbf{compiler clues}, which the compiler computes exactly from the clauses and the RM: each rule in plain words, the hard clauses that the others already imply, and the events that every way to finish includes;
    \item The \textbf{warnings}, which flag a task with no goal and a soft clause that changes no reward.
\end{itemize}

\noindent A clause passes when it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Comes from a sentence of the task;
    \item Uses the right template and argument order;
    \item Carries the priority that its wording asks for;
    \item Uses the declared propositions.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The critic also rejects a clause whose event can never occur in the environment and a hard Existence that the other clauses already imply. It does not judge the topology of the RM, its number of states or the size of its rewards, and it treats the warnings, such as a task with no goal, as valid output. Its verdict is either \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED} or a list of changes that starts with \texttt{CHANGES REQUIRED}.

\subsection{Fixing mistakes}
A rejection starts a new attempt. The generator's prompt then carries the history of the task's earlier attempts: for each, the clauses it proposed, the RM compiled from them when one was built, and why the attempt failed, the comments of the critic or the error of the compiler. The generator proposes new clauses, from which the compiler rebuilds the RM (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The critic sees none of this history and judges every attempt afresh. One stored case shows such a repair.

\textbf{Task.} \textit{Try to move the ball off the door before you take the key. Pick up the box in the other room.}

\textbf{First answer.} Two clauses: a hard Existence(\texttt{target\_picked\_up}) and a soft Precedence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}, \texttt{has\_door\_key}).

\textbf{Critic.} It asked for a soft Existence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}) as well, ``so the preferred move is expressed''.

\textbf{Accepted answer.} The second answer, with three clauses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Existence(\texttt{target\_picked\_up}), hard: $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{target\_picked\_up}$;
    \item Existence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}), soft: $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{blocker\_moved}$;
    \item Precedence(\texttt{blocker\_moved}, \texttt{has\_door\_key}), soft:\[(\neg\texttt{has\_door\_key}\ \mathrm{U}\ \texttt{blocker\_moved}) \lor \mathrm{G}\,\neg\texttt{has\_door\_key}\]
\end{itemize}
The second answer adds the soft Existence that the critic asked for.

\subsection{Different LLMs for generation and critique and bias}
A review helps only when it does not come from the model that wrote the clauses, to avoid any inherited model bias. Generator and critic are therefore configured separately, and the build refuses a critic that resolves to the same model as the generator. FARM's \textbf{default pair} is \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} as generator and \texttt{glm-5.3-flash} as critic.

\noindent To further this ``no-bias'', the critic also sees none of the earlier attempts.

\subsection{Clauses and environment facts}
The critic reads the environment description, but it judges the clauses against the task, not against the layout of one environment.

\keyidea{The RM matches what the task says, not what one environment happens to force.}
\noindent The critic rejects a clause set that drops an order the task states, even when the environment already forces that order, and it never asks for an order the task does not state. As a result, an RM can hold edges that the environment never takes.

The \runex{sequence} RM (Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1}) has edges out of u0 on an open door without the key and on the goal before the door, while in DoorKey only the key opens the locked door. These edges never fire, so \textbf{they pay nothing in training}. Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations} discusses what the compiler cannot know about the environment.

\subsection{Things to keep in mind}
Two points bound what this step can promise. Both models can be wrong, and how often depends on their thinking level.

\subsubsection{LLMs can hallucinate}
The critic can hallucinate as well as the generator \cite{huang2025survey}.

\keyidea{The compiler builds the RM exactly from the chosen clauses and adds nothing they do not state, so a wrong RM always comes from a wrong clause set: \textbf{the generator proposed it and the critic missed it}.}


\subsubsection{Thinking level of the models}
Both models can think before they answer. Thinking levels go from none to low, medium, high or max. The default is max. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} shows how the share of correct RMs changes with the level.

\subsection{Running examples: the verdict of the critic}
\label{sec:run-step5}
The critic accepted the three RMs of Step~4 at the first attempt, each time with the verdict \texttt{NO CHANGES NEEDED}. The generator therefore did not try again, and the RMs of Step~4 are the final output of FARM.

\section{The output format}
\label{sec:output-format}
The RM is what FARM hands over to training. FARM writes it in the plain-text format of ARM-FM (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}), whose training it reuses, and extends that format; Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} lists the differences.

\subsection{The fields}
Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1} shows the \runex{sequence} RM in this format.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=rm,caption={The RM that FARM builds for the \runex{sequence} example.},label={lst:farm-example1}]
REWARD_MACHINE:
STATES: u0, u1, u2, u3, u4
INITIAL_STATE: u0
FINAL_STATES: u4
REJECTING_STATES: u2
DEFAULT_REWARD: 0
TRANSITION_FUNCTION:
(u0, !at_goal&!door_open&has_key) -> u1
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&!has_key) -> u2
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&has_key) -> u3
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&!has_key) -> u2
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&has_key) -> u2
(u0, at_goal&door_open&!has_key) -> u2
(u0, at_goal&door_open&has_key) -> u4
(u1, !at_goal&door_open) -> u3
(u1, at_goal&!door_open) -> u2
(u1, at_goal&door_open) -> u4
(u3, at_goal) -> u4
REWARD_FUNCTION:
(u0, !at_goal&!door_open&has_key, u1) -> 1
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&!has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, !at_goal&door_open&has_key, u3) -> 2
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&!has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, at_goal&!door_open&has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, at_goal&door_open&!has_key, u2) -> -10
(u0, at_goal&door_open&has_key, u4) -> 13
(u1, !at_goal&door_open, u3) -> 1
(u1, at_goal&!door_open, u2) -> -11
(u1, at_goal&door_open, u4) -> 12
(u3, at_goal, u4) -> 11
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}

\noindent After the \texttt{REWARD\_MACHINE:} header come seven fields:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{STATES}: every state.
    \item \texttt{INITIAL\_STATE}: the start.
    \item \texttt{FINAL\_STATES}: the final state, at most one; empty when the task has no hard goal.
    \item \texttt{REJECTING\_\allowbreak STATES}: the well, left out when there is none.
    \item \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD}: the reward of a step that matches no transition. The RM then stays in its state and pays 0.
    \item \texttt{TRANSITION\_\allowbreak FUNCTION}: one line per transition, \texttt{(source, event) -> destination}, where the event combines propositions with \texttt{!} for ``not'' and \texttt{\&} for ``and''.
    \item \texttt{REWARD\_FUNCTION}: the reward of each transition, \texttt{(source, event, destination) -> reward}, leaving out the transitions that pay 0.
\end{itemize}

In the listing, u0 is the start, u1 holds the key, u3 holds the key with the door open, u4 is the final state and u2 the well. Each stage closed pays \gain{$+1$} and the goal adds \gain{$+10$}, so doing all three steps at once pays \gain{$+13$}. Every way to break the order costs \loss{$-10$}, or \loss{$-11$} from u1, which is one stage ahead of the start (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}, \emph{Taking progress back}).

\subsection{The RM in training}
Two more rules decide how the RM meets training: when an episode ends, and which reward the agent is trained on. An episode ends when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The RM enters its \textbf{final} state;
    \item The RM enters the \textbf{rejecting well};
    \item The environment reaches its \textbf{own end};
    \item The \textbf{time limit} runs out.
\end{itemize}


\noindent The setting \texttt{reward\_source} picks the reward:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{rm}, the default: the reward of the RM alone, plus a flat time-limit penalty (default \loss{$-5$}) on the last step of an episode that times out. The end-charge option of Table~\ref{tab:rewards} replaces that flat charge.
    \item \texttt{both}: the reward of the RM plus the reward of the environment.
    \item \texttt{env}: the reward of the environment alone, with no RM.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Limitations}
\label{chap:limitations}

A limit, in this chapter, is something FARM does not do or does not guarantee today. FARM's compiler knows the task only through the clauses the generator chooses, while it does not know the environment at all. The generator and the critic know the environment only through its description.

Each limit below is given with a case, so that a person knows what to avoid when writing a task or describing an environment. The first two limits concern the RMs the compiler builds, the next four what a task can state and how the generator reads it, and the last one the tasks that RMs are not meant for.

\section{States the environment rules out}
\label{sec:lim-impossible}
An RM can hold states that no run in its environment ever visits. The compiler prunes the states that no path from the start reaches (Section~\ref{sec:step4}), but it knows nothing of the environment, so it keeps the states and transitions that only the environment rules out.

This follows from a choice of the generator prompt. An order that the task states stays in the RM even when the environment already forces it, so the RM follows the instruction and not one layout of the environment (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). The \runex{sequence} RM of Listing~\ref{lst:farm-example1} shows the result. It has transitions out of u0 on an open door without the key and on the goal before the door, while in DoorKey only the key opens the locked door (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). Such a transition never fires, and a state that only such transitions enter is never visited. These states and transitions make the RM bigger and harder to read, but they \textbf{do not make training worse}.

FARM does not cut them, because a cut needs facts about the environment that are certain. The constraints an environment description states rest on reading the code of the environment and on random episodes, which can show a constraint false but never prove it. A wrong cut would also fail silently: if the environment did take a cut transition, the RM would have no edge for it, so it would stay in its state and pay nothing.

\section{RMs grow with the clauses}
\label{sec:lim-size}
An RM built by FARM is the product of one automaton per clause, so it can grow exponentially with the number of clauses. On the tasks of the shop, an environment of the evaluation's benchmark of tasks (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), where the agent takes orders, cooks and serves (Table~\ref{tab:eval-suites}), each added rule that waits for an answer nearly doubles the states (Table~\ref{tab:res-shop}). 

On the trained tasks where both FARM and ARM-FM built an RM, FARM's RMs are larger than ARM-FM's (Table~\ref{tab:res-size}), and the largest RM, that of a shop task with six hard rules, has more than ten thousand transitions (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). An RM of that size still follows its clauses exactly, but it is hard for a person to read. Section~\ref{sec:fw-size} takes it up.

\section{A rule alone does not make the agent act}
\label{sec:lim-rules}
Whether an agent has a reason to act depends on the kind of clause its task holds. Of the eight templates, only Existence and ExistenceTwo ask for something to happen. The other six are rules, which hold while nothing happens (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).

A task made of rules alone is therefore kept by an agent that does nothing, unless the environment sets the rules off by itself or produces some event the agent reacts to. Apart from such an environment, only a goal makes the agent act.

\keyidea{A person who wants the agent to act has to state a goal, or make the environment cause the agent to react.}

This is intended, since a rule says what must not happen or how to answer an event, not that the agent must begin anything. It limits what such a task can test. The benchmark leaves out of training the tasks with no goal that an agent doing nothing passes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards}), and it adds environments that keep setting the rules off: the zombie lanes, where zombies enter three lanes by themselves, and the shop, which for example rings a siren the agent has to turn off.


\section{Ambiguous wording}
\label{sec:lim-ambiguous}
Everyday language is ambiguous. A sentence can read two ways, and the generator may take either.

The task \textit{Try not to let zombies through both the top lane and the side lanes} can mean 
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``not the two together'', a soft NotCoExistence(\texttt{top\_\allowbreak breach}, \texttt{side\_\allowbreak breach}), $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{top\_breach} \rightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{side\_breach}$, 
    \item or ``neither one nor the other'', a soft Absence(\texttt{top\_\allowbreak breach}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{top\_breach}$, and a soft Absence(\texttt{side\_\allowbreak breach}), $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{side\_breach}$. 
\end{itemize}

\noindent FARM's generator takes the second reading, which is one of the clause sets that the benchmark accepts as an answer for this task.

\keyidea{The compiler builds the reading the generator takes, so the wording decides the RM.}
\noindent In the evaluation (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}), the few clause sets that differed from the first of the answers the benchmark accepts for their task were, all but one, judged afterwards to be fair readings of their wording (Section~\ref{sec:res-clauses}). They are cases, not a measure of how often the generator errs. The guide to task wording in the repository of this thesis (\texttt{docs/LTLF\_TASK\_LANGUAGE.md}) lists the readings found and how to write each one.

\section{Anomalies in the task}
\label{sec:lim-anomalies}
An anomaly is a task that breaks the assumption of Section~\ref{sec:step1}, that the task can be done. It is a fault in the task itself, which no choice of clauses can fix. The generator's answer is a list of one to 25 clauses with no field for a refusal, so an anomaly is caught only when a later check rejects the answer, and when every attempt is rejected the task fails with no RM (Section~\ref{sec:step5} and Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

Each of the three kinds of anomaly is caught only in part:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{An event that cannot occur}: the critic rejects a clause whose event can never occur. It checks each event alone, so a task whose events cannot happen the way it asks still gets an RM.
    \item \textbf{A fact the environment does not declare}: a clause that names an undeclared fact is rejected. The generator can instead use the nearest declared facts and drop part of the task.
    \item \textbf{Clauses that contradict each other}: the compiler rejects hard clauses that leave no way to reach a hard goal. A task without a hard goal is not checked, so its rules can contradict each other and it still builds. Soft clauses that conflict build too.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conditions the templates cannot state}
\label{sec:lim-conditions}
Some conditions cannot be written as clauses. A template argument is always one declared fact, never a negated condition or a combination of facts. All the clauses of a task also hold together, so the generator cannot join them with ``or'' or make one optional.

For example, a condition such as \textit{serve no food while the siren rings} joins two facts, so it works only if the environment declares one event that carries both, \texttt{served\_\allowbreak while\_\allowbreak ringing}.

\section{Tasks that RMs are not meant for}
\label{sec:lim-quantities}
The limits above are things FARM does not do today. This last section is about tasks it is not meant to do, because they do not fit an RM at all. They are of three kinds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{As much or as little as possible}: a clause is met or broken over a whole run, so it cannot ask for as many items as possible or for the shortest path. The templates that count stop at a fixed number and LTLf cannot express the unbounded counting. An RM that paid every time something happens would need a loop that pays, which the loop check refuses (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). 
    
    A reward paid for each event (such as \texttt{kill\_zombie}) is simple to write in the environment instead if needed.
    \item \textbf{Behaviour no one can put into words}: something the user cannot define with words, such as a movement that looks natural, gives no clauses to write. It needs a reward learnt from human feedback \cite{christiano2017deep}.
    \item \textbf{Behaviour close to single moves}: a task such as \textit{Stay close to the obstacles on your way} can be stated only with a fact declared for it or with clauses over single moves. The RM then works at the level of moves, not at the level of the steps of the task, where it is meant to work, so such a task is out of scope.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Evaluation methodology}
\label{chap:evaluation}
The evaluation tests two claims, each with its own experiment. The \textbf{generation experiment} tests the first, that FARM builds a right RM from a task written in plain language and the \textbf{training experiment} tests the second, that an agent trained on such an RM learns the task. 

For the first FARM and a rebuilt ARM-FM build RMs for the same tasks, and each RM is checked against its task with no agent trained (Section~\ref{sec:eval-machines}). For the second, agents train on the RMs of FARM and of ARM-FM plus with no RM (only on the environment's own reward) as a baseline. A judge checks what they do against the task to asses the result of the runs (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learning}). 

Smaller side experiments also train agents on the tasks of the ARM-FM paper, the shop and the zombie lanes under change (Section~\ref{sec:eval-armfm-paper}). Two smaller \textbf{studies} evaluate empirically how the thinking levels of the LLMs vary across LLMs of differen providers (Section~\ref{sec:eval-machines}).

For the terminology used, mention that we have:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tasks}: a task is one plain-language instruction, with its environment and its expected clauses. A task can be trained or not, and it can be repeated under different settings. If trained, it will come with the expected agent behaviour.
    \item \textbf{Experiments}: a set of tasks used to test a claim. The evaluation has two experiments, one for generation and one for training.
    \item \textbf{Runs}: a run is one instance execution of a task of one experiment. Either just build an RM, or rain an agent with or without an RM. 
\end{itemize}

\section{The benchmark tasks}
\label{sec:eval-cards}
The benchmark gathers the tasks of both experiments. Each of its tasks states what counts as a right RM and what an agent must do to pass. Together, all the tasks cover both the lists of steps that ARM-FM was built for and the rules and events that FARM adds. This section describes what a task holds, how the tasks are grouped and which of them are trained. A task holds  

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{text}: the plain-language instruction that defines the task.
    \item \textbf{environment}: the environment in which the task is executed.
    \item \textbf{markdown description}: a markdown description of the environment.
    \item \textbf{goal}: whether the task has a goal.
    \item \textbf{Rewordings}: to show whether the generation of a RM depends on the wording and validate robustness, a 'rewording' says the same task in other words.
    \item \textbf{answers accepted}: we found out that some tasks can have multiple valid interpretations, and therefore solutions. This is a set of lists of clauses, each list being a valid solution to the task.
    \item \textbf{scripted plans}: a task holds three programs of commands written in advance for its environment (see Section~\ref{sec:eval-plan-check}). One the plan, solves the task, other breaks a named rule, and the last 'idle' plan only waits. Playing these plans checks an RM before any agent trains on it.  
\end{itemize}

The benchmark holds 126 tasks in the eight \textbf{groups} of Table~\ref{tab:eval-suites}, each made for one kind of task. The base group holds the tasks of ARM-FM, as a baseline. Each is a list of steps in one of four MiniGrid levels, small variations of one idea in which the agent must pick up a key to open a locked door. The thesis calls them the \textbf{key levels}. At the start of every episode, a key level places its walls, door, key and other objects anew, and this arrangement is the \textbf{layout} of the episode. The other groups try different rules and events where most of them use four small environments made for this thesis: the \texttt{service room}, the \texttt{zombie lanes}, \texttt{KeyGift} and the \texttt{shop}, in which things happen by themselves and the agent has to react. Appendix~\ref{app:eval-cards} shows one whole task as an example.

Training needs one more property of a task, because an if and \textbf{idle agent}, plays like the idle plan, and the taks passes, we cannot show whether an agent learned it. 

\keyidea{A task that an idle agent passes tests nothing in training.}

An idle agent passes 18 tasks of the benchmark, 17 of the \textbf{templates group} and one of the \textbf{complex group}. None of them has a goal, and an idle agent keeps all their hard rules. They are therefore not trained, and only their RMs are scored. A task with only soft rules stays in training when the idle agent breaks one of its rules or leaves one unmet. The \textbf{forcing group} and the \text{shop} exist for this same reason, their environments keep triggering the rules, so an agent that waits fails.

Out of the 126 tasks of Table~\ref{tab:eval-suites} two are the same, leaving 124 different ones. The two pilot tasks repeat two base tasks with the same layout in every episode. The training experiment trains 53 of these 124. Of the other 71, 18 tasks an idle agent passes and the other 53 tasks not trained, are only scored in the generation experiment (Section~\ref{sec:eval-machines}). Most of these 53 are variants of a trained task, with the same rule in another environment, size or layout, so they do nos who anything meaningful at training.

Seven of the 53 trained tasks train twice, each time under a second setting. The two \textbf{pilot} tasks train with the same layout in every episode, and five \textbf{shop} tasks train with the environment's reward added to the RM's. Each repeat counts as a task of its own, so the training experiment has 53 + 7 = 60 tasks. Eleven of them are \textbf{step-by-step tasks}, the kind ARM-FM was built for, which name every step in order, and the other 49 are new kinds of task. Appendix~\ref{app:eval-cards} gives the details of the groups and of the 60 trained tasks.

\paragraph{Tasks IDs} Appendix~\ref{app:cards} gives the text of every task named in the thesis because the tables name tasks by ID. An ID starts with its group, as \texttt{tpl-} for templates or \texttt{cx-} for complex. A template task then names its template, adds \texttt{soft} for a soft rule, a number that tells apart tasks of the same kind, and \texttt{goal} or \texttt{nogoal}, as in \card{tpl-absence-soft-1-goal}. A \texttt{\textasciitilde p} with a number marks a rewording such as \cardrow{tpl-precedence-1-goal~p3} . 

\section{Qualitative evaluation of the Reward Machines}
\label{sec:eval-machines}
The generation experiment tests the first claim, that FARM builds a right RM from a task in plain language with no human intervention. It sets FARM beside ARM-FM, the method that FARM extends, and it trains no agent. 

This section first presents the two methods and how each builds its RMs. It then presents what the experiment measures on them: whether an RM is built, whether it is right, whether every wording of a task gives the same RM and how large the RMs are.

\subsection{The two methods}
\label{sec:eval-arms} We compare the ARF-FM as the baseline to our FARM method. The ARM-FM method is a reconstruction, built from the appendices of its paper and, for the tasks in MiniGrid, the first author's public repository \cite{armfm_code}, without access to the official ARM-FM code. Parts that neither source documents are reconstructed. The rebuilt baseline may therefore differ from the original, and the results compare FARM with this reconstruction. 

Between the two methods, only the way the RM is built changes (which is the goal of this work). In FARM, the generator chooses clauses and the compiler builds the RM from them (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}). In ARM-FM, the generator writes the RM itself as text. The rest is shared. 

Each method builds one RM for the text of every task and one for each of its rewordings. Then, a second phase writes the labelling function, and every RM state gets a plain-language description. Both methods use the default pair of models, \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} as generator and \texttt{glm-5.3-flash} as critic. Others are tested on the thinking-level study (Section~\ref{sec:eval-built}).


\subsection{What is measured}
Three things are measured on the RMs of both methods, and a fourth compares their sizes. The first three are whether an RM is built, whether it is right (only with FARM) and whether every wording of a task gives the same RM. The fourth is the number of states and transitions of an RM.


\subsubsection{Whether an RM is built}
\label{sec:eval-built}The first measure is \textbf{how many RMs each method builds without human intervention}. For this to be accounted as such, the generated RM has to be right, and appropiate for that taks. This is, pass its critic (see Section~\ref{sec:step5} for FARM's), its method's own checks (see Section~\ref{sec:checks} for FARM's) and run the plans check of that task (see next). If all holds, the generated RM is counted as built. Though is not yet said if that RM alone could make an agent learn the task.

\label{sec:eval-plan-check}\paragraph{Scripted-plan check} The \textbf{scripted-plan check} looks at what an RM pays rather than at what it states. It plays each scripted plan of the task through the RM, with the method's own labelling function reading every step. An RM passes when 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the solving plan does not enter the well and ends in a final state if there is one,
    \item when no plan that breaks a hard rule reaches a final state or ends in a well,
    \item and when every plan that breaks only soft rules is penalised,
    \item it does not end before the agent acts,
    \item and an RM with no final state must meet three more conditions (Appendix~\ref{app:eval-machines}).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Whether an RM is right}
The second measure is whether an RM is right. A FARM RM is \textbf{right} when its chosen clauses match one of the task's accepted answers, exactly or by an equivalent formula. ARM-FM stores no clauses, so its RMs are not scored this way. 

In other words, given that the answers of FARM are not open, we can reliably define a test set for 'how it is should generate'. The same cannot be said for ARM-FM since its machine can get any shape.

\subsubsection{Whether an RM is robust}
The third measure uses the rewordings and asks whether every wording of a task gives the same RM. We check if the build machines on tasks with at least two built wordings, the RMs are identical, have the same size without being identical, or differ in size.

\subsubsection{Size of the RMs}
\label{sec:eval-size}The last measure is size, the number of states and of transitions of an RM. The sizes of FARM's and ARM-FM's RMs are compared on the trained tasks where both methods built an RM, and each method's sizes are summarised by their median.

\subsection{Two smaller studies}
Two smaller studies look at the language models behind FARM with the goal of observing the performance of different models againts our tasks. Both run only FARM's generation, with no ARM-FM and no training, on 46 texts chosen where the generator had made mistakes before. Each text runs three times in each setting, all timed, and as said before, a run is right when its final clauses match an accepted answer of the task.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{thinking-level study} changes how much the models reason before they answer, from level none to max (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). It measures how the share of right RMs and the time per RM change with the level.
    \item \textbf{study of other language models} replaces the default pair with one model from another makers such as Anthropic's, OpenAI's and other opensource ones. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical evaluation in a real environment}
\label{sec:eval-learning}
The training experiment tests the second claim, that an agent learns a task from its RM. Agents train on the 60 tasks of Section~\ref{sec:eval-cards} with three methods (\texttt{FARM}, \texttt{ARM-FM}, and \texttt{no RM}), and one judge decides for every method whether the trained agents do what the task asks. This section presents how the agents train, when a task counts as learned and which counts the results report. Appendix~\ref{app:eval-learning} gives the exact rules.

\subsection{How the agents train}
\label{sec:eval-training}The three methods differ in the reward the agent trains on. In FARM and ARM-FM, the agent trains on the reward of the method's RM, which can also be added to the environment's own reward. Training with no RM takes the environment's reward alone and serves as a baseline.

% NOTE-OPEN: cite PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) and MiniGrid; no keys in bib-keys.md.
All three methods train the same learner, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a reinforcement learning algorithm. Every task trains under the setting of its group. Most tasks share one setting, and the pilot and shop tasks have their own (Table~\ref{tab:eval-training}). In the FARM and ARM-FM methods, the RM follows the method's own labelling function, and the agent sees the environment's observation together with its current RM state. Most of their tasks train on the RM's reward alone. On the five repeated shop tasks, FARM's agents train on the RM's reward plus the environment's, and ARM-FM trains no shop task (Section~\ref{sec:res-react}). A side test adds the environment's reward to FARM's RM in the same way on the zombie lanes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-armfm-paper}).

% PICK RB24 (accepted by the author, 23 November, about 01:10)
With no RM, the reward depends only on the environment. Several tasks, even if they ask different things, for a no-RM agent are the same. They share the environment, the layout and every hyperparameter match. Of the 60 tasks to train, this method does 19 trainings because the other 41 are repeats of these 19.

\paragraph{Note:} To avoid the influence of randomness, each method trains three runs per task, with different random seeds. Each run is a full training but with the same RM and settings.

\subsection{When a task is learned}
\label{sec:eval-learned}After training, each run plays a set of \textbf{judged episodes}. A \textbf{judge} scores each of them by reading it through the benchmark's reference labelling, written with each environment, and checking the task's expected clauses on the trace. The judge is the same for every method and never uses a method's labelling function or RM, so a wrong RM cannot turn a failed episode into a pass. First of all:

\keyidea{A task whose RM was not built counts as not learned for that method.}

\paragraph{Success} A judged episode \textbf{succeeds} when every hard clause of the task holds, the goal included when the task has one. It fails when the method's RM, not the environment, ends it in a final state before the agent could act (Appendix~\ref{app:eval-learning}). Note that \textit{soft rules never decide success}. Each break of a soft rule counts instead in the \textbf{soft breaks per episode}. The \textbf{judged success} of a run is the share of its judged episodes that succeed, and the run \textbf{passes} at 0.8 or more.

\keyidea{A task counts as learned for a method when at least two out of three runs pass.}

\paragraph{Success on soft rules}  This rule does not work for a task with no goal and only soft rules, since with no hard rule every judged episode succeeds. The runs of such a task are compared instead with an idle agent on the same layouts, through their soft breaks and their soft rules \textbf{left unmet}, those still pending when the episode ends. 


\subsection{What the results count}
The results count the tasks that each method learns, over the 60 tasks and over four kinds of subset. They split the tasks by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{kind}: step-by-step or new, 
    \item \textbf{goal}: whether the task has a goal or not, 
    \item \textbf{what}: what the task asks, 
    \item \textbf{group}: those listed at \ref{tab:eval-suites}.
\end{itemize} 
% PICK H2H
A last count pairs FARM and ARM-FM task by task. The \textbf{head-to-head} count sorts each of the 60 tasks by which of the two methods learn it, both, FARM only, ARM-FM only or neither.

\subsection{Further comparisons}
% NOTE-OPEN: cite XLand-MiniGrid; no key in bib-keys.md.
\label{sec:eval-armfm-paper}Smaller experiments go beyond the 60 tasks. Each changes one thing, the tasks, the environment or the training, to find where the methods reach their limits. They are side tests with fewer tasks and runs, so they are not counted with the 60, and Section~\ref{sec:res-further} gives their results.

\paragraph{The tasks of the ARM-FM paper} The first trains all three methods on tasks of the ARM-FM paper~\cite{creus2026armfm}. They are DoorKey from MiniGrid, UnlockToUnlock from BabyAI and tasks of XLand-MiniGrid, a second grid world. In XLand-MiniGrid, one agent can learn several tasks together. Each of its tasks therefore trains on its own and also in a mix of three or five tasks, where one agent learns them all.

\paragraph{The shop} This is one of the environments created just for this thesis. The shop is the hardest group of the benchmark, since it puts several complex rules together while the agent has to serve food to its customers. Several experiments are run on this group only to get an agent to learn it, as FARM's RMs for it are already right.

\paragraph{The zombie lanes} This is another of the environments created for this thesis. The agent has to react to the zombies and keep them from crossing the lanes, guarding one lane before the others. Three experiments change the setup. They make the environment smaller, add obstacles that force the agent to guard one lane first, and add the environment's own reward to FARM's RM.

\paragraph{Other limits} Four last experiments each test one limit: three times the training on two tasks, other room sizes for KeyCorridor, training the ARM-FM RMs that were not built because they fail a check, and FARM's promise cost turned on.

\chapter{Results}

The two experiments of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} test two claims. The first is that FARM builds a right RM from a task in plain language, and the second that an agent trained on that RM learns the task. The chapter follows the same order. \section{Comparison between ARM-FM and FARM}
\label{sec:res-comparison}

FARM and ARM-FM are compared in two ways. The first is the RMs they build from the same tasks, and the second is what agents learn with those RMs, beside training with no RM. For the first, both are asked for the same 266 RMs, 111 of them for a task's own wording and the rest for its rewordings. FARM builds its RMs at thinking level max.

\subsection{RMs built with no hand edit}
\label{sec:res-built}

An agent can train with an RM only once the RM is built, so the first comparison counts the RMs each method builds and the rounds each build takes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-arms} and Appendix~\ref{app:res-counts}).

No RM was edited by hand. Under that condition, FARM builds 265 of the 266 RMs asked (99.6\%, 95\% Wilson interval 97.9\% to 99.9\%). Of these, 262 passed every model check at the first round of each phase. The other 3 needed a second round after one automatic rejection by a critic.

FARM builds the RM of every task's own wording, and its one miss is a rewording of a shop task (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}).

In each round, the critic is the model that can stop a wrong candidate. It reads each RM together with the compiler clues, the facts that the compiler computes exactly from the clauses and the RM (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). These clues help it. On clause sets with known defects, they raise the share of defects that the critic flags at its first call from 30\% to 56\% (Appendix~\ref{app:res-prompts}).

The rebuilt ARM-FM builds far fewer, 152 of the 266 (57\%, 95\% Wilson interval 51\% to 63\%). It builds 132 at the first round and 20 after further rounds.

The built RMs of both methods take few rounds, 2.01 on average for FARM and 2.16 for ARM-FM against a least possible of two (Section~\ref{sec:eval-built}), and Figure~\ref{fig:res-built} splits them by the round at which each was built.

\subsection{FARM's RMs}
\label{sec:res-farm-machines}

All FARM RMs come from the same compiler, and this subsection shows how they pay and whether they are right. A FARM RM is built from the product of the automata of the clauses the generator chooses, and it keeps hard and soft rules apart. It pays in whole units:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Progress.} Each step of progress pays \gain{$+1$}, and the finish pays \gain{$+10$} more.
    \item \textbf{Hard break.} It sends the RM to the well and ends the episode. The edge into the well pays \loss{$-10$} when the task has no soft clause and more when it has one, about \loss{$-96$} for a step limit of 300 steps (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}). It also takes back the progress already paid.
    \item \textbf{Soft break.} Each one costs \loss{$-1$}, and the episode goes on.
\end{itemize}

% PICK RB17: wording to confirm with the author
% alt: A model reviews every built machine against its task, and at the end none is flagged as wrong in its rules.
A model reviewed every built RM against its task, and none is wrong in its rules.
The review covers only the RMs that were built, not FARM's one miss on a shop rewording. The shop environment has no event for putting food down, so the pick-up that this wording asks for has no exact rule, and the generator added a seventh clause for it. With that clause, one wrong plan fails a condition that the scripted-plan check sets for an RM with no final state, and the check refuses the RM (Appendix~\ref{app:eval-machines}).

\subsubsection{Which clauses FARM chooses}
\label{sec:res-clauses}

The compiler builds exactly the clauses the generator chooses, so a FARM RM is as right as that choice. Every RM that FARM builds matches one of the answers its task accepts. The only wording without a right RM is the shop rewording above, whose RM was never built.

The generator's choice also repeats from run to run. When it runs more than once on one wording, the runs that give a right RM almost always choose the same answer (Appendix~\ref{app:res-choice}).

\subsection{ARM-FM's RMs}
\label{sec:res-armfm-machines}

ARM-FM's RMs are made in another way. Its generator writes each RM as text, and no compiler builds it. This subsection shows the shape these RMs take and why so many of them were not built.

An ARM-FM RM is a chain of steps through the level. It pays a fraction of a unit for each step and \gain{$+1$} for the last. In the 31 distinct ARM-FM RMs trained, a step pays \gain{$+0.05$} to \gain{$+0.3$}, and over all 152 built \gain{$+0.05$} to \gain{$+0.4$}. Some RMs also charge for an event, such as the loss of the key. Unlike a FARM RM, an ARM-FM RM stores no clauses and marks no rule as hard or soft.

The 114 RMs that ARM-FM did not build all pass both phases and then fail the scripted-plan check. Table~\ref{tab:res-armfm-failures} sorts them by the first problem the check finds, and one problem stands out. In half of them the start state is final, so every episode ends before the agent acts.

\subsubsection{Size}
The third difference is size, the number of states and transitions of an RM (Section~\ref{sec:eval-size}). FARM's RMs are the larger ones.

\subsubsection{New kinds of task}
The other 49 tasks are of new kinds, and they split into tasks with no goal, tasks with a goal and the shop (Table~\ref{tab:res-groups}). FARM's largest gain is on the 15 tasks with no goal, outside the shop. FARM learns 14 of them (93\%, 70\% to 99\%), while ARM-FM learns 4 (27\%, 11\% to 52\%), because its RM was not built on 11 of them. On 7 of these 11, its RM was not built because it starts in a final state, the most common failure seen above.

Split by clause instead, the same 49 tasks form two subsets, 29 tasks with hard rules but no order and no soft rule, and 20 tasks with at least one soft rule. FARM is ahead in both. It learns 16 of the 29, against 13 for ARM-FM. Of the 20, it learns 16, against 8. By group, ARM-FM learns none of the 4 forcing tasks, the 6 shop tasks and the 5 shop tasks with the environment's reward, since its RM was not built on any of them.

\subsubsection{Soft rules}
On a task with a goal, only the hard clauses decide whether the task is learned, and a task with only soft rules is compared with an idle agent instead (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). For this reason the soft rules are measured on their own, with two measures, the soft breaks per judged episode and the soft rules still unmet when the episode ends. Each is a mean over the three runs of a task, and the three methods are compared on both. The zombie-lane tasks, where the environment itself sets off the soft rules, are left for Section~\ref{sec:res-react}. Of the other soft-rule tasks, four have only soft rules and are also compared with the idle agent. The other 11 have a goal and are not. Table~\ref{tab:res-soft}, in Appendix~\ref{app:res-soft}, gives both measures on each of these tasks.

Four comparisons stand out:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Soft-only tasks.} Against an idle agent, FARM's agents keep the soft rules on the four tasks with only soft rules. They break none and leave none unmet, while the idle agent breaks one rule on \card{tpl-chain-response-soft-1-nogoal}, which asks the agent to pick up a fallen box on its very next move, and leaves one unmet on the other three.
    \item \textbf{Tasks with a goal.} FARM's agents leave a soft rule unmet on one of the 11 tasks, \card{tpl-responded-existence-soft-1-goal}, on two of its three runs.
    \item \textbf{Training with no RM.} It breaks as few soft rules as FARM's agents. In every episode, however, it leaves unmet a soft rule that asks for something to happen at some point, such as the soft Existence rule. It does so on four tasks with a goal and on two tasks with only soft rules, \card{tpl-existence-soft-1-nogoal} and \card{tpl-responded-existence-soft-1-nogoal}, so it does not learn those two.
    \item \textbf{Order tasks.} Agents break a soft rule on two tasks whose soft rule sets an order (Precedence). On \card{tpl-precedence-soft-1-goal}, which neither FARM nor training with no RM learns, the whole count of each, 1.91 and 1.92, comes from one judged episode of the 300 that breaks the rule 574 times (575 with no RM), and no other episode breaks a soft rule. ARM-FM learns that task and breaks none. On \card{tpl-precedence-soft-2-goal}, FARM's agents and those trained with no RM break the soft rule once per episode (both 1.00), and ARM-FM's fewer (0.67). This task still counts as learned, even though FARM's agents break the rule on every run.
\end{itemize}
Taken together, FARM's agents break no more soft rules than an idle agent where only soft rules decide, and on none of these tasks leave more unmet than training with no RM.

Over all 20 soft-rule tasks, the zombie-lane tasks included, FARM's agents break no soft rule on 14. The other six are the two order tasks above and four lane tasks, \card{lanes-rules}, \card{tpl-absence-soft-1-nogoal}, \card{tpl-not-co-existence-soft-1-nogoal} and \card{tpl-precedence-soft-1-nogoal}, on which the environment itself sets off the soft rules (Section~\ref{sec:res-react}).

\section{Reacting to events}
\label{sec:res-react}

A rule that answers an event holds while the event never happens, so an agent that does nothing passes it. The forcing environments of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} set such rules off again and again, and this section reports what agents learn in them. The zombie lanes are the main forcing environment, and the box-carry environments the second. Four things define the zombie lanes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Field.} It has 9 by 9 cells crossed by three lanes. The top lane is the one to protect, and plants grow off the lanes.
    \item \textbf{Zombies.} They enter at the right end of a random lane, with odds that rise from 4\% to 17\% per step, and walk one cell left every three steps.
    \item \textbf{Agent.} It starts on the middle lane and kills a zombie by moving onto it.
    \item \textbf{Episode.} It lasts 300 steps, and the environment pays 1 for each zombie killed.
\end{itemize}
In this environment, an idle agent lets 26.2 zombies through per episode, 8.7 in the top lane and 17.5 in the side lanes, and it is the reference for the tasks with only soft rules. Seven tasks run in the zombie lanes, and Appendix~\ref{app:res-lanes} gives the clauses FARM chose for each, the size of its RMs and what each method learns on them, run by run. FARM learns all seven. Two of them, \card{lanes-guard} and \card{lanes-rules}, have a hard rule that forbids letting a zombie through the top lane, and training with no RM learns the other five. ARM-FM learns one, \card{tpl-existence-two-5-nogoal}, which asks for at least two kills. On the other six its RM was not built, because it starts in a final state on five of them and pays nothing for breaking the soft rule of \card{tpl-existence-two-soft-1-nogoal} (Table~\ref{tab:res-armfm-failures}).

Where the top lane is a hard rule, FARM learns the task and training with no RM does not. On the task that only forbids letting a zombie through the top lane (\card{lanes-guard}), FARM passes on all three runs and training with no RM on none. The task that adds two soft rules to it (\card{lanes-rules}) also has this hard rule, so it is not compared with an idle agent. There FARM passes on two of the three runs, and training with no RM again on none. On its soft rules, FARM's agents break 15.6 per episode, against 10.6 for training with no RM.

Three tasks have only soft rules that zombies break, each named here by its template. The absence task, \card{tpl-absence-soft-1-nogoal}, asks the agent to let no zombie through any lane. The not-both task, \card{tpl-not-co-existence-soft-1-nogoal}, asks it not to let zombies through both the top lane and the side lanes, and the precedence task, \card{tpl-precedence-soft-1-nogoal}, to let none through before it has killed one. Training with no RM runs on the environment's reward alone and serves none either, so the RM is not the only obstacle. Whether an agent learns to serve depends on the environment, the learner and how the RM pays each order, and these results do not separate the three. On this last point, an order nets nothing on FARM's shop RMs. Each client who comes in sets off rules that wait for an event, and such a trigger costs at once what the reaction that the rule asks for pays back, so a served order totals zero (Section~\ref{sec:step4}). The optional charges for stages left open are off and add nothing.
Making the start of the shop easier changes this on two simpler shop tasks, where FARM's agents then serve (Section~\ref{sec:res-curriculum}).

\section{Thinking level and other LLMs}
\label{sec:res-thinking}
\label{sec:res-models}

The thinking level sets how much the models reason before they answer, from none to max. FARM uses max by default for both of its models, the generator and the critic. This section first shows what each level gives and what it costs with the default pair, and then, in a second study, sets other language models beside it on the same tasks.

To measure how the share of right RMs (Section~\ref{sec:eval-built}) and the time per RM change with the level, the study runs FARM's generation three times at each thinking level of its models, on 46 tasks chosen where the generator had erred before. These runs use one model pair and one provider.

\subsection{The shop in smaller steps}
\label{sec:res-curriculum}

An agent starts with no knowledge of the environment, so its first episodes are close to a random walk, and it meets its first reward only by chance. The larger the shop and the more happens in it, the rarer that first reward. The experiments of this section therefore make the start of the shop easier. They train two shop tasks simpler than those of the training experiment. In the first, the agent delivers food to the clients, and in the second it also takes their orders.

\textbf{Holding the falls back works.} In the full 8$\times$8 shop, FARM's agents learn neither task, in 0 of 6 runs, while training with no RM learns them in 5 of 6. When food starts to fall only once the agent serves 10 orders per episode, all 6 runs learn (95\% Wilson interval 61\% to 100\%), and they serve 18.7 and 11.6 orders per judged episode on the two tasks. When a quarter of the falls land behind the agent, however, no run learns.

\textbf{A smaller shop works as well.} In a 3$\times$3 shop, FARM learns the two tasks in all 6 of its runs (61\% to 100\%), and in 5$\times$5 and 7$\times$7 shops in none of 12 (0\% to 24\%). In the larger shops, its runs serve no order in their first 100 training episodes, so the agent has almost nothing to learn from. A shop that grows within one run, from 3$\times$3 to 7$\times$7, each time the agent serves 10 orders per episode, learns at 7$\times$7 in 3 of 6 runs, against 0 of 6 without growth. The intervals overlap, however, and training with no RM learns the 7$\times$7 shop in 5 of 6 runs. Over five tasks of one job each in the same four shops, where food falls only in the task about falls, FARM and training with no RM learn about as many, 11 and 10 of 20. Both learn serving and orders in every shop and cooking in the two smallest, and neither learns the falls or the siren.

\textbf{Splitting the jobs does not help on its own.} In the 8$\times$8 shop, FARM learns none of five tasks that add the shop's jobs one by one. A curriculum of tasks, in which one run of 15M steps adds the jobs one at a time and grows the shop, serves no order in any of its 9 runs.

In the shop, then, FARM's agents learn once their first rewards come early, in a small shop or with the falls held back. Training with no RM needs no such help on these two simpler tasks. No curriculum yet learns the shop tasks of the training experiment, with all their jobs.

\subsection{The zombie lanes under change}
\label{sec:res-lanes-more}

The zombie lanes test how an agent reacts to events it does not cause (Section~\ref{sec:res-react}). Three changes to the environment show when FARM's RM helps there. Table~\ref{tab:res-lanes-more} counts the runs that pass on the two lane tasks with a hard clause, \card{lanes-guard} and \card{lanes-rules}.

\textbf{Closer lanes keep FARM ahead.} The lanes of the training experiment are four rows apart. Moving them two rows or one apart also shrinks the environment, from 11$\times$11 cells to 11$\times$7 and 11$\times$5. FARM's agents then pass both tasks in every run, and training with no RM learns them only with the lanes one row apart.

\textbf{With plants, training with no RM catches up.} Plants stun the agent for two steps, and a zombie that crosses the top lane ends the episode, whatever the method. Training with no RM on the reward for each kill then learns both tasks at every spacing, while FARM learns the rules task only with the lanes two rows apart. The early end makes the difference, since without it the same reward learns neither task. It likely gives training with no RM what FARM's RM gives it in the plain lanes, a cost for the top lane. Other rewards do worse. A penalty for each breach added to the kill reward learns both tasks only with the lanes two rows apart, and the penalty alone learns neither.

\textbf{Adding the kill reward to FARM's RM changes little.} FARM with the environment's reward passes about as often as FARM alone, except on the rules task with the lanes four rows apart, where only 1 of 3 runs passes. On the two soft-only tasks of Section~\ref{sec:res-react}, its agents kill slightly more zombies on the absence task, 9.4 to 9.8 per episode against 7.8 to 8.1, and still let about 8.6 zombies per episode through the top lane, as FARM alone does. Paying each kill is therefore not enough to close the gap with training with no RM. These runs do not tell whether the late charge or the equal price of both lanes holds the agent back.

\subsection{Other limits}
\label{sec:res-other-limits}

Four last experiments each test one limit.

\textbf{Longer training.} Two tasks of the training experiment have a right FARM RM and are still not learned. With three times the training, 4.5M steps, one of them is learned, at 0.80 to 0.85 of its judged episodes against 0.55 to 0.67 before. The other still passes in no run.

\textbf{Room size.} FARM learns KeyCorridor with rooms of size 3 in all 3 runs, and training with no RM in none. With rooms of size 5 or 6, the size the ARM-FM paper uses, neither learns it. The training experiment uses size 4.

\textbf{ARM-FM's own checks.} On nine tasks of the training experiment, ARM-FM's RM is not built because it fails a check, yet it still lets the agent act. Trained anyway, seven of them are learned. ARM-FM would not hand these RMs over, so they are not counted (Section~\ref{sec:eval-learned}). Counted, they would raise ARM-FM's learned tasks from 29 to 36 of 60, still below FARM's 43.

\textbf{The promise cost.} Turning on FARM's promise cost, an optional charge of Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, changes no result on the tasks trained both ways. Five of them are shop tasks that FARM learns neither way.

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

The main objective of this thesis is a fully automated generation of RMs from tasks stated in plain language, on which agents learn the tasks. A further aim of the thesis is a curriculum for the shop. FARM addresses the main objective by changing one step of ARM-FM, the generation of the RM. A language model writes the task as clauses from a fixed catalogue of templates, each hard or soft, and fixed tools then compile the clauses into the RM and price its transitions.

\keyidea{The RM follows the chosen clauses by construction, but whether the clauses say what the person meant rests on the generator and its critic.}

\noindent The main objective is \textbf{met}. No RM was edited by hand, and FARM builds 265 of the 266 RMs asked for (99.6\%, Section~\ref{sec:res-built}). Agents also learn more tasks with these RMs than in the other two methods. Of the 60 tasks of the training experiment, they learn:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 43 of 60 (72\%, 95\% Wilson interval 59\% to 81\%) with FARM's RMs;
    \item 29 of 60 (48\%, 36\% to 61\%) with the RMs of the rebuilt ARM-FM;
    \item 23 of 60 (38\%, 27\% to 51\%) with no RM.
\end{itemize}
\noindent A task whose RM was not built counts as not learned for that method (Section~\ref{sec:res-learning}). The claim is about tasks learned, not about how fast they are learned.

% PICK H2H: from here to END PICK H2H, cut as one block.
FARM's lead over ARM-FM comes mostly from tasks on which ARM-FM builds no RM. Of the 21 tasks that only FARM's agents learn, ARM-FM's RM was not built on 18. In the other direction, the 7 tasks that only ARM-FM's agents learn all sit on one level, \mbox{BlockedUnlockPickup}, where the agent moves a ball, takes a key and opens a door before it picks up a box. They name one or two of these three steps, never all three (Table~\ref{tab:res-h2h}). ARM-FM's RM pays for each of those steps, asked for or not, while FARM's keeps to the task and leaves little reward before the box. That difference in reward may explain these 7 tasks, but no run tests it.
% END PICK H2H

As for the RMs themselves, rewording a task seldom changes FARM's, and it changes ARM-FM's more often (Section~\ref{sec:res-machines}). The rewordings were built and not trained, however, so this holds for the RMs and says nothing about learning.

Two things can still go wrong. First, the generator can add a rule of its own. It did so on one rewording of a shop task, and the scripted-plan check refused the RM (Section~\ref{sec:res-farm-machines}). Second, where the wording is ambiguous, the reading the generator takes decides the RM (Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}).

% PICK SHOP: framing to confirm with the author
In the shop, however, no trained agent serves an order, whether it trains on FARM's RM, on the environment's reward or on both, and ARM-FM, whose shop RMs start in a final state, trains none of the shop tasks. The RM alone does not explain this. An order nets nothing on FARM's shop RMs, but training on the environment's reward, which pays each order served, serves none either. Whether an agent learns to serve depends on the environment, the learner and how the RM pays each order, and the results do not separate these causes (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}).

This further aim, a curriculum for the shop, is met \textbf{in part}. Holding the falls back lets FARM's agents serve on two simpler shop tasks, and no curriculum yet learns the shop tasks with all their jobs (Section~\ref{sec:res-curriculum}).

RM size stays open as well. FARM's RMs are larger than ARM-FM's and grow with the number of clauses, so the largest are hard to read. Making them smaller is work still to do (Chapter~\ref{chap:future-work}).

\section{Scope and limits of the study}
\label{sec:scope}
The conclusions above hold within the bounds of the study. Some of these bounds come from how the comparison with ARM-FM is set up and from the models it uses, and others from the tasks that FARM can state.

FARM changes only one step of ARM-FM, the generation of the RM. The labelling functions, the state descriptions and their embeddings follow ARM-FM and sit outside the main comparison, so the comparison with ARM-FM is isolated to that step. The ARM-FM that FARM is compared with is a reconstruction, rebuilt from the paper and from a public repository of its first author. Its results may therefore differ from the original ones (Section~\ref{sec:critique}). Both methods also run on the same two models, \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} as generator and \texttt{glm-5.3-flash} as critic, where the ARM-FM paper used GPT-4o. These models are small and cheap on purpose. They are not the strongest available, but they are enough to test the method and they keep it accessible. Whether the comparison holds with other models is not tested. FARM's generation alone is scored with two other models, on 46 tasks (Section~\ref{sec:res-models}).

The other bounds concern the tasks. FARM states tasks with the eight Declare templates of IBM nl2ltl, read as LTLf over finite traces. A task that these templates cannot state is outside its reach (Section~\ref{sec:lim-conditions}), and a model that writes LTLf directly is future work (Section~\ref{sec:fw-ltlf}). The work also leaves out tasks that ask to optimise a quantity, such as \textit{Kill as many zombies as you can} or \textit{Reach the goal by the shortest path}, and behaviour that a person cannot put into words. A clause is met or broken over a whole run, so it cannot ask for as much of something as possible, and a behaviour with no words gives no clauses to write (Section~\ref{sec:lim-quantities}). The first group of bounds limits what the comparison with ARM-FM shows, and the second limits the tasks that FARM can state.

\chapter{Future work}
\label{chap:future-work}

One study of the Results chapter still waits for data, the study of other language models, which still lacks some models (Section~\ref{sec:res-models}). Beyond them, the five directions below go past this thesis. Each one gives what is missing today, why it matters and a first step.

\section{Smaller RMs}
\label{sec:fw-size}
A FARM RM is the product of one automaton per clause. It is minimised by merging the states that behave the same, but it still grows with the number of clauses (Sections~\ref{sec:step4} and~\ref{sec:lim-size}).

The boil task of the shop, which has six hard rules, shows this growth. Each of these rules is either waiting for its answer or not, so the RM holds one state for each combination of the six, plus the well. That gives 65 states and 10,662 transitions (Section~\ref{sec:res-shop}). The RM is already minimised giving the conditions in which it was built, so none of its states can be merged without changing what it pays.

The first step in this direction could be to measure how the size grows with the number and the kind of rules, on tasks built for that purpose. Another more direct direction is presented in the next section. 

\section{A model that writes LTLf}
\label{sec:fw-ltlf}
The generator picks clauses from eight templates whose arguments are single declared facts. A task can therefore say no more than those templates state (Section~\ref{sec:lim-conditions}), and the generator cannot use other logical constructs to simplify the formulas of a task.

A further step is a generator that writes the LTLf formulas itself. Such a model could state negated conditions, alternatives and nested conditions. This way, it could give shorter formulas and smaller RMs, and state tasks that the templates cannot. In exchange, it would give up what the fixed catalogue gives, a known meaning, automaton and price for every clause. 

For example, a shop task with a chain of four steps: every client who arrives ($a$) has the order taken ($b$), then the food prepared ($c$), then the order served ($d$). With the templates, the generator states the chain link by link, as three Response clauses:
\[
\mathrm{G}\,(a \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,b) \;\wedge\; \mathrm{G}\,(b \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,c) \;\wedge\; \mathrm{G}\,(c \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,d)
\]
Each clause is a promise of its own, so the RM keeps one state for every combination of promises still open, 8 states and 55 transitions. A model that writes LTLf can state the chain as a single promise, with each step nested inside the one before:
\[
\mathrm{G}\,\bigl(a \rightarrow \mathrm{F}\,(b \wedge \mathrm{F}\,(c \wedge \mathrm{F}\,d))\bigr)
\]
Its RM has 4 states and 15 transitions, one with nothing owed and one for each step still owed. The reward changes with it, however. With three clauses, the RM charges three units when the chain opens and pays one back at each later step. With the single formula, it charges one unit when the chain opens and pays it back when the chain closes, with nothing in between.

The rest of the pipeline should hold. Whether it does still needs testing. A first step is to ask such a model for formulas on tasks the catalogue cannot state, and to pass them through the same checks.

\section{A way to refuse}
\label{sec:fw-refusal}
The generator's answer is a list of clauses with no way to refuse. A task it should refuse therefore ends in failed attempts, or in clauses that come close and do not say what they leave out (Section~\ref{sec:lim-anomalies}).

A field for a refusal, with its reason, would let the model report a task that cannot be done, a fact the environment does not declare or a wording it cannot settle. A person could then fix the task or the environment description. 

The first step is to add the field  on the generator output schema, adjust the prompt and test it on tasks built to be refused.

\section{Counting}
\label{sec:fw-counting}
The templates count only to two, with ExistenceTwo, which asks for an event at least twice (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). Beyond two, ``at least $n$ times'' needs an LTLf formula (Section~\ref{sec:fw-ltlf}) that grows with $n$. But an \textbf{unbounded} count, such as ``one kill for each zombie that enters``, cannot be stated in LTLf.

A count would let an RM pay for each kill up to a fixed number, with no loop that pays. Such an RM would come closer to the reward of the zombie lanes, where the environment pays for each kill (Section~\ref{sec:res-react}). 

The first step is to find how a count would be written as a clause and priced, if even possible at all with RMs, since their nature does not allow unbounded counting.

\section{The parts inherited from ARM-FM}
\label{sec:fw-inherited}
The parts that FARM takes from ARM-FM unchanged, the labelling function, the state descriptions and the training, can each be improved.

The labelling function is one case. A model writes its code, and its critic reads that code without seeing the labels it gives in the environment. The scripted-plan check catches some faults, such as the labelling function of one ARM-FM RM, which crashes (Table~\ref{tab:res-armfm-failures}). However, it catches them only on the plans it runs. 

The state descriptions are another. The agent sees its current RM state as a text embedding of that state's description, which has 768 values (Appendix~\ref{app:training-settings}). In the shop, the agent sees a one-hot code of the RM state instead. The 768 values do not reach the agent as they are. A small network of two layers first reduces them to 192. These then join the features that the agent takes from its observation. The training script of ARM-FM's first author does this \cite{armfm_code}. The paper itself does not describe the network.

Even after this reduction, 192 values are many beside those of the observation. With MiniGrid's usual view of 7$\times$7 cells, the agent takes 1,024 features from its observation, so the RM state is 192 of 1,216 values, and in XLand-MiniGrid it is 192 of 448. The small network itself adds about 185,000 weights to learn. The states of one RM also have descriptions that lie close together. Closeness here is the cosine similarity of two embeddings, which is 1 when they point the same way. The mean similarity between the embedding of a state and that of its nearest state in the same RM is 0.873, over the 82 RMs with at least two states that agents trained on in the training experiment. Any two states of one RM score 0.823 on average, against 0.639 for two states of different RMs.

Future work could therefore reduce the size of these embeddings and make their values tell the states of one RM apart better.

For the labelling function, a first step could be a critic that also sees the labels on sample episodes. For the state descriptions, it could be a network with smaller layers or another compression technique, such as principal component analysis (PCA) or singular value decomposition (SVD). A PCA of the 2,141 distinct state embeddings of the 417 RMs that FARM and ARM-FM built in the generation experiment finds that 32 components hold 80\% of the variance of the 768 values, and about 70 hold 90\%.

\section{Reflection on the Sustainable Development Goals}
This thesis is a technical work in reinforcement learning. It proposes FARM, a method that builds RMs from tasks written in plain language, and measures the RMs and the agents that learn with them in simulated environments. It does not address a social or environmental problem directly, so most goals do not apply. Its contribution is a research method, not a deployed system.

\subsection{SDG 9: Industry, innovation and infrastructure}
The work is research, so this is the goal it relates to most. Within it, the relation is highest with target 9.5, which asks to enhance scientific research and upgrade technological capabilities. The thesis contributes a method to one research problem, the writing of rewards for reinforcement learning. It also extends the open tools it builds on in public forks of IBM nl2ltl and FL-AT (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

The intended benefit is reward design that needs no expert in reinforcement learning. People who train agents, and people who know a task but not reinforcement learning, could then state the task and get an RM that generates the reward (Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}). The method also uses small and cheap language models on purpose, which keeps it within reach of groups without large computing budgets. These benefits are intended. No study with users was run, so they are not measured.

\subsection{SDG 8: Decent work and economic growth}
Writing and tuning a reward function by hand takes time and needs expertise in both the task and reinforcement learning. Target 8.2 asks for higher productivity through technological upgrading and innovation. FARM builds the RM from the task with no edit by hand, so it could save part of that work. The relation is low because the time saved is not measured.

\subsection{SDG 12: Responsible consumption and production}
This goal relates to the computing that the method needs. Target 12.2 asks for the efficient use of natural resources. Of the five steps of FARM, only the generator and the critic call a language model, and both models are small. Steps 2 to 4, the compiler, call none, since fixed tools build the RM from the clauses. The training runs of the evaluation still use computing power. The energy used by the work was not measured, so the relation is low.

\subsection{SDG 16: Peace, justice and strong institutions}
The goal includes accountable and transparent institutions. The work relates to it only indirectly, through the transparency of automated systems. In FARM each clause states its part of the task in one plain sentence. The RM follows the chosen clauses exactly, and a broken hard rule ends the episode in the well (Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}). A person can therefore read what an agent is rewarded for, and check it against the task, before the agent trains. The model can still choose clauses that do not fit the task (Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}). This transparency therefore does not replace a review of the clauses.

Most of this work is built and checked through a workflow with AI agents: the system, its methodology and experiments, and this document. This appendix is about the AI used to build the work, not about the two language models inside FARM. The generator and the critic belong to the method and are described in Chapter~\ref{chap:farm}.

\section{How the work started}
\label{app:dev-start}
The work goes through four periods, the last of which is the main development:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RMs in practice}: the work starts by studying what RMs are, with examples built and tried by hand. A first implementation of counterfactual experiences for RMs (CRM) \cite{icarte2022reward} shows how RMs work and what can be done with them.
    \item \textbf{Aims}: the aim of bringing AI closer to what people want (Section~\ref{sec:proposal}) is set against what other work does with RMs and what is feasible.
    \item \textbf{A first prototype}: with the goal framed and ARM-FM as the reference, a base prototype of the whole pipeline is built. It joins IBM's nl2ltl and FL-AT with a small post-process and the critic.
    \item \textbf{Iteration}: from the prototype on, the work runs in cycles of investigating, changing and testing. AI agents take the largest part in this period.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The workflow with AI agents}
\label{app:ai-workflow}
In the iterative period the developer talks to one main agent, which orchestrates other agents behind it. A change goes through seven steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grounding}: the conversation first settles what is to be done. It covers the ideas behind the change, its aim, the tools and methods, what can and cannot be done, and where in the code it belongs. Mock-ups and first-hand tests come at this point.
    \item \textbf{Requirements that everyone understands}: planning sets down every requirement, in enough detail that the authors, student and supervisor, understand exactly what will happen.
    \item \textbf{Feasibility}: small ad hoc experiments show that the change is achievable, that the algorithms work and that the ideas hold. They are run by hand, or by an agent with temporary code.
    \item \textbf{Plan}: once everything is settled, an agent writes a plan and every reference is checked. The agent that codes then knows what to do, how to do it and what result it must reach.
    \item \textbf{Code, review and live tests}: every change is reviewed, tested live on real runs and compared with the references.
    \item \textbf{Reviewers of different providers}: several coding agents from different providers review the code independently, from different angles, and try to break it. They check that the code is robust and clean and gives the right results.
    \item \textbf{The human last}: a person checks the work last. The person checks the outputs rather than every line of the code, and asks the reviewers for clearer evidence where it is missing. Anything unclear is redone, checked again or compared with the standards and the goals.
\end{itemize}
The same workflow applies to this document. Agents draft text and figures from the authors' notes and the measured results, and the authors review them.

\chapter{Implementation details}
\label{app:implementation}

Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} describes the method, and this appendix holds the implementation behind it. It gives the contributions of FARM, how it uses each tool, the format of its RMs, the models and settings of the runs, and the training values of the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:eval-training}).

\section{Contributions}
\label{app:contributions}
FARM makes fifteen additions to the tools it builds on, IBM nl2ltl, FL-AT and MONA, and to the generation step of ARM-FM:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prompt}: it offers all eight Declare templates, with the meaning, kind and formula of each. It serves any environment, because the propositions and their meaning come from the environment description (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item \textbf{Restricted answer}: each clause is a template, its propositions, a priority and one plain sentence. Only the first three reach the compiler, so tasks of one schema should get RMs of one shape (Sections~\ref{sec:step1} and~\ref{sec:task-schemas}).
    \item \textbf{Priority}: each clause is hard or soft, as the preference cues of the task set it. IBM's templates have no such priority (Section~\ref{sec:step1}).
    \item \textbf{FL-AT fork}: it drops MONA's first state only when it is redundant, and it returns the state of every clause in each product state (Sections~\ref{sec:step2} and~\ref{sec:step3}).
    \item \textbf{Final states and well}: the hard clauses set the final states and one well, in place of the accepting states and the rewards of FL-AT (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Rewards}: they count the stages still owed. A step closer pays, a new thing to do costs, and reaching the final state pays \gain{$+10$} (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Re-arming and folding}: soft rules are re-armed, so that every new break is charged, and soft Absence clauses are folded and add no state to the RM (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
    \item \textbf{Hard-break price}: a hard break costs more than breaking soft rules at every step up to the step limit. This price applies when the task has a soft clause (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).
    \item \textbf{Take-back}: the edge into the well takes back the progress already paid (Section~\ref{sec:well-charge}).
    \item \textbf{Merge and minimisation}: MONA's start state is merged with its idle successor, and the priced RM is minimised (Sections~\ref{sec:start-merge} and~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Loop check}: it refuses any RM with a loop whose rewards add up to more than zero (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Pay order}: a pay order for the runs of an RM, from finished to hard break, is reported when FARM prepares an RM for training (Section~\ref{sec:step4}).
    \item \textbf{Critic}: it reviews the clauses, not the RM text, and never edits the RM. Its model must differ from the generator's (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
    \item \textbf{Compiler clues}: the critic gets each rule in plain words, the hard clauses that the others imply, and the events that every way to finish includes (Section~\ref{sec:step5}).
    \item \textbf{RM format}: ARM-FM's format is extended with explicit final and rejecting states and one event expression per transition (Section~\ref{sec:output-format}).
\end{itemize}

\section{IBM nl2ltl}
In Step~1 the LTLf formula of each clause comes from the templates of IBM's nl2ltl. FARM uses them through a fork (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/nl2ltl}) of \url{https://github.com/IBM/nl2ltl} \cite{nl2ltl_code}. It reuses only the template classes and the LTLf builders. The GPT engine of nl2ltl is bypassed, and FARM supplies its own engine in its place. That engine asks a language model for a list of clauses under a JSON schema, and then passes each clause to the Declare template classes of nl2ltl, which build the LTLf formula.

FARM talks to three model providers, OpenAI, OpenCode and Antigravity, through one engine each, and every engine returns the clauses under the same JSON schema. The study of other language models calls its two models through their makers' command-line tools, outside these engines (Section~\ref{sec:res-models}). The generator and the critic are configured separately.

The template classes of nl2ltl cover all eight Declare templates of Table~\ref{tab:templates}. The GPT prompt of nl2ltl, by contrast, lets the model choose only five: Existence, ExistenceTwo, Response, \mbox{ChainResponse} and RespondedExistence.

\section{FL-AT}
Steps 2 and 3 run on FL-AT. FARM uses it through a fork (\url{https://github.com/MiquelGomezCorral/Flat}) of \url{https://github.com/Jamidd/Flat} \cite{flat_code}, which makes four main changes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item MONA runs in a subprocess with temporary files, where the original wrote a file in the working folder through \texttt{os.system}. Its output is parsed strictly, so an automaton that repeats a guard out of one state stops the build with an error.
    \item Proposition names are renamed with regular expressions, where the original split strings and evaluated labels.
    \item MONA's first state is removed when it is redundant (Section~\ref{sec:redundant}).
    \item The state tuples of the product are exported, so that each product state tells which state every clause is in.
\end{itemize}

\section{The generator prompt}
The prompt of the generator has three parts: a system message, a user message and a catalogue of the Declare templates. The catalogue gives, for each template, its arguments, its kind (goal or rule), its meaning in English and its LTLf formula in IBM's syntax. It states that only Existence and ExistenceTwo are goals and that every rule holds when nothing happens. Beside the catalogue, a second table maps thirteen typical wordings to templates.

The instructions for the priority list the preference cues that make a clause soft: ``if possible'', ``preferably'', ``ideally'', ``try to'', ``when you can'', ``it would be nice'' and, for a ban, ``try not to''. Every other clause is hard. A cue covers the step it qualifies, not the order word that leads into that step.

A step the task asks for is \textbf{lone} when no hard order puts it before a later step that must happen, that is, when no hard Precedence chain links it to a later step that has a hard Existence. Every lone step gets its own hard Existence, and no other step does. Three kinds of step are lone:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A step asked for on its own;
    \item The last hard step of an order;
    \item A step whose later step is only preferred, since a soft Existence forces nothing.
\end{itemize}

An order is written as a hard Existence of its last step and one Precedence for each pair of consecutive steps. The \runex{sequence} example is built this way: Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}), Precedence(\texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open}) and Precedence(\texttt{door\_open}, \texttt{at\_goal}), with no \mbox{Precedence}(\texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{at\_goal}) (Table~\ref{tab:running}).

\section{The post-process}
Three parts of the post-process of Step~4 need more detail than Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} gives: the count of stages, the optional end charges and the minimisation of the RM. The pay-order report of Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment} also needs the step limit, and without one it records the pay order as not checked.

\subsection{Stages}
The stages that a state $u$ owes, $\Phi(u)$ (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}), are counted in one of two ways, depending on whether the RM has a final state.

With a final state, the hard part of $\Phi(u)$ is the fewest events that take $u$ to a final state. Each step on the way counts the propositions that its guard needs true, and at least one.

To this hard part, each soft clause, a soft goal included, adds its own local count. That count is the longest path from the clause's state to an accepting state in its own automaton, with every cycle collapsed into one node. Its length is then multiplied by the chain weight of the clause. The chain weight is one plus the weights of the clauses that the event closing the clause makes pending, so a rule that hands off to another is worth the chain behind it. A violated state counts zero.

Without a final state, every clause, hard or soft, uses this local count.

\subsection{End charges}
Two options of Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, both off by default, read the count of stages: the end charges and the promise cost. The end charges of Section~\ref{sec:open-promises} are for the stages still open when an episode ends. They sum the local, chain-weighted count of every clause, hard clauses included. They read that count on the state that the edge into the well leaves or, when the environment ends the episode, on the state reached. The promise cost is for each step out of a state that still owes a hard stage, and it reads the hard part of the same count.

\subsection{Minimisation}
Minimisation makes the RM smaller. After pricing, states that pay the same, lead to the same states on every input and end an episode alike are merged into one. Minimisation is a setting, on by default.

\section{Retries and the critic}
A rejection by the critic starts a new attempt (Section~\ref{sec:step5}). Failures of providers and tools, and inputs too large for the critic, follow rules of their own:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generator provider}: a passing failure of the generator's provider, such as a timeout or a rate limit, uses no attempt. The same attempt is rerun, up to ten times in a row.
    \item \textbf{Invalid generator answer}: it uses an attempt, like a rejection.
    \item \textbf{Critic}: a failed critic request, or a critic answer that cannot be read, is repeated on the same candidate, up to ten times.
    \item \textbf{Limits and permanent errors}: when either limit of ten runs out, or when a provider gives a permanent error, the run ends.
    \item \textbf{MONA or FL-AT}: a failure of MONA, or of FL-AT's translation of the formula into MONA's input, also ends the run instead of using an attempt.
    \item \textbf{Large inputs}: an RM text longer than 200{,}000 characters reaches the critic as a summary. A task with more than twelve propositions gets no compiler clues.
\end{itemize}

\section{State descriptions}
A LARM, the output of ARM-FM, is an RM with the code of its labelling functions and a description of each state (Section~\ref{sec:sota-armfm}). FARM builds these two parts the way ARM-FM does. After the RM is built, a \textbf{tagger} writes one description per state. It sends the states in batches of twenty, with up to three requests per batch, and it also sees the information that the critic received.

\section{RM format}
The format of FARM differs from that of ARM-FM (Listing~\ref{lst:armfm-doorkey}) in five points:
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are no \texttt{else} self-loops. A state with no matching transition stays where it is and pays \texttt{DEFAULT\_REWARD}, which is 0.
    \item The states with a special role are listed explicitly. \texttt{FINAL\_\allowbreak STATES} is always written and may be empty. \texttt{REJECTING\_\allowbreak STATES} is left out when the RM has no well.
    \item One event expression combines propositions with \texttt{!} and \texttt{\&}, such as \texttt{has\_key\&\allowbreak !door\_open}, instead of a separate predicate such as \texttt{not\_has\_key}.
    \item \texttt{REWARD\_FUNCTION} lists only the transitions with a reward other than 0.
    \item Four fields appear only when the optional charge at the end of an episode is on (Section~\ref{sec:open-promises}), and it is off by default. They are \texttt{ACCEPTING\_\allowbreak STATES}, \texttt{HARD\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD}, \texttt{SOFT\_\allowbreak FAILED\_\allowbreak REWARD} and \texttt{PENDING\_\allowbreak PROMISES}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Drawing the RMs}
\label{app:drawing}
The drawings of RMs in this thesis colour each arrow by the sign of what it pays. In the simplified drawings, all transitions between two states share one arrow, which is drawn in one of three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{as a cost} when its rewards are negative or zero;
    \item \textbf{as a gain} when they are positive or zero;
    \item \textbf{neutral} only when it holds both signs or only zeros.
\end{itemize}
The label \textit{mixed} means that the rewards differ, not that the colour is neutral. The drawings follow the RM viewer of Section~\ref{app:viewer}, without its \texttt{else} loops.

The 60 tasks of the training experiment come from the 126 tasks of the task files in six ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tasks left out.} Of the 18 tasks that are never trained because an idle agent keeps their hard rules, 17 are template tasks and one is a complex task.
    \item \textbf{Template tasks.} The templates group holds variants of the eight Declare templates as hard or soft rules, with or without a goal; some train and others are checked only as RMs. Each of the 28 trained template tasks takes one of the eight Declare templates of Table~\ref{tab:templates}, as a hard or a soft rule, and tests that it becomes an RM an agent can learn from.

    \item \textbf{Repeated shop tasks.} Five shop tasks train a second time, with the environment's reward added (Section~\ref{sec:eval-training}).

\end{itemize}

\noindent Just as some tasks repeat another, some tasks share an RM. A task that reuses another task's RM can still train. For example, two tasks of the box-carry group train on the RM of another task in a changed version of its environment. The five repeated shop tasks also train on the RMs of the shop tasks they repeat. In all, seven tasks train on an RM built for another task, the two box-carry tasks and the five repeated shop tasks, so the 60 tasks run on 53 FARM RMs.

A judged episode that the method's RM ends too early fails, whatever the rules say. Too early means that the RM ends the episode in a final state within $\max(1, \lceil 0.1L \rceil)$ actions, where $L$ is the median length of the solving plan on the task's layouts, or 0 when there is none.

A run of a task with no goal and only soft rules passes when three things hold, per episode, against the idle agent:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Its soft breaks and its soft rules left unmet are both no higher than the idle agent's;
    \item At least one of them is lower;
    \item The labels the judge reads change more often than the idle agent's, which shows that the agent acts.
\end{itemize}

Besides the learned count, each task reports two means over its runs, its judged success and its soft breaks per episode, and a shop task adds a third, its orders served per episode. The learned counts themselves are given over the 60 tasks and over four kinds of subset, in this order:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item ARM-FM's own kind of task, the step-by-step tasks, against the 49 tasks of new kinds;
    \item Whether the task has a goal, outside the shop and the step-by-step tasks;
    \item Three kinds of task by what they ask: the step-by-step tasks, tasks with hard rules but no order of steps and no soft rule, and tasks with at least one soft rule;
    \item Each group.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Qualitative evaluation of the Reward Machines}
\label{app:eval-machines}
Some measures of the generation experiment in Section~\ref{sec:eval-machines} rest on rules that the chapter only names. This section gives them in the order of that section.

In the model review, the reviewer, Claude Opus 5.5, reads one sheet per RM, and each sheet shows three things:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The RM's clauses beside the task's accepted answers;
    \item The results of the build checks and of the scripted-plan check;
    \item For every state, its role, its transitions, the clauses still owed there and its description.
\end{itemize}
\noindent From these, the reviewer gives each RM one of three verdicts, ok, unsure or anomaly.

The thinking-level study counts more strictly on a task that asks for steps in order. Clauses that also make an earlier step a goal of its own pass the task, but they are not counted as right.

In the scripted-plan check, each plan plays in the task's environment and stops at the first final state or at the well. A plan that breaks only soft rules counts as penalised when it receives a negative reward before it stops, or when it ends like the solving plan on its layout and is paid less in total. The total adds the rewards up without discount. The three more conditions that an RM with no final state must meet are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An idle plan that keeps every rule does not enter the well, and both it and the solving plan end with nothing owed;
    \item A wrong or idle plan that breaks a hard rule for good ends in the well;
    \item Every wrong plan pays less than the solving plan, both with and without discount.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prompt versions before and after}
\label{app:res-prompts}

Two changes made during development are measured here: the versions of the generator prompt, and the compiler clues added to the critic.

The generator prompt went through twelve versions, v0 to v11, measured over 2,430 runs on the tuning set. From the first version to the last, the share of ordered tasks written in the chain form rises from 52\% (22 of 42, 95\% Wilson interval 38\% to 67\%) to 81\% (68 of 84, 71\% to 88\%). That is short of the 90\% set as its target. Over the same versions, wrong RMs fall from 14\% (18 of 126 runs, 9\% to 22\%) to 12\% (31 of 252, 9\% to 17\%).

Adding the compiler clues to the critic raises the share of labelled defects that the critic flags at its first call from 30\% (74 of 246, 95\% Wilson interval 25\% to 36\%) to 56\% (23 of 41, 41\% to 70\%). The measure covers 38 items: 28 wrong clause sets holding 41 defects, and 10 right ones. Through the full loop of generator and critic on 46 tasks, right RMs rise from 77\% (213 of 276, 72\% to 82\%) to 85\% (39 of 46, 72\% to 92\%).

\section{The ARM-FM paper's own tasks}
\label{app:res-armfm-paper}

Section~\ref{sec:res-armfm-paper} trains the three methods on tasks of the ARM-FM paper. This section lists those tasks, gives two more trainings of ARM-FM, each with one of its parts removed, and says where every value is kept. The tasks are of four kinds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DoorKey.} \textit{Use the key to open the door, then reach the goal.} At 5$\times$5 and 16$\times$16 the layout is either new every episode or one fixed layout, and at 8$\times$8 there is only the fixed layout.
    \item \textbf{UnlockToUnlock.} A BabyAI level: \textit{Pick up the first key, open its matching door, pick up the second key, open its matching door, and then pick up the target ball.}
    \item \textbf{X1 to X5.} Each is one ruleset of XLand-MiniGrid, that is, fixed rules that turn objects into others plus a goal, on a new layout every episode. X1 makes a purple star and then a blue pyramid, and ends with the pyramid one tile right of the agent on the map. X2 makes a white key and then a grey star, and ends the same way with the star. X3 makes a green star and then an orange star, which the agent picks up. X4 makes a brown pyramid and then a blue ball, and ends with a step forward next to the ball. X5 makes a pink ball and ends the same way as X1. X1 also trains alone, X1 to X3 form the 3-task mix and X1 to X5 the 5-task mix.
    \item \textbf{ZA, ZB and ZC.} ZA makes an orange star by putting a blue key down next to a purple ball, and ends with the agent picking the star up. ZB turns a blue star and ZC a blue key into a blue pyramid, and both end the same way as X1. Each method trains on ZA and ZB at once and is judged on ZC, following its own RM of ZC. ZC also trains alone.
\end{itemize}

The two more trainings run ARM-FM on the 5-task mix, three runs each. Without the RM's reward, the environment alone pays, and the RM only gives the agent its state and ends no episode. Without the embeddings, the RM still pays. The agent no longer sees the RM state, which it otherwise reads as a text embedding of the state's description. Removing the RM's reward lowers ARM-FM's mean on all five tasks. The intervals are apart only on X2, and only just, at 0.017 (95\% interval 0.000 to 0.055) against 0.087 (0.058 to 0.115) for the full ARM-FM. Removing the embeddings lowers the mean on four of the five tasks, with overlapping intervals, and raises it on X2.

The folder \texttt{docs/paper/armfm-baseline-results/} of the repository of this thesis keeps every task's text, its expected clauses with their LTLf and both RMs, and the value of every run.

\chapter{IBM template examples}
\label{app:templates}
This appendix shows what the compiler builds from each template alone, once hard and once soft, sixteen RMs in all. Each is the RM of a task with that one clause. The appendix goes with Table~\ref{tab:templates} of Step~1, which gives the eight Declare templates with their LTLf formulas and how to read them.

Each row shows one template, hard on the left and soft on the right, and its caption gives the plain sentence of each version. As in Section~\ref{sec:run-step4}, a double circle marks the final state, the well is grey and implicit stays are not drawn. These RMs are small, so each arrow also carries its guard next to its reward. The colour of an arrow follows the sign of its reward.

Three rules of Chapter~\ref{chap:farm} account for the shapes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Only a hard goal gives a final state.} Existence and ExistenceTwo are the goals. Each gives an RM with a final state when it is hard, and neither does when it is soft. A hard rule alone gives a rule-only RM, with no final state (Section~\ref{sec:rule-only}).
    \item \textbf{A broken hard rule leads to the well.} Absence, Precedence, ChainResponse and NotCoExistence have a violated state, so their hard RMs have a well. The hard Absence RM is drawn on the left of panel~(c) of Figure~\ref{fig:templates-alone-1}. \mbox{RespondedExistence} and Response have no violated state, so their hard RMs have no well. In them the trigger costs at once, and the answer pays it back.
    \item \textbf{A soft clause never ends the episode.} No soft RM has a well. A soft rule that can break is re-armed, so every new break costs again. A soft Absence is folded, so it adds no state and its break is a loop that costs \loss{$-1$} (Section~\ref{sec:recurrent}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The rewards step by step}
\label{sec:rewards-example}
The \runex{dropped-key} RM has no soft clause, but it shows four kinds of reward of Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}: progress, a step back, completion and a hard break. This part follows them step by step.

\textbf{What each state owes.} Table~\ref{tab:ex2-todo} gives the stages that each state still owes.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{State} & \textbf{Still to do} & \textbf{Stages} \\ \midrule
u0, start & reach the goal & 1 \\
u1 & pick up the key at the next step, then reach the goal & 2 \\
u3 & pick up the key at the next step & 1 \\
u2, final & nothing & 0 \\
u4, well & the key was not picked up in time & -- \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[Stages owed by the states of the \runex{dropped key}]{The stages that each state of the \runex{dropped-key} RM still owes.}
\label{tab:ex2-todo}
\end{table}

\textbf{What each step pays.} A step pays the change in stages, and the step that reaches the final state adds the completion reward:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dropping the key}, from u0 to u1, adds a stage and costs \loss{$-1$}.
    \item \textbf{Picking it up again}, from u1 back to u0, closes that stage and pays \gain{$+1$}.
    \item \textbf{Reaching the goal} pays \gain{$+10$} plus the progress of the step: \gain{$+11$} from u0, and \gain{$+12$} from u1 when the key comes back in the same step.
    \item \textbf{Reaching the goal in the step that drops the key}, from u0 to u3, closes the goal but owes the key, so it pays 0. Picking up the key in u3 then pays \gain{$+11$}.
    \item \textbf{A step without the key after a drop} breaks the hard rule. Every edge into the well u4 pays \loss{$-10$} and takes nothing back, because neither u1 nor u3 is ahead of the start (Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}, \emph{Taking progress back}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What five runs total.} Table~\ref{tab:ex2-traces} adds up five traces.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Trace} & \textbf{Rewards} & \textbf{Total} \\ \midrule
Reach the goal & $11$ & $11$ \\
Drop the key, pick it up, reach the goal & $-1,\ 1,\ 11$ & $11$ \\

Every trace that does the task totals \gain{$+11$}, with or without a drop. A drop that is repaired therefore costs nothing in the end. Leaving the key on the floor totals \loss{$-11$}, below a trace in which nothing happens. Finished, not started and hard break therefore keep the pay order of Section~\ref{sec:reward-assignment}.

\section{Careful key}
\label{app:ex3}
The \runex{careful key} joins the goal with a preference, which costs reward when it is broken but never ends the episode.

\textbf{Task.} \textit{Reach the goal, ideally without ever losing the key.}

\textbf{Clauses.} Existence(\texttt{at\_goal}), hard, $\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{at\_goal}$; Absence(\texttt{key\_lost}), soft, $\neg\,\mathrm{F}\,\texttt{key\_lost}$.

\textbf{RM.} Two states and three transitions (Listing~\ref{lst:ex3-rm}). Figure~\ref{fig:ex3-rm} draws it, and Section~\ref{sec:run-step4} explains what each transition pays. The RM has no well.

INITIAL_STATE: u0
